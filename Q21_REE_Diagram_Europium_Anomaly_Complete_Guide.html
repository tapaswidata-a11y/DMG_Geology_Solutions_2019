Lanthanide Contraction, Oddo-Harkins Smoothing, Europium Valence Multiplicity & The Sm-Gd Baseline

1. The Rare Earth Elements (Lanthanides) & Chondrite Normalization

The Rare Earth Elements (REE) comprise the 15 elements with atomic numbers $Z = 57$ (Lanthanum, $\text{La}$) through $Z = 71$ (Lutetium, $\text{Lu}$). Promethium ($\text{Pm}, Z = 61$) has no stable isotopes and is absent in nature. Across this series, electrons fill the inner $4f$ subshell while the outer valence shell remains constant:

The Lanthanide Contraction: As atomic number increases from $\text{La}$ ($Z=57$) to $\text{Lu}$ ($Z=71$), increasing nuclear charge pulls the electron cloud closer, causing ionic radii to shrink smoothly from $1.16\text{ \AA}$ ($\text{La}^{3+}$) down to $0.977\text{ \AA}$ ($\text{Lu}^{3+}$). This creates a predictable gradient in chemical behavior: Light REE (LREE: $\text{La} \to \text{Sm}$) are larger and more incompatible, while Heavy REE (HREE: $\text{Gd} \to \text{Lu}$) are smaller and more compatible in minerals like garnet.

Because nucleosynthetic processes favor even-atomic-number nuclei (the Oddo-Harkins Effect), raw REE abundances display an irregular "sawtooth" pattern. Geochemists normalize rock abundances against primitive carbonaceous chondrite meteorites (CI chondrites) to produce a smooth curve:

Chondrite Normalization Formulation
$[\text{REE}_i]_N = \frac{[\text{REE}_i]_{\text{sample}}}{[\text{REE}_i]_{\text{chondrite}}}

Plotting $[\text{REE}_i]_N$ on a logarithmic y-axis against atomic number ($Z$) generates a smooth, diagnostic REE pattern. Any deviation from a smooth curve reflects an anomalous geochemical process affecting that specific element.

However, at Europium, $D_{\text{plagioclase}}$ jumps to $>1.5$ because divalent $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ substitutes freely for $\text{Sr}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ in the plagioclase crystal lattice.

4. The Redox Mechanism: Why Europium Behaves Uniquely

Under terrestrial magmatic oxygen fugacities (typically around the QFM buffer), Europium exists in a multivalent equilibrium:

The Europium Redox Equilibrium & Anomaly Metric
$\text{Eu}^{3+} + e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{Eu}^{2+}$

• In the trivalent state ($\text{Eu}^{3+}$), its radius is $1.066\text{ \AA}$, nearly identical to $\text{Sm}^{3+}$ and $\text{Gd}^{3+}$.
• In the divalent state ($\text{Eu}^{2+}$), its radius swells to $1.250\text{ \AA}$, matching $\text{Sr}^{2+}$ ($1.18\text{ \AA}$) and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($1.00\text{ \AA}$).

Because plagioclase accepts $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Sr}^{2+}$, it preferentially absorbs $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ ($D_{\text{plag}}^{\text{Eu}} \gg 1$), while rejecting all other REEs ($D_{\text{plag}}^{\text{REE}^{3+}} \ll 1$).

Mathematical Calculation of the Anomaly ($\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^*$):
$\text{Eu}^* = \sqrt{\text{Sm}_N \times \text{Gd}_N} \quad \text{or} \quad \text{Eu}^* = \frac{\text{Sm}_N + \text{Gd}_N}{2}$

$\frac{\text{Eu}}{\text{Eu}^*} = \frac{\text{Eu}_N}{\sqrt{\text{Sm}_N \times \text{Gd}_N}}$
• $\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^* < 1.0$: Negative anomaly (plagioclase removed by fractional crystallization).
• $\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^* > 1.0$: Positive anomaly (plagioclase accumulated, e.g. anorthosite).
"The magnitude of the Eu anomaly is commonly expressed as $\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^*$, where $\text{Eu}^*$ is the hypothetical value of $\text{Eu}$ if no $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ was present, determined by interpolating between the chondrite-normalized values of $\text{Sm}$ and $\text{Gd}$... A negative europium anomaly is a good indicator that the liquid was at one time in equilibrium with now-absent plagioclase."
— John D. Winter, Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology, Chapter 9, p. 175.

EXAM QUESTION & RIGOROUS OPTION-BY-OPTION DISSECTION
DGMS 2019 Examination · Official Question Statement, Verified Answer & Detailed Distracter Analysis
DGMS 2019 · QUESTION 21
In a rare earth element (REE) diagram, europium anomaly is seen between which one of the following REE pairs?
(a) La-Nd
(b) Nd-Sm
(c) Sm-Gd
(d) Gd-Dy
✓
Correct Option: (c) Sm-Gd
Verified via John D. Winter, Principles of Igneous & Metamorphic Petrology (Ch. 9, pp. 173–175)
Incorrect Option (a): La-Nd

Detailed Refutation: Lanthanum ($\text{La}, Z=57$) and Neodymium ($\text{Nd}, Z=60$) are both Light Rare Earth Elements. The elements intervening between them are Cerium ($\text{Ce}, Z=58$) and Praseodymium ($\text{Pr}, Z=59$). While a Cerium anomaly ($\text{Ce}/\text{Ce}^*$) can occur in marine environments between $\text{La}$ and $\text{Pr}$ due to oxidation of $\text{Ce}^{3+}$ to insoluble $\text{Ce}^{4+}$, Europium ($Z=63$) does not lie anywhere near this interval.

Incorrect Option (b): Nd-Sm

Detailed Refutation: Neodymium ($\text{Nd}, Z=60$) and Samarium ($\text{Sm}, Z=62$) flank the synthetic/radioactive element Promethium ($\text{Pm}, Z=61$). Promethium does not occur in natural geological samples. No Europium anomaly can exist here, as Europium has atomic number $Z=63$, which lies beyond Samarium.

Correct Answer Option (c): Sm-Gd

Detailed Validation: Europium ($\text{Eu}, Z = 63$) is positioned directly between Samarium ($\text{Sm}, Z = 62$) and Gadolinium ($\text{Gd}, Z = 64$) in the periodic table and along the x-axis of every standard chondrite-normalized REE diagram. Because $\text{Sm}$ and $\text{Gd}$ exist strictly as trivalent cations ($\text{Sm}^{3+}, \text{Gd}^{3+}$), they establish the smooth, undisturbed background baseline. Europium's multivalent behavior ($\text{Eu}^{2+}$ entering plagioclase) causes its normalized concentration to deviate sharply above or below this baseline, creating the diagnostic Europium anomaly precisely between $\text{Sm}$ and $\text{Gd}$. The baseline value $\text{Eu}^*$ is mathematically calculated as the geometric mean $\sqrt{\text{Sm}_N \times \text{Gd}_N}$.

Incorrect Option (d): Gd-Dy

Detailed Refutation: Gadolinium ($\text{Gd}, Z=64$) and Dysprosium ($\text{Dy}, Z=66$) are Heavy Rare Earth Elements that flank Terbium ($\text{Tb}, Z=65$). All three elements ($\text{Gd, Tb, Dy}$) are strictly trivalent and define the smooth downward or flat trajectory of the HREE profile, completely past Europium.

High-Yield Exam Practice Questions

Practice Q1 (Numerical / Anomaly Calculation): Quantifying Eu/Eu*

A rhyolitic lava displays chondrite-normalized abundances of $\text{Sm}_N = 36.0$, $\text{Eu}_N = 6.0$, and $\text{Gd}_N = 16.0$. Calculate the Europium anomaly ($\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^*$) and state whether it is positive or negative.

Solution & Rationale:
Interpolated baseline: $\text{Eu}^* = \sqrt{\text{Sm}_N \times \text{Gd}_N} = \sqrt{36.0 \times 16.0} = \sqrt{576} = 24.0$.
$\frac{\text{Eu}}{\text{Eu}^*} = \frac{\text{Eu}_N}{\text{Eu}^*} = \frac{6.0}{24.0} = \mathbf{0.25}$.
Because $\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^* = 0.25 < 1.0$, the rock displays a severe negative Europium anomaly, indicating extensive fractional crystallization and removal of plagioclase feldspar.
Practice Q2 (Planetary Petrology): The Lunar Magma Ocean

The lunar highlands (anorthosite crust) display a huge positive Europium anomaly, while lunar mare basalts display a pronounced negative Europium anomaly. How does this prove the existence of the primordial Lunar Magma Ocean?

Answer: During crystallization of the global Lunar Magma Ocean, low-density plagioclase floated to form the buoyant lunar anorthositic crust, scavenging $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ and creating a massive positive Eu anomaly. The complementary residual liquid, depleted in Eu, sank into the lunar mantle; subsequent remelting of this residue formed mare basalts carrying the complementary negative Eu anomaly.
Practice Q3 (Marine Geochemistry): The Cerium Anomaly

Which other Rare Earth Element produces a prominent anomaly in modern seawater and pelagic sediments, what is its redox state, and which REE pair flanks it?

Answer: Cerium ($\text{Ce}$). Under oxidizing surface seawater conditions, soluble $\text{Ce}^{3+}$ oxidizes to insoluble $\text{Ce}^{4+}$, precipitating into ferromanganese nodules. This leaves seawater with a strong negative Ce anomaly between $\text{La}$ and $\text{Pr}$ ($\text{Ce}^* = \sqrt{\text{La}_N \times \text{Pr}_N}$).
Practice Q4 (Garnet Signature): Slope of the REE Profile

A basalt has an extremely steep chondrite-normalized REE pattern with a very high $(\text{La}/\text{Yb})_N$ ratio ($>20$) and heavy depletion in HREE ($\text{Yb, Lu}$). What residual mineral in the mantle source is diagnostic of this signature?

Answer: Garnet. Garnet has an extraordinarily high partition coefficient for heavy REEs ($D_{\text{garnet}}^{\text{Lu}} \approx 12$), locking them into the solid mantle residue. This proves that melting occurred deep within the garnet peridotite stability field ($>80\text{ km}$ depth).
Practice Q5 (Redox Sensitivity): Eu Valence in the Upper Mantle vs. Terrestrial Atmosphere

Why does Europium exhibit anomalous divalent behavior in basaltic magma chambers, but not in low-temperature oxidizing surface waters?

Answer: Divalent $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ is stable only under reducing conditions (low oxygen fugacity $f_{\text{O}_2} \le \text{QFM}$ buffer), typical of deep magmatic chambers. In oxygenated surface waters, all Europium oxidizes to trivalent $\text{Eu}^{3+}$, behaving identically to its neighbors and producing no anomaly.
Practice Q6 (Plagioclase Accumulates): Anorthosites

An igneous cumulate rock composed of $>90\%$ bytownite plagioclase displays a chondrite-normalized REE profile. What characteristic feature will be observed at $Z = 63$?

Answer: A prominent positive Europium anomaly ($\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^* \gg 1.0$), because the accumulated plagioclase crystals concentrated $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ from the parental liquid.
Practice Q7 (Oddo-Harkins Rule): Nucleosynthetic Cause

What fundamental nuclear property explains why natural abundances of $\text{Ce}, \text{Nd}, \text{Sm}, \text{Gd}$ are systematically higher than those of $\text{La}, \text{Pr}, \text{Eu}, \text{Tb}$ in raw rock analyses?

Answer: The Oddo-Harkins Rule. Atomic nuclei with even atomic numbers ($Z = 58, 60, 62, 64$) have paired protons, providing greater nuclear binding energy and stability during stellar nucleosynthesis than nuclei with odd atomic numbers ($Z = 57, 59, 63, 65$).
Practice Q8 (Crustal Composition): Post-Archean Australian Shale (PAAS)

Why does the average upper continental crust (and PAAS) display a pervasive negative Europium anomaly ($\text{Eu}/\text{Eu}^* \approx 0.65$)?

Answer: The upper continental crust is dominated by differentiated granitic rocks and their sedimentary erosional products. These granites formed by partial melting or fractional crystallization that left plagioclase-rich residues in the lower crust, permanently depriving the upper crust of Europium.
