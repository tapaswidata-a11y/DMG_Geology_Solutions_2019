QUESTION 47
The mean age of the oceanic floor is approximately:
(a) 20 Ma
(b) 60 Ma CORRECT ANSWER
(c) 80 Ma
(d) 230 Ma

1. Executive Summary & Answer Verification

The correct option is (b) 60 Ma (approximately 60–65 million years).

The Earth's oceanic floor is dynamically recycled through seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges and subduction at convergent margins. Consequently, oceanic lithosphere has a remarkably youthful age profile compared to continental crust:

  • Maximum in situ Age: The oldest existing in situ oceanic crust is only ~180–200 Ma (Early Jurassic, located in the western Pacific Pigafetta basin and the northwest Atlantic margins).
  • Area-Weighted Mean Age: Global digital seafloor age compilations (e.g., Müller et al., 2008; Sclater et al., 1980; Cogné & Humler, 2004) establish that the global area-weighted mean age of the ocean basins is 64.2 Ma, matching the standard textbook value of ~60 Ma.
  • Theoretical Derivation: Because seafloor is continually subducted, its age-area distribution is roughly triangular between $0\text{ Ma}$ (ridge crest) and $T_{\max} \approx 180\text{ Ma}$. For a triangular distribution, the centroid / mean age is $\bar{T} = \frac{1}{3} T_{\max} = \frac{180}{3} = \mathbf{60\text{ Ma}}$.

2. Geodynamic Derivation: Why is the Oceanic Floor So Young?

2.1 The Oceanic "Conveyor Belt" and Subduction Recycling

Unlike continental crust—which has an average age of ~2.0 to 2.5 Ga and contains rocks exceeding 4.0 Ga (e.g., the Acasta Gneiss)—oceanic crust rarely survives past 180–200 Ma. The underlying physical reasons are:

  1. High Density & Negative Buoyancy: As oceanic lithosphere moves away from mid-ocean ridges, it conducts heat upward into seawater, cools, and thickens by underplating lithospheric mantle. Because cold lithospheric mantle ($\rho \approx 3.30\text{ g/cm}^3$) is denser than the underlying asthenosphere ($\rho \approx 3.20\text{ g/cm}^3$), oceanic lithosphere develops negative buoyancy once it exceeds ~20–30 Ma, driving slab pull and subduction.
  2. Continuous Creation & Destruction: New oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges at a global rate of ~3.0 to $3.4\text{ km}^2/\text{yr}$ (generating ~20 $\text{km}^3/\text{yr}$ of new basaltic crust). An equal area must be subducted into the deep mantle to conserve global surface area.

2.2 Mathematical Age-Area Distribution

If we model the global ocean basin area $A(t)$ as a function of age $t$:

At the ridge crest ($t = 0$), new crust is continually generated. As crust ages toward $T_{\max} \approx 180\text{ Ma}$, older slabs are progressively consumed by subduction. If the probability of subduction per unit time is roughly uniform, the area of seafloor of age $t$ decreases approximately linearly:

\frac{dA}{dt} = C \cdot \left(1 - \frac{t}{T_{\max}}\right)   \text{for } 0 \le t \le T_{\max}

The area-weighted mean age $\bar{t}$ is obtained by integrating:

\bar{t} = \frac{\int_0^{T_{\max}} t \cdot \left(1 - \frac{t}{T_{\max}}\right) dt}{\int_0^{T_{\max}} \left(1 - \frac{t}{T_{\max}}\right) dt} = \frac{\left[\frac{t^2}{2} - \frac{t^3}{3T_{\max}}\right]_0^{T_{\max}}}{\left[t - \frac{t^2}{2T_{\max}}\right]_0^{T_{\max}}} = \frac{\frac{T_{\max}^2}{6}}{\frac{T_{\max}}{2}} = \frac{T_{\max}}{3}

Substituting $T_{\max} \approx 180\text{ Ma}$:

\bar{t} = \frac{180\text{ Ma}}{3} = \mathbf{60\text{ Ma}}

4. Evaluation of Multiple Choice Options

  • (a) 20 Ma — INCORRECT: 20 Ma is the age of young flank lithosphere near ridge crests. While a substantial fraction of seafloor is young, the overall mean including abyssal plains is three times higher (~60 Ma).
  • (b) 60 Ma — CORRECT: Exactly corresponds to the accepted mean age of oceanic floor (empirical global average is 64.2 Ma, standard approximation 60 Ma).
  • (c) 80 Ma — INCORRECT: 80 Ma corresponds to the age where thermal subsidence begins to flatten toward the thermal plate boundary asymptote, but it exceeds the global area-weighted mean.
  • (d) 230 Ma — INCORRECT: 230 Ma is Triassic. Virtually no oceanic crust of this age exists in situ in the global ocean (except rare, isolated trapped relics in the Eastern Mediterranean Herodotus basin, ~280 Ma, which represent trapped Tethyan crust rather than typical open ocean floor).
