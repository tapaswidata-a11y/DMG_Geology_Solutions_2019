1. Executive Summary & Answer Verification

The correct option is (c) A-2, B-3, C-1, D-4.

Volcanic edifices are morphologically and genetically classified on the basis of their eruptive mechanism, magma viscosity, and relative proportions of effusive lavas versus explosive pyroclastic ejecta:

  • A. Shield Volcano → 2 (Has shape of a flattened dome): Formed by thousands of successive, highly fluid, low-viscosity basaltic lava flows that travel tens of kilometers from central/fissure vents, building an enormous, broad, convex-upward profile resembling a flattened warrior's shield (slopes typically 2°–10°, e.g., Mauna Loa).
  • B. Cinder Cone Volcano → 3 (Formed exclusively of pyroclastic materials): Steep conical hills (slopes 30°–35° at the angle of repose) constructed exclusively or predominantly of vesicular, fragmental tephra (scoria, cinders, bombs) ejected during short-lived strombolian gas fountaining (e.g., Parícutin, Sunset Crater).
  • C. Stratovolcano → 1 (Also known as composite volcano): Classic symmetrical conical peaks composed of alternating strata of viscous intermediate (andesitic/dacitic) lava flows and explosive pyroclastic fall/flow deposits; hence universally termed composite volcanoes (e.g., Mt. Fuji, Mt. Rainier, Mt. St. Helens).
  • D. Lava Dome Volcano → 4 (Formed by highly viscous lava): Bulbous, steep-sided mounds or plugs formed by the sluggish extrusion of highly viscous, degassed, silica-rich (dacitic/rhyolitic) lava that cannot flow away from the vent (e.g., Lassen Peak, Novarupta dome, Mt. St. Helens dome).

2. Comprehensive Analysis of the Four Volcano Classes

2.1 Shield Volcanoes (Flattened Dome Morphology)

Shield volcanoes are the largest single volcanic landforms on Earth and in the Solar System (e.g., Olympus Mons on Mars). Key physical and petrological attributes include:

  • Magma Type: Low-silica, high-temperature (1100°–1250°C) tholeiitic to alkali basalt.
  • Eruptive Style: Non-explosive, effusive Hawaiian-type eruptions. Due to exceptionally low viscosity ($\sim 10\text{ to }100\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$), lavas flow swiftly as pahoehoe and aa, traveling up to 50 km before solidifying.
  • Edifice Architecture: Gentle slope profile ($<10^\circ$, averaging $2^\circ\text{--}5^\circ$) with a broad summit caldera. The ratio of basal diameter to height is massive ($>20:1$). For example, Mauna Loa rises ~9 km above the abyssal floor with a base diameter exceeding 100 km and a total basalt volume $>40,000\text{ km}^3$.

2.2 Cinder Cones (Pyroclastic / Scoria Cones)

Cinder cones are the simplest and most widespread volcanic landforms:

  • Constituent Material: Formed exclusively of loose pyroclastic fragments (pyroclasts, cinders, scoria, lapilli, and volcanic spindle bombs). If a lava flow occurs, it typically breaches the weak base of the cone rather than overflowing the summit crater.
  • Edifice Geometry: Steep, straight slopes fixed by the internal friction angle (angle of repose $\approx 30^\circ\text{--}35^\circ$) of loose tephra, with a bowl-shaped summit crater. Heights rarely exceed 300–400 meters.
  • Lifespan: Typically monogenetic (formed during a single eruptive episode lasting months to a few years, e.g., Mexico's Parícutin in 1943–1952).

2.3 Stratovolcanoes (Composite Volcanoes)

Stratovolcanoes are majestic, steep-sided, concave-upward conical mountains located predominantly along subduction-zone volcanic arcs (the "Ring of Fire"):

  • "Composite" Nature: The term composite volcano directly reflects their layered internal architecture: alternating strata of solid lava flows and explosive pyroclastic tephra (ash-fall beds, pumice lapilli, block-and-ash flow deposits, lahars).
  • Magma Chemistry: Intermediate to felsic calc-alkaline magmas (andesite, dacite, with subordinate basalt and rhyolite) containing moderate-to-high volatile contents ($\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{CO}_2, \text{SO}_2$).
  • Eruptive Style: Highly explosive Plinian to Vulcanian eruptions alternating with calmer effusive phases. Slopes reach 30°–36° near the summit and taper to 6°–10° at the apron.

2.4 Lava Domes (Volcanic Domes)

Lava domes (also called coulées or tholoids) are steep-sided, rounded, bulbous knolls:

  • Viscosity Factor: Formed when highly viscous, gas-depleted, silica-rich lava (dacite, rhyolite, trachyte; $>65\%\text{ SiO}_2$) erupts slowly. The viscosity is so high ($10^8\text{ to }10^{11}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) that the magma cannot flow away; instead, it is squeezed out like toothpaste, piling up directly over or within the vent.
  • Structural Hazards: As the dome inflates internally (endogenous growth) or breaks through its carapace (exogenous growth), steep oversteepened flanks collapse under gravity, triggering devastating block-and-ash pyroclastic flows (e.g., Mount Pelée in 1902; Soufrière Hills, Montserrat).
