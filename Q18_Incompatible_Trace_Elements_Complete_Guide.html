THEORETICAL FOUNDATION: TRACE ELEMENT FRACTIONATION & PARTITIONING
Distribution Coefficients ($D$), Batch Melting, Rayleigh Fractional Crystallization & Geochemical Reservoirs

1. The Physics and Chemistry of Trace Element Partitioning

Trace elements are defined as elements present in rocks at concentrations less than $0.1\text{ wt}\%$ ($< 1000\text{ ppm}$). Because their abundances are so low, trace elements do not form distinct mineral phases of their own (such as quartz or feldspar); instead, they substitute into the crystal lattices of major rock-forming minerals governed by Goldschmidt’s Rules of Substitution (matching ionic radius and charge):

The Distribution Coefficient ($K_D$ or $D_i$)
$D_i = \frac{C_{\text{solid}}}{C_{\text{liquid}}}$

Where:
• $C_{\text{solid}}$ = Weight concentration of element $i$ in the crystalline mineral phase.
• $C_{\text{liquid}}$ = Weight concentration of element $i$ in the coexisting silicate melt.

For a multi-mineralic rock, the Bulk Distribution Coefficient ($D$) is the weighted average:
$D = \sum_{j} W_j \cdot D_j = W_{\text{ol}}D_{\text{ol}} + W_{\text{cpx}}D_{\text{cpx}} + W_{\text{plag}}D_{\text{plag}} + \dots$
where $W_j$ is the weight fraction of mineral $j$ in the solid residue or cumulate.
Compatible vs. Incompatible Elements:
  • Compatible Element ($D > 1$): Fits comfortably into the mineral lattice sites of common early-crystallizing minerals (e.g., $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ into olivine; $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ into chromite and clinopyroxene). The element preferentially remains in or enters the solid phase, becoming rapidly depleted in evolving liquids.
  • Incompatible Element ($D < 1$, often $D \ll 1$): Possesses an ionic radius or valency that is poorly suited for major rock-forming mineral lattices. The element is strongly rejected by solid crystals, partitioning preferentially into the silicate melt ($C_{\text{liquid}} \gg C_{\text{solid}}$).
"Practically all elements distribute themselves unevenly between any two phases at equilibrium. This effect is known as chemical fractionation. An incompatible element is one that has a bulk distribution coefficient $D$ significantly less than 1. Such elements are rejected by the solid and are concentrated into the liquid phase during both partial melting and fractional crystallization."
— John D. Winter, Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology (2nd Ed., 2010), Chapter 9: Chemical Petrology II: Trace Elements and Isotopes, p. 167.

3. Mathematical Behavior During Melting and Crystallization

Trace elements obey rigorous thermodynamic conservation-of-mass equations during magmatic evolution:

Batch Melting (Equilibrium Partial Melting)
$\frac{C_L}{C_0} = \frac{1}{D(1 - F) + F}$

Where $C_0$ is source rock concentration, $C_L$ is melt concentration, and $F$ is melt fraction ($0 \le F \le 1$).
• For a highly incompatible element ($D \to 0$):
$\frac{C_L}{C_0} \approx \frac{1}{F}$
Physical significance: At small degrees of partial melting (e.g., $F = 0.01 = 1\%$), incompatible elements are concentrated in the first melt by a factor of $1/0.01 = \mathbf{100\times}$ relative to the mantle source!
Rayleigh Fractional Crystallization
$\frac{C_L}{C_0} = F^{(D - 1)}$

Where $F$ is the fraction of liquid remaining ($1 \to 0$).
• For an incompatible element where $D \ll 1$ (say $D \approx 0$):
$\frac{C_L}{C_0} \approx F^{-1} = \frac{1}{F}$
Physical significance: As crystallization proceeds from basalt $\to$ andesite $\to$ dacite $\to$ rhyolite/granite, the fraction of liquid remaining shrinks ($F \to 0.05$), concentrating incompatible trace elements exponentially in the residual granitic melt!
Figure 2 (Winter Fig. 1, p. 169): Harker variation diagrams for compatible $\text{Ni}$ and incompatible $\text{Zr}$ versus $\text{SiO}_2$ for the Crater Lake volcanic suite. Compatible $\text{Ni}$ collapses abruptly from $150\text{ ppm}$ to $<10\text{ ppm}$ during early olivine fractionation. In sharp contrast, incompatible $\text{Zr}$ continuously climbs as $\text{SiO}_2$ increases, reaching maximum concentrations in high-silica rhyolites and granites.

5. Classification of Incompatible Trace Elements

Geochemists subdivide incompatible trace elements into two major groups based on the ratio of ionic charge ($z$) to ionic radius ($r$), known as ionic potential ($z/r$):

  • Large-Ion Lithophile (LIL) Elements ($z/r < 2.0$): Low charge ($+1$ or $+2$) and large ionic radius ($>1.0\text{ \AA}$). Includes $\text{Cs}^+, \text{Rb}^+, \text{K}^+, \text{Ba}^{2+}, \text{Sr}^{2+}, \text{Eu}^{2+}$. Highly mobile in aqueous fluids; concentrated heavily in continental crust and granites.
  • High Field Strength (HFS) Elements ($z/r > 2.0$): High formal charge ($+3, +4, +5$) and small/intermediate radius. Includes $\text{Zr}^{4+}, \text{Hf}^{4+}, \text{Nb}^{5+}, \text{Ta}^{5+}, \text{Th}^{4+}, \text{U}^{4+}, \text{Ti}^{4+}, \text{Y}^{3+}$. Chemically immobile in hydrous fluids; concentrated by magmatic differentiation into felsic magmas and accessory minerals (zircon, monazite, allanite).
  • Rare Earth Elements (REE): Lanthanides ($\text{La}^{3+} \to \text{Lu}^{3+}$), where Light REE (LREE, large radii) are significantly more incompatible than Heavy REE (HREE, smaller radii).

EXAM QUESTION & RIGOROUS OPTION-BY-OPTION DISSECTION
DGMS 2019 Examination · Official Question Statement, Verified Answer & Detailed Distracter Analysis
DGMS 2019 · QUESTION 18
Which one of the following rocks is normally expected to have the highest abundance of incompatible trace elements?
(a) Basalt
(b) Dunite
(c) Peridotite
(d) Granite
✓
Correct Option: (d) Granite
Verified via John D. Winter, Principles of Igneous & Metamorphic Petrology (Ch. 9, pp. 167–172; Ch. 11, p. 219)
Incorrect Option (a): Basalt

Detailed Refutation: Basalt is a mafic volcanic rock generated by moderate degrees of partial melting ($F \approx 10\%–25\%$) of mantle peridotite. While basalt is substantially enriched in incompatible elements compared to its peridotite mantle source, it is far less differentiated than granite. During subsequent fractional crystallization of basaltic magma, the liquid undergoes an additional $80\%–90\%$ crystal removal, concentrating incompatible elements several orders of magnitude higher into terminal granitic melts.

Incorrect Option (b): Dunite

Detailed Refutation: Dunite is an ultramafic rock composed of $>90\%$ olivine. Because the olivine crystal lattice strongly excludes incompatible trace elements ($D_{\text{ol}} \approx 0.001–0.01$ for $\text{Rb, Ba, Th, U, REE}$), dunites (whether formed as mantle melting residues or early ultramafic cumulates) have the lowest incompatible trace element concentrations of any terrestrial rocks.

Incorrect Option (c): Peridotite

Detailed Refutation: Peridotite is the primary ultramafic rock of the Earth's upper mantle. It represents the un-melted starting source (primitive mantle) or the depleted residue after basalt extraction (harzburgite). By geochemical definition, the mantle source has baseline trace element concentrations prior to the massive enrichment that occurs during partial melting and prolonged differentiation.

Correct Answer Option (d): Granite

Detailed Validation: Granite is a highly evolved, felsic, silica-rich continental plutonic rock. It represents either the extreme residual melt produced after prolonged fractional crystallization of more mafic parent magmas ($F \ll 0.1$) or the product of low-degree crustal anatexis. Because incompatible trace elements ($D \ll 1$) are continuously rejected by early-crystallizing minerals (olivine, pyroxene, calcic plagioclase, spinels), they accumulate relentlessly in the residual liquid, reaching their absolute maximum natural abundances in granites, pegmatites, and associated felsic differentiates.

High-Yield Exam Practice Questions

Practice Q1 (Numerical / Batch Melting): Enrichment Calculation

A perfectly incompatible element ($D = 0$) is present in a mantle peridotite source at a concentration of $C_0 = 1.5\text{ ppm}$. If the mantle undergoes $F = 3\%$ ($0.03$) batch partial melting, what is the concentration of this element in the resulting primary basaltic melt?

Solution & Rationale:
Using the batch melting equation: $C_L / C_0 = \frac{1}{D(1-F) + F}$.
Since $D = 0$, $C_L / C_0 = \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{0.03} = 33.33$.
$C_L = 1.5\text{ ppm} \times 33.33 = \mathbf{50.0\text{ ppm}}$.
The melt is enriched by more than $33$-fold relative to the solid mantle source.
Practice Q2 (Rayleigh Fractionation): Compatible Element Depletion

Nickel is strongly compatible in olivine ($D_{\text{Ni}} = 10$). If a basaltic magma undergoes fractional crystallization until only $F = 50\%$ ($0.5$) of the liquid remains, what fraction of the original nickel remains in the melt?

Solution & Rationale:
Rayleigh equation: $C_L / C_0 = F^{(D - 1)}$.
Here $D - 1 = 10 - 1 = 9$.
$C_L / C_0 = (0.5)^9 = \frac{1}{512} \approx 0.00195 = \mathbf{0.195\%}$.
Over $99.8\%$ of the original nickel is locked into the early-crystallized olivine cumulate, explaining why evolved rocks (andesites, granites) are virtually devoid of nickel.
Practice Q3 (Element Taxonomy): Distinguishing LILE from HFSE

Which of the following trace elements is classified as a High Field Strength Element (HFSE) rather than a Large-Ion Lithophile Element (LILE): (a) $\text{Rb}$, (b) $\text{Ba}$, (c) $\text{Zr}$, (d) $\text{Sr}$?

Answer: (c) $\text{Zr}$ (Zirconium). $\text{Zr}^{4+}$ has a high valence charge ($+4$) and relatively small ionic radius ($0.72\text{ \AA}$), yielding a high ionic potential ($z/r = 5.56$). In contrast, $\text{Rb}^+, \text{Ba}^{2+}, \text{and } \text{Sr}^{2+}$ are all low-valence, large-radius LIL elements.
Practice Q4 (Petrogenesis): Incompatible Behavior in Granitic Pegmatites

Why do giant rare-metal pegmatites (containing spodumene, columbite-tantalite, beryl, and uraninite) form almost exclusively as peripheral haloes around granitic plutons?

Answer: Incompatible elements with extreme ionic charges or radii ($\text{Li, Be, B, Nb, Ta, U, Th, REE}$) cannot fit into quartz or feldspar lattices during the crystallization of granite. As the granite body cools, these elements are squeezed into the final, water-saturated residual volatile melt fraction ($F < 0.01$), which injects into surrounding country rocks to crystallize pegmatites.
Practice Q5 (Bulk Partition Coefficient): Mineral Weight Influence

Explain why a rock containing just $3\%$ garnet can behave as a compatible reservoir for Heavy Rare Earth Elements (HREE) like Lutetium ($\text{Lu}$), even when all other minerals in the rock have $D < 1$.

Answer: The partition coefficient for $\text{Lu}$ in garnet is exceptionally high ($D_{\text{garnet}}^{\text{Lu}} \approx 12.0$). In the bulk equation $D = W_{\text{gt}} D_{\text{gt}} + \sum W_j D_j$, the garnet term alone contributes $0.03 \times 12.0 = 0.36$. When combined with pyroxenes, the bulk $D$ easily exceeds $1.0$, retaining HREE in the garnet-bearing mantle residue.
Practice Q6 (Spidergrams): Primitive Mantle Normalization

Why are trace element concentrations in igneous rocks normalized to Chondrite or Primitive Mantle in "spidergrams" (incompatible element multi-element diagrams)?

Answer: To eliminate the natural "sawtooth" abundance variation caused by the Oddo-Harkins effect (elements with even atomic numbers are cosmochemically more abundant than elements with odd atomic numbers). Normalization produces smooth patterns where slopes and anomalies directly reflect petrogenetic processes.
Practice Q7 (Subduction Signatures): LILE vs. HFSE Decoupling

Why are subduction zone magmas (island arc basalts and calc-alkaline andesites) characteristically enriched in LILE ($\text{Rb, Ba, Sr}$) but depleted in HFSE ($\text{Nb, Ta, Ti}$)?

Answer: LILE are fluid-mobile and are readily transported by aqueous dehydration fluids from the subducting slab into the mantle wedge. HFSE are highly insoluble in aqueous fluids and are retained in the slab within residual rutile ($\text{TiO}_2$), producing the classic negative $\text{Nb-Ta}$ and $\text{Ti}$ anomalies on arc spidergrams.
Practice Q8 (Mantle Residues): Fertile vs. Depleted Mantle

Between a fertile lherzolite and a depleted harzburgite, which mantle rock has higher incompatible trace element abundances, and why?

Answer: Fertile lherzolite. Harzburgite has already experienced one or more episodes of basaltic melt extraction, which stripped away its clinopyroxene and incompatible trace elements into the extracted magma, leaving a refractory, depleted residue of olivine + orthopyroxene.
