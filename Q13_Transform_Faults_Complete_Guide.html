1. J. Tuzo Wilson & The Formulation of Transform Faults

Prior to 1965, all strike-slip faults were interpreted as simple transcurrent faults that cut across preexisting geological features. In 1965, the Canadian geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson published a revolutionary paper titled "A new class of faults and their bearing on continental drift" (Nature), recognizing that oceanic strike-slip faults that offset mid-ocean ridges represent an entirely new mechanical and kinematic category: transform faults.

"In the preferred sense, transform faults are plate boundaries at which lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed... A transform fault links two other plate boundaries (divergent or convergent) and accommodates the relative horizontal motion between the separating plates. Seismicity along ridge-ridge transform faults is strictly confined to the active segment connecting the two ridge crests." — Van der Pluijm & Marshak, Earth Structure (2nd Ed., Table 8.2, p. 171 & Chapter 19, p. 450)
2. Diagnostic Distinctions: Transform Faults vs. Other Strike-Slip Faults

In structural geology (Van der Pluijm & Marshak, Chapter 8, Table 8.2), strike-slip faults are subdivided into distinct genetic classes based on their tectonic scale and boundary terminations:

Strike-Slip Fault Type Tectonic Nature & Termination Displacement Distribution Seismic Activity Pattern
Transform Fault Major lithospheric plate boundary; terminates abruptly at other plate boundaries (ridges, trenches) Constant displacement along the active plate boundary segment Earthquakes strictly confined to segment between ridge crests; fossil extensions (fracture zones) are aseismic
Transcurrent (Wrench) Fault Intraplate strike-slip fault; dies out into rock within a single plate at tip lines Displacement is maximum at center and decreases to zero at both tips Earthquakes distributed along the entire length of the fault plane
Tear Fault Local strike-slip fault bounding a thrust sheet or graben segment to accommodate differential advance Limited to the moving thrust nappe Shallow, local seismicity during thrust emplacement
Scissor Fault Rotational fault where displacement changes sign across a pivotal axis of zero slip Variable along strike (rotational hinge) Local block tilting
II
Official Exam Question Statement & In-Depth Critical Analysis
Directorate General of Mines Safety (DGMS) 2019 · Junior Scientific Officer Exam · Question 13
Question Statement
Q.13: In ocean, regional strike-slip faults associated with lithospheric plate boundaries are known as:
(a) Transform faults
(b) Tear faults
(c) Wrench faults
(d) Scissor faults
✓
Correct Answer: (a) Transform faults
Regional strike-slip faults that offset oceanic ridges and function as conservative lithospheric plate boundaries are universally designated as transform faults.
Exhaustive Option-by-Option Critical Analysis
(a) Transform faults CORRECT
Why it is uniquely correct: In global tectonics and structural geology (Van der Pluijm & Marshak, Table 8.2, p. 171; Wilson, 1965; Kearey, Klepeis & Vine, 2009), transform faults are explicitly defined as regional strike-slip faults that constitute primary conservative plate boundaries connecting segments of mid-ocean ridges or subduction zones. Across the global ocean floor (such as the Romanche and Charlie-Gibbs Fracture Zones in the Atlantic, and the Mendocino and Clarion Transforms in the Pacific), these faults accommodate horizontal motion between major tectonic plates without generating or destroying lithosphere.
(b) Tear faults INCORRECT
Why it is incorrect: A tear fault is a localized intraplate strike-slip fault that develops within a single thrust sheet or hanging wall block, accommodating differential forward motion or lateral termination of thrust nappes. Tear faults are minor structural features confined to individual fold-thrust belts and are not regional lithospheric plate boundaries.
(c) Wrench faults INCORRECT
Why it is incorrect: Wrench fault (or transcurrent fault) is an older descriptive term for large, high-angle strike-slip faults that occur within continental plates (e.g., the Great Glen Fault in Scotland). Crucially, transcurrent/wrench faults terminate at tip lines within intact crust rather than ending at plate boundary junctions, and their slip diminishes toward both ends.
(d) Scissor faults INCORRECT
Why it is incorrect: A scissor fault (or pivotal fault) is a fault characterized by differential rotation around an axis perpendicular to the fault plane, such that displacement increases in opposite directions away from a neutral pivot point. It is not an oceanic plate boundary.

8 High-Yield Practice Questions on Transform Faults & Strike-Slip Tectonics
1. Who first proposed the existence and mechanics of transform faults in 1965?
(a) Alfred Wegener    (b) Harry Hess    (c) J. Tuzo Wilson    (d) Fred Vine
Answer: (c) J. Tuzo Wilson — Wilson published the foundational paper demonstrating that transform faults link divergent and convergent plate boundaries.
2. Along an oceanic ridge-ridge transform fault, where do earthquakes occur?
(a) Across the entire length of the fracture zone extending across the whole ocean basin
(b) Exclusively along the active segment between the two offsetting ridge crests
(c) Only at the exact center of the fracture zone
(d) Earthquakes never occur on transform faults
Answer: (b) — Between the ridge segments, adjacent lithospheric plates move in opposite directions, generating seismicity; outside this active offset, plates move in the same direction at equal speeds, forming aseismic fracture zones.
3. If a mid-ocean ridge trending north-south is stepped to the right (dextral apparent offset), what is the actual sense of slip along the active transform fault?
(a) Dextral (right-lateral)    (b) Sinistral (left-lateral)    (c) Pure dip-slip normal    (d) Pure thrust
Answer: (b) Sinistral (left-lateral) — In a ridge-ridge transform, spreading from the two offset ridges forces the block between them to slide in the opposite sense (sinistral) to the apparent geographic offset of the ridge crests.
4. The San Andreas Fault in California is classified tectonically as a:
(a) Mid-plate intraplate fault
(b) Continental transform fault connecting the East Pacific Rise with the Mendocino Triple Junction
(c) Local tear fault in a fold belt
(d) Subduction megathrust
Answer: (b) — The San Andreas Fault is a classic right-lateral continental transform plate boundary connecting spreading ridges and triple junctions.
5. Oceanic fracture zones extending beyond the active ridge segments represent:
(a) Active subduction trenches
(b) Aseismic scars marking juxtaposed lithosphere of different ages and thermal subsidence histories
(c) Active mid-ocean ridges
(d) Collisional mountain belts
Answer: (b) — Fracture zones are fossil, inactive traces of transforms where crust of different ages (and therefore different depths) are juxtaposed.
6. Which term describes a regional strike-slip fault that terminates within a single tectonic plate without connecting other plate boundaries?
(a) Transform fault    (b) Transcurrent (wrench) fault    (c) Spreading center    (d) Benioff zone
Answer: (b) Transcurrent (wrench) fault — Transcurrent faults are intraplate features whose slip dies out toward both tips.
7. In a pull-apart basin formed along a strike-slip fault system, what type of stepover geometry is required in a right-lateral (dextral) fault?
(a) Left stepover (releasing bend)    (b) Right stepover (releasing bend)    (c) Planar continuous trace    (d) Perpendicular intersection
Answer: (b) Right stepover (releasing bend) — A right step on a right-lateral fault creates local crustal extension and subsidence, forming a rhomboidal pull-apart basin.
8. What is the fundamental boundary condition that distinguishes conservative plate boundaries from convergent and divergent boundaries?
(a) Lithosphere is created
(b) Lithosphere is consumed
(c) Lithospheric surface area is conserved (neither created nor destroyed)
(d) Rocks are converted to diamond
Answer: (c) — Conservative boundaries (transforms) preserve total surface area of oceanic/continental lithosphere.
