1. Thermodynamics & Parameters of Primary Magma Generation

A primary magma is defined as a pristine melt generated directly by the partial melting of a deep mantle or crustal source rock, which has reached the surface or emplaced in the crust without having undergone subsequent fractional crystallization, crustal assimilation, or magma mixing.

The definitive thermodynamic and geochemical composition of any primary magma is rigorously governed by four interdependent physicochemical variables (Winter, 2014, p. 205):

Demonstrating how variations in source composition, volatile fugacity ($H_2O, CO_2$), pressure ($P$), and degree of partial melting ($F$) dictate the primary magma composition.

The Four Master Parameters Governing Primary Melt Chemistry

  1. (i) Composition of the Source Material: The bulk chemical and mineralogical composition of the mantle or crustal protolith sets the elemental and isotopic baseline. For instance, melting of fertile lherzolite ($Ol + Opx + Cpx + \text{Al-phase}$) yields basaltic liquids, whereas melting of depleted harzburgite ($Ol + Opx$) produces silica-rich, refractory boninites. Metasomatized mantle enriched in incompatible elements generates alkaline suites.
  2. (ii) Relative Concentrations of Volatiles ($H_2O$, $CO_2$): Dissolved volatiles fundamentally alter liquidus/solidus phase boundaries and silica activity:
    • Water ($H_2O$): Expands the stability field of olivine relative to orthopyroxene via incongruent melting ($\text{Enstatite} + \text{Liquid} \rightleftharpoons \text{Forsterite} + \text{Liquid}$), shifting primary melts toward silica-saturation and oversaturation (quartz-normative tholeiites and calc-alkaline andesites).
    • Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$): Depresses silica activity by stabilizing orthopyroxene over olivine and promotes carbonate complexing ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$), shifting cotectics toward extreme silica-undersaturation, generating primary nephelinites, melilitites, basanites, and carbonatites.
  3. (iii) Confining Pressure ($P$ / Depth of Melting): With increasing confining pressure, the eutectic/cotectic minima in basaltic systems shift systematically toward the nepheline/olivine-rich apex:
    • Shallow Depths ($P < 1.0\text{ GPa}, < 30\text{ km}$): High silica activity, yielding primary quartz-normative tholeiitic basalts.
    • Intermediate Depths ($1.0\text{--}2.5\text{ GPa}, 30\text{--}75\text{ km}$): Transitional to olivine-tholeiitic and alkali olivine basalts.
    • Deep Mantle ($P > 2.5\text{--}3.0\text{ GPa}, > 80\text{--}100\text{ km}$): Extremely silica-undersaturated liquids (basanites, melilitites, kimberlitic melts).
  4. (iv) Degree of Partial Melting ($F$): The mass fraction of melt generated ($F$) strictly governs major and trace element concentrations according to the batch melting equation. Incompatible elements ($D \ll 1$) and volatile-loving alkalis are concentrated at low degrees of melting ($F < 5\%$, generating alkali basalts and nephelinites), whereas high degrees of melting ($F = 15\text{--}25\%$) dilute incompatible elements to generate Mid-Ocean Ridge Basalts (N-MORB). Ultra-high degrees of melting ($F > 30\text{--}40\%$) dissolve refractory olivine and enstatite, yielding Archean komatiites ($MgO > 18\text{--}30\text{ wt}\%$).
2. Quantitative Melting Models: Batch vs. Fractional Melting

The geochemical evolution of a primary magma as a function of the degree of partial melting ($F$) is mathematically described by two end-member models established by D.M. Shaw (1970):

1. Equilibrium (Batch) Melting Equation:
$$\frac{C_L}{C_0} = \frac{1}{D_0 + F(1 - D_0)}$$
where $C_L$ is trace element concentration in the primary melt, $C_0$ is source concentration, $F$ is melt fraction ($0 \le F \le 1$), and $D_0$ is bulk distribution coefficient ($\sum X_i K_{d,i}$).
2. Fractional (Rayleigh) Melting Equation:
$$\frac{C_L}{C_0} = \frac{1}{D_0} (1 - F)^{\frac{1}{D_0} - 1}$$

Behavior of Highly Incompatible Elements ($D_0 \to 0$)

For elements such as $Rb, Ba, Th, U, Nb$, where $D_0 \approx 0$, the batch melting equation simplifies directly to:

$$\frac{C_L}{C_0} \approx \frac{1}{F}$$

This fundamental inverse relationship demonstrates that at $F = 2\%$ ($0.02$), incompatible elements are enriched 50-fold in the primary magma relative to the mantle source. At $F = 20\%$ ($0.20$), the enrichment factor drops to only 5-fold.

3. Petrologic Diagnostic Criteria for True Primary Mantle Magmas

Because magmas inevitably cool and differentiate during ascent through the lithosphere, true primary magmas are rare in the geological record. Igneous petrologists apply strict geochemical filters to verify primary status:

  • Mg-Number ($Mg\#$): Defined as $\text{Mg}\# = \frac{\text{Mg}^{2+}}{\text{Mg}^{2+} + \text{Fe}^{2+}} \times 100$ (in molar units). Melts in thermodynamic equilibrium with mantle peridotitic olivine ($\text{Fo}_{89\text{--}92}$) using Roeder & Emslie's (1970) exchange partition coefficient $K_{D(\text{Fe-Mg})}^{\text{ol-liq}} = 0.30 \pm 0.03$ must possess: $$\mathbf{Mg\# = 68\text{ to } 75}$$
  • High Compatible Transition Elements: High concentrations of elements compatible in mantle olivine and chromite: $$\mathbf{Ni > 400\text{--}500\text{ ppm}} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{Cr > 1000\text{ ppm}}$$
  • Mantle Xenoliths: The presence of dense, angular mantle peridotite (spinel/garnet lherzolite) xenoliths proves rapid ascent from the mantle without intermediate crustal magma chamber staging.

Question 76
The composition of a primary magma depends on the following parameters : (i) Composition of the source material being partially melted, (ii) Relative concentrations of volatiles, (iii) Confining pressure, (iv) Degree of partial melting. Choose the correct set of answer from the following options :
(A) (i), (ii), (iii)
(B) (ii), (iii), (iv)
(C) (i), (iii), (iv)
(D) (i), (ii), (iii), (iv)
CORRECT ANSWER: (D)
(i), (ii), (iii), (iv): All four parameters strictly control the chemical, mineralogical, and normative composition of a primary magma during partial melting of the mantle or crust.
Forensic Analysis of Answer Choices
(A) (i), (ii), (iii) — INCORRECT

This option arbitrarily omits parameter (iv) Degree of partial melting ($F$). Melt fraction ($F$) is one of the most critical variables in igneous petrogenesis: small degrees of partial melting ($F = 1\text{--}5\%$) generate alkali-rich, silica-undersaturated melts (basanites, nephelinites), whereas large degrees ($F = 20\text{--}30\%$) produce tholeiitic and picritic liquids. Omitting (iv) violates elementary chemical thermodynamics.

(B) (ii), (iii), (iv) — INCORRECT

This option erroneously excludes parameter (i) Composition of the source material. The primary magma composition is fundamentally constrained by mass balance of the source protolith; a depleted peridotite source cannot generate an alkaline basalt rich in incompatible trace elements, nor can a pelitic crustal source generate an ultramafic picrite.

(C) (i), (iii), (iv) — INCORRECT

This option incorrectly omits parameter (ii) Relative concentrations of volatiles. Volatile fugacity ($f_{H_2O}, f_{CO_2}$) completely reconfigures mantle phase equilibria: $H_2O$ shifts melts toward quartz-normative silica-oversaturation (tholeiites and andesites), while $CO_2$ induces silica-undersaturation, generating carbonatites and melilitites.

(D) (i), (ii), (iii), (iv) — CORRECT

All four variables—source composition, volatile concentrations, confining pressure, and melt fraction ($F$)—are universally recognized in modern igneous petrology (Winter, 2014, p. 205; Best, 2003) as the comprehensive, governing set of parameters controlling primary magma chemistry.

Problem 1: Equilibrium Batch Melting Quantitative Calculation

A mantle peridotite source has an initial concentration of lanthanum ($C_0 = 0.65\text{ ppm}$). The bulk distribution coefficient for lanthanum during melting is $D_0 = 0.015$. Calculate the concentration of lanthanum in the primary melt ($C_L$) generated at: (a) $F = 2\%$ ($0.02$) partial melting (alkali basalt); and (b) $F = 20\%$ ($0.20$) partial melting (tholeiite).

Step-by-Step Batch Melting Derivation:
Using Shaw's equilibrium batch melting formula: $$C_L = \frac{C_0}{D_0 + F(1 - D_0)}$$
(a) For $F = 0.02$:
$$\text{Denominator} = 0.015 + 0.02(1 - 0.015) = 0.015 + 0.02(0.985) = 0.015 + 0.0197 = 0.0347$$
$$C_{L,2\%} = \frac{0.65}{0.0347} \approx \mathbf{18.73\text{ ppm}}$$ (Enrichment factor $\approx 28.8\times$)
(b) For $F = 0.20$:
$$\text{Denominator} = 0.015 + 0.20(0.985) = 0.015 + 0.1970 = 0.2120$$
$$C_{L,20\%} = \frac{0.65}{0.2120} \approx \mathbf{3.07\text{ ppm}}$$ (Enrichment factor $\approx 4.7\times$)
Conclusion: Low-degree partial melts are enriched in incompatible elements by a factor of more than 6 relative to high-degree tholeiitic melts.
Problem 2: Primary Melt Mg# Equilibrium with Mantle Olivine

Calculate the equilibrium molar $Mg\#$ of a primary basaltic liquid in equilibrium with residual mantle olivine of composition $\text{Fo}_{90}$ ($\text{Mg}\#_{\text{ol}} = 0.90$), given Roeder and Emslie's distribution coefficient $K_D = \frac{(\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Mg})_{\text{ol}}}{(\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Mg})_{\text{liq}}} = 0.30$.

Thermodynamic Exchange Derivation:
1. For olivine of $\text{Fo}_{90}$: $$\left(\frac{\text{Fe}^{2+}}{\text{Mg}}\right)_{\text{ol}} = \frac{1 - 0.90}{0.90} = \frac{0.10}{0.90} = 0.1111$$
2. Calculate liquid ratio: $$\left(\frac{\text{Fe}^{2+}}{\text{Mg}}\right)_{\text{liq}} = \frac{(\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Mg})_{\text{ol}}}{K_D} = \frac{0.1111}{0.30} \approx 0.3704$$
3. Calculate liquid $Mg\#$: $$\text{Mg}\#_{\text{liq}} = \frac{1}{1 + (\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Mg})_{\text{liq}}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.3704} = \frac{1}{1.3704} \approx 0.7297 \implies \mathbf{73.0\%}$$
Rule of Thumb: A primary mantle melt in equilibrium with $\text{Fo}_{90}$ must have an $\mathbf{Mg\# \approx 70\text{--}73\%}$. Basalts with $Mg\# < 65\%$ have experienced fractional crystallization of olivine.
Problem 3: Incongruent Melting of Enstatite and the Effect of Water

At 1 atmosphere under anhydrous conditions, enstatite melts incongruently: $$\text{Enstatite (Opx)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Forsterite (Ol)} + \text{Silica-rich Liquid}$$ Explain how dissolved water ($H_2O$) influences this reaction at elevated mantle pressures and why subduction-related magmas tend toward silica oversaturation.

Phase Equilibrium Mechanism:
- Anhydrous vs. Hydrous Liquidus Fields: In anhydrous mantle systems at high pressure ($P > 0.5\text{ GPa}$), enstatite melts congruently, and the olivine primary phase field contracts.
- Role of $H_2O$: Water dissolved as hydroxyl ions ($\text{OH}^-$) depolymerizes silica tetrahedra in the silicate melt ($Si\text{--}O\text{--}Si + H_2O \rightleftharpoons 2 Si\text{--}OH$), selectively depressing the liquidus of silicates. Crucially, water expands the liquidus stability field of olivine at the expense of orthopyroxene. Consequently, partial melting of hydrous mantle peridotite drives eutectic compositions toward the enstatite-quartz side of the ternary, generating primary quartz-normative, silica-saturated to oversaturated magmas (boninites and high-Mg andesites).
Problem 4: Carbon Dioxide and the Genesis of Carbonatitic Liquids

Explain the thermodynamic mechanism by which $CO_2$ causes extreme silica undersaturation in deep mantle partial melts, leading to primary nephelinites, melilitites, and carbonatites.

Thermodynamic Reaction:
In the deep mantle ($P > 2.0\text{--}2.5\text{ GPa}$, depths $> 70\text{ km}$), $CO_2$ dissolves not as molecular gas but by reacting with network-modifying cations to form carbonate complexes ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$). This stabilizes the carbonation reaction: $$\text{Enstatite} + \text{Dolomite} \rightleftharpoons \text{Forsterite} + \text{Diopside} + \text{CO}_2$$
The carbonate complexing drastically reduces silica activity in the melt ($a_{\text{SiO}_2} \ll 1$), shifting cotectic crystallization paths away from silica-saturated minerals and producing highly silica-undersaturated, alkali-rich primary liquids that can unmix via liquid immiscibility into silicate and carbonatite melts.
Problem 5: Archean Komatiite Petrogenesis & Mantle Potential Temperature ($T_p$)

Archean komatiite lavas possess $MgO = 28\text{ wt}\%$ and spinifex textures requiring extrusion temperatures of $T = 1580^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the degree of partial melting ($F$) and mantle potential temperature ($T_p$), explaining why komatiites ceased forming after the Proterozoic.

Thermal and Petrological Evaluation:
1. Melt Fraction ($F$): Generating a liquid with $28\text{ wt}\% MgO$ from a pyrolite mantle ($MgO \approx 38\text{ wt}\%$) requires melting nearly all pyroxene and garnet, leaving only refractory olivine ($\text{Fo}_{94}$), which corresponds to an ultra-high melt fraction of $F \approx 40\text{--}50\%$.
2. Mantle Potential Temperature ($T_p$): An extrusion temperature of $1580^\circ\text{C}$ corresponds to an adiabatic mantle potential temperature of $T_p \approx 1850\text{--}1900^\circ\text{C}$ (approximately $300\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$ hotter than the modern convecting asthenosphere, $T_p \approx 1350\text{--}1400^\circ\text{C}$).
3. Post-Archean Cessation: Radioactive secular cooling of Earth's interior reduced mantle temperatures, making large-scale $F > 40\%$ mantle melting impossible in the Phanerozoic.
Problem 6: Adiabatic Decompression vs. Thermal Influx Melting

Compare the physical mechanism of magma generation at mid-ocean ridges (adiabatic decompression) with subduction zone island arcs (flux melting). Draw schematic $P\text{-}T$ trajectories relative to the mantle solidus.

Geodynamic Melting Mechanisms:
1. Mid-Ocean Ridges (Decompression Melting): Solid asthenospheric peridotite rises buoyantly beneath spreading centers. Because rocks have very low thermal diffusivity ($\kappa \approx 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$), the ascent is virtually adiabatic ($dT/dP \approx 10\text{--}15^\circ\text{C/GPa}$). The adiabatic path crosses the dry peridotite solidus at $\sim 60\text{ km}$ ($P \approx 2\text{ GPa}$), generating MORB tholeiitic magma without adding external heat.
2. Subduction Zones (Flux Melting): The subducting oceanic lithosphere dewaters, releasing hydrous fluids ($H_2O + \text{solutes}$) into the overlying mantle wedge. Water depresses the peridotite solidus by $200\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$, causing the lowered wet solidus to cross the ambient mantle geotherm, triggering melting at constant or decreasing temperature.
Problem 7: Residual Mantle Mineralogy and Rare Earth Element (REE) Fractionation

Explain why Ocean Island Basalts (OIB) display steep, highly fractionated chondrite-normalized REE patterns with high $(\text{La/Yb})_N$ ratios ($> 15$), whereas Normal Mid-Ocean Ridge Basalts (N-MORB) display flat or depleted Heavy REE (HREE) slopes ($(\text{La/Yb})_N \approx 0.8\text{--}1.2$).

Mineralogical Partitioning Explanation:
- Garnet Compatibility: Garnet is the only common mantle mineral that strongly partitions Heavy Rare Earth Elements (HREE, e.g., $Lu, Yb$) over Light Rare Earth Elements (LREE, e.g., $La, Ce$), with $K_{d,\text{Yb}}^{\text{garnet-liq}} \approx 4\text{--}10 \gg 1$.
- OIB Genesis (Garnet Lherzolite Facies): OIB magmas originate from deep mantle plumes at depths $> 80\text{--}100\text{ km}$ ($P > 2.8\text{ GPa}$), where garnet is stable in the residual mantle. Garnet retains HREE in the residue, leaving the primary melt strongly depleted in HREE and resulting in steep $(\text{La/Yb})_N$ slopes.
- MORB Genesis (Spinel Lherzolite Facies): MORB melting occurs at shallower depths ($< 60\text{ km}$, $P < 2\text{ GPa}$), where spinel replaces garnet. Spinel does not fractionate HREE from LREE, producing flat HREE patterns.
Problem 8: Critical Melt Fraction ($F_c$) and Melt Extraction Permeability

In a partially melting porous mantle peridotite, define the critical melt fraction ($F_c$, or compaction threshold) and calculate whether melt can segregate when the wetting angle is $\theta = 48^\circ$ versus $\theta = 68^\circ$.

Fluid Dynamics of Melt Segregation:
- Dihedral (Wetting) Angle ($\theta$): Controls the geometry of melt droplets along solid grain edges. $$\text{If } \theta < 60^\circ: \text{Melt forms an interconnected, continuous network along 3-grain junctions}$$ $$\text{If } \theta > 60^\circ: \text{Melt remains trapped as isolated, non-connecting pockets at 4-grain intersections}$$
- Evaluation:
At $\theta = 48^\circ$ ($< 60^\circ$), basaltic melt forms an interconnected permeable network at extremely low porosities ($F < 0.1\text{--}1.0\%$), allowing efficient porous flow and matrix compaction to extract primary magma.
At $\theta = 68^\circ$ ($> 60^\circ$), melt cannot segregate until a high critical threshold ($F_c \approx 5\text{--}10\%$) is reached where isolated pockets coalesce.
