1. Definition, Etymology & Petrogenetic Architecture of Skarns

The term skarn is an old Swedish mining term originally applied by miners in the Bergslagen ore district of central Sweden to designate the tough, coarse-grained, calc-silicate gangue rocks enclosing iron-ore and sulfide deposits. In American geological literature, it is often termed tactite.

In modern metamorphic petrology and economic geology (Meinert et al., 2005; Winter, 2014, pp. 635–648; Bangar, p. 81), a skarn is formally defined as a coarse-grained, calc-silicate metamorphic rock or reaction zone developed in the contact area where an igneous intrusion invades carbonate sedimentary rocks (limestone, $\text{CaCO}_3$, or dolomite, $\text{CaMg(CO}_3)_2$).

Skarn formation is driven by high-temperature contact metasomatism: hot, silica- and iron-bearing hydrothermal fluids exsolved from the cooling magma react aggressively with the calcium- and magnesium-rich carbonate wall rocks, replacing calcite and dolomite with a complex suite of calc-silicate minerals.

Figure 120.1 — Contact Metasomatic Zonation and Skarn Evolution: Authentic textbook diagram from John D. Winter (Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology, p. 494). Illustrates the spatial development of Endoskarn (within the intrusive pluton margin) and Exoskarn (within the invaded carbonate bedrock), with concentric calc-silicate reaction zones (Garnet $\rightarrow$ Pyroxene $\rightarrow$ Wollastonite $\rightarrow$ Marble).

Spatial Anatomy: Exoskarn vs. Endoskarn

  • Exoskarn: Metasomatic replacement that develops outside the intrusion within the carbonate sedimentary host rock. Exoskarns typically comprise the vast majority of the skarn volume and host major metallic ore deposits.
  • Endoskarn: Metasomatic replacement that develops within the margin of the igneous intrusion itself. Inward diffusion of calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) from dissolving limestone alters the granitic magma/rock, converting primary quartz and K-feldspar into secondary plagioclase, diopsidic pyroxene, epidote, and scapolite.

Depending on whether the invaded carbonate protolith is a pure limestone or a magnesian dolomite, two major geochemical skarn suites develop:

The Four Sequential Stages of Skarn Evolution

  1. Stage 1: Isochemical Contact Metamorphism (Early Thermal): Conductive heating of carbonate beds before significant fluid release. Produces fine-grained, barren calc-silicate hornfelses and crystalline marble.
  2. Stage 2: Prograde Metasomatism (High-Temperature Magmatic Fluids, $T \approx 500\text{--}700^\circ\text{C}$): Exsolution of saline, supercritical magmatic-hydrothermal fluids carrying $\text{Si, Fe, Al, Mg}$. Massive fluid infiltration drives decarbonation, crystallizing coarse-grained anhydrous garnet, pyroxene, and wollastonite.
  3. Stage 3: Retrograde Alteration & Ore Deposition ($T \approx 300\text{--}450^\circ\text{C}$): As the pluton cools, meteoric groundwaters collapse inward, mixing with hydrothermal brines. Early anhydrous silicates are hydrously altered (garnet $\rightarrow$ epidote; pyroxene $\rightarrow$ actinolite/tremolite). This cooling and pH-drop triggers the massive precipitation of economic metal sulfides and oxides.
  4. Stage 4: Post-Metasomatic Carbonatization ($T < 300^\circ\text{C}$): Final deposition of low-temperature calcite, quartz, and fluorite veins in open fractures.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 120: Metamorphic zone developed in the contact area around igneous rock intrusions when carbonate sedimentary rocks are invaded called :
(A) Carbonatites
(B) Metamorphites
(C) Skarn
(D) Hornblendite
Verified Correct Answer: (C) Skarn
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Refuted — Carbonatites

Incorrect: Carbonatites are rare igneous rocks of mantle origin composed of greater than $50\%$ primary carbonate minerals (calcite, dolomite, ankerite). They crystallize directly from mantle-derived carbonatite magmas associated with continental rifts and alkaline complexes (e.g., Oldoinyo Lengai in Tanzania, Amba Dongar in Gujarat). They are not contact metamorphic reaction zones formed around invaded carbonate sediments.

Option (B) Refuted — Metamorphites

Incorrect: "Metamorphites" is an obsolete, non-specific general German term (Metamorphite) loosely referring to any metamorphic rock in the broadest sense. It does not designate the specific contact metasomatic zone developed between igneous intrusions and invaded carbonate sedimentary rocks.

Option (C) Confirmed — Skarn

Correct: By universal definition in metamorphic petrology and economic mineralogy (Meinert et al., 2005; Winter, 2014, p. 635; Bangar, p. 81), skarn (or tactite) is the definitive geological term for the contact metasomatic zone and calc-silicate rock body developed when an igneous intrusion invades carbonate sedimentary rocks (limestone or dolomite).

Option (D) Refuted — Hornblendite

Incorrect: Hornblendite is a coarse-grained ultramafic igneous plutonic rock composed of $> 90\%$ hornblende amphibole. It is neither a contact metamorphic zone nor derived from invaded carbonate rocks.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A granitic pluton intrudes a sequence of pure limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$). At the contact, a prominent white bladed calc-silicate mineral forms according to the reaction $\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{SiO}_2 \rightarrow \text{Mineral X} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$. Identify Mineral X, name the reaction type, and calculate its equilibrium condition.

Solution: - Mineral X: Wollastonite ($\text{CaSiO}_3$), a triclinic single-chain inosilicate. - Reaction Type: Decarbonation contact metasomatic reaction. - Equilibrium Condition: In the presence of silica-rich magmatic fluids, calcite reacts with dissolved silica: $$\text{CaCO}_3\text{ (calcite)} + \text{SiO}_2\text{ (quartz/fluid silica)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CaSiO}_3\text{ (wollastonite)} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$ At a shallow contact pressure of $P = 1.0\text{ kbar}$, this reaction proceeds to the right at temperatures of $550^\circ\text{C}$ to $650^\circ\text{C}$ (depending on $X_{\text{CO}_2}$), defining the innermost wollastonite zone of calcic exoskarns.

Practice Problem 2

Explain why garnets in calcic skarns belong to the "Grandite" series rather than the "Pyralspite" series.

Solution: Garnet group minerals are divided into two solid-solution series based on chemistry: - Pyralspite (Pyrope-Almandine-Spessartine): Calcium-poor, aluminous garnets characteristic of pelitic schists and mantle peridotites. - Ugrandite / Grandite (Grossular-Andradite): Calcium-rich garnets ($\text{Ca}_3\text{(Al,Fe}^{3+})_2\text{Si}_3\text{O}_{12}$). Because the host rock of a skarn is limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$), the system is flooded with calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$). Concurrently, magmatic hydrothermal fluids introduce $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ and $\text{Al}^{3+}$. Consequently, skarn garnets crystallize exclusively as Grossular ($\text{Ca}_3\text{Al}_2\text{Si}_3\text{O}_{12}$) - Andradite ($\text{Ca}_3\text{Fe}_2\text{Si}_3\text{O}_{12}$) solid solutions (Grandites), frequently displaying spectacular oscillatory optical zoning and anomalous birefringence under crossed polars.

Practice Problem 3

What is an "endoskarn" and what mineralogical transformation occurs within the margin of an invaded granodiorite pluton?

Solution: An endoskarn is the metasomatically altered zone developed within the intrusive igneous rock itself bordering the carbonate contact. Diffusive mass transfer forces calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) from the dissolving limestone inward into the igneous margin, while silica ($\text{SiO}_2$) and alkalis diffuse outward. This calcium enrichment destroys primary igneous biotite, hornblende, and quartz, converting the granodiorite margin into an endoskarn assemblage of calcic plagioclase, diopsidic pyroxene, epidote, and scapolite.

Practice Problem 4

Distinguish between a "calcic skarn" and a "magnesian skarn" in terms of carbonate protolith and characteristic pyroxene/olivine mineralogy.

Solution: - Calcic Skarn: Formed in pure limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$); pyroxenes belong to the diopside-hedenbergite series ($\text{Ca(Mg,Fe)Si}_2\text{O}_6$); olivine is absent; dominant garnet is andradite/grossular. - Magnesian Skarn: Formed in dolomite ($\text{CaMg(CO}_3)_2$); dominated by magnesium-rich phases: forsterite olivine ($\text{Mg}_2\text{SiO}_4$), pure diopside, spinel ($\text{MgAl}_2\text{O}_4$), humite group minerals, and serpentine/talc retrograde pseudomorphs.

Practice Problem 5

Why does economic sulfide ore mineralization (e.g., chalcopyrite, sphalerite, galena) precipitate predominantly during the retrograde stage of skarn evolution rather than during prograde metasomatism?

Solution: During early prograde metasomatism ($550\text{--}700^\circ\text{C}$), fluids are ultra-hot, highly saline, oxidized, and slightly acidic, keeping metals in soluble chloride complexes ($\text{CuCl}_2^-, \text{ZnCl}_4^{2-}$). During the retrograde stage ($300\text{--}450^\circ\text{C}$): 1. Fluid temperatures drop drastically. 2. Meteoric water incursion dilutes salinity and neutralizes acidity via extensive reaction with surrounding marble. 3. Sulfur activity rises as hydrous silicates form. This cooling and pH neutralization destabilizes metal chloride complexes, driving the spontaneous, massive precipitation of metal sulfides (chalcopyrite, sphalerite, galena) replacing earlier anhydrous garnets and pyroxenes.

Practice Problem 6

What is a "stratiform skarn" (or mantos) and what geological control dictates its geometry?

Solution: A stratiform skarn (manto skarn) is a tabular, bedding-parallel skarn body that extends horizontally away from a central feeder dike or fault for hundreds of meters along a specific, highly receptive sedimentary carbonate bed. Its geometry is controlled by lithological permeability and chemical reactivity: hydrothermal fluids preferentially infiltrate a porous, reactive limestone bed sandwiched between impermeable shale or sandstone aquitards.

Practice Problem 7

In exploration geochemistry, what diagnostic heavy mineral found in panned stream concentrates serves as a pathfinder for tungsten skarns?

Solution: The primary pathfinder mineral is Scheelite ($\text{CaWO}_4$). Scheelite is heavy (specific gravity $5.9\text{--}6.1\text{ g/cm}^3$) and highly resistant to mechanical abrasion, concentrating in stream sediments. Crucially, scheelite exhibits brilliant bright sky-blue to creamy-yellow fluorescence under shortwave ultraviolet (UV) light ($254\text{ nm}$), allowing field geologists to detect trace grains in night pan-sampling.

Practice Problem 8

How does the formation of a skarn zone impact geotechnical tunneling and foundation engineering in carbonate terrains?

Solution: Skarns introduce extreme, erratic mechanical heterogeneity into a rock mass: 1. Abrasiveness: Prograde skarn zones containing grandite garnet and pyroxene have exceptionally high unconfined compressive strength ($\sigma_c > 200\text{--}300\text{ MPa}$) and high Cerchar abrasiveness, causing severe wear on Tunnel Boring Machine (TBM) cutter discs. 2. Water Inrush Hazards: Retrograde skarn zones and marble contact interfaces frequently harbor cavernous dissolution voids, open vugs, and intense jointing that channel high-pressure groundwater inrushes into excavations.
