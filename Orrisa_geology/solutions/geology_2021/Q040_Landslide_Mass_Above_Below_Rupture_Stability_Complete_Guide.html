1. The Anatomical Anatomy of a Landslide: Rupture Surface Mechanics

In slope engineering and geotechnical geomorphology, a landslide is defined according to the Varnes (1978) and Cruden & Varnes (1996) international classification as the down-slope movement of a mass of rock, debris, or earth under the direct influence of gravity.

  • Surface of Rupture (Slip Surface / Shear Plane): The continuous or composite subterranean boundary surface separating the unstable moving slide mass from the stable, undisturbed, stationary parent rock or soil beneath it.
    • In rotational slides (slumps): The rupture surface is characteristically curved, spoon-shaped, and concave upward.
    • In translational slides (planar slides): The rupture surface is predominantly planar, controlled by preexisting structural discontinuities (bedding planes, joints, foliation, or fault contacts).
  • Mass Above the Surface of Rupture (The Displaced Mass): The portion of soil or rock that has undergone internal shear strain, structural disruption, and downslope translation. Because mobilizing gravitational shear stresses have exceeded shear resistance ($\tau > \tau_f$), this entire displaced body is dynamically unstable.
  • Mass Below the Surface of Rupture (The Stable In-Situ Substratum): The underlying intact bedrock, unyielding regolith, or firm basement that remains anchored, coherent, and stationary. The shear stress within this lower mass remains well within its elastic yield limit ($\tau \ll \tau_f$), rendering it fundamentally stable.

2. Geotechnical Criterion: Factor of Safety Inversion Across the Failure Plane

The stability state of any slope element is mathematically quantified by the Factor of Safety ($FS$):

Mohr-Coulomb Limit Equilibrium & Factor of Safety
$$FS = \frac{\tau_f}{\tau_d} = \frac{c' + (\sigma_n - u)\tan\phi'}{\tau_d}$$

Where $c'$ is effective cohesion, $\sigma_n$ is total normal stress, $u$ is pore water pressure, $\phi'$ is effective internal friction angle, and $\tau_d$ is the driving gravitational shear stress ($\tau_d = \rho g z \sin\beta$).

  • Along and Above the Rupture Surface ($FS \le 1.0$): When triggering factors (heavy precipitation, pore pressure rise $\Delta u$, toe undercut, seismic acceleration) reduce effective stress ($\sigma' = \sigma_n - u \to 0$), the factor of safety drops below unity ($FS < 1.0$), initiating shear failure and active sliding.
  • Below the Rupture Surface ($FS > 1.5$): The intact bedrock below the basal detachment experiences high confining stresses, low pore pressure, and possesses unweathered rock mass cohesion ($c_{\text{rock}} \gg c_{\text{soil}}$), ensuring $FS \gg 1.0$.
  • The Fundamental Rule: Therefore, across the landslide rupture plane, the distinguishing feature is invariant: the mass above is unstable, whereas the mass below is stable.

3. Varnes Classification of Slope Movements

Rotational Slide (Slump) Curved, concave upward; circular cylindrical. Backward rotation of blocks toward the scarp; highly disrupted and unstable. Stationary, intact substratum forming the spoon-shaped sliding basin.
Translational Slide (Planar) Planar discontinuity (bedding, foliation, fault). Translates downslope parallel to planar dip; disaggregates into blocks. Unyielding footwall block with dip angle $\theta < \phi'$.

Official Examination Question
Question 40
What is the distinguishing feature of the mass above and below a landslide ?
(A) Both masses are stable
(B) Mass above is stable, whereas mass below is unstable
(C) Mass above is unstable, whereas mass below is stable
(D) Both masses are unstable
Verified Correct Answer: (C) Mass above is unstable, whereas mass below is stable [The displaced sliding mass situated above the shear rupture plane has suffered shear failure and motion ($FS < 1$), while the intact substratum below the plane remains anchored and stable ($FS > 1$).]
Correct Option: (C) Mass above is unstable, whereas mass below is stable

Detailed Geotechnical Forensic Analysis:

  • By standard geological and geotechnical definition (Varnes 1978; Cruden & Varnes 1996; K.M. Bangar Ch. 10), a landslide consists of a body of displaced rock or soil moving along a discrete surface of rupture (slip plane).
  • The material situated above the surface of rupture represents the active slide mass that has overcome its shear strength threshold ($\tau > \tau_f$); it undergoes translation, rotation, cracking, and downslope displacement, making it unambiguously unstable.
  • The material situated below the surface of rupture is the intact, undisturbed bedrock or unyielding substratum that did not fail and remains structurally anchored in place, making it unequivocally stable.
  • Thus, the distinguishing feature is that the mass above is unstable, whereas the mass below is stable.
Incorrect Distractor: (B) Mass above is stable, whereas mass below is unstable

Detailed Refutation:

  • This option completely reverses the physical reality of slope failure.
  • If the mass above were stable, no downhill displacement or sliding would occur. Gravitational potential energy drives the movement of the overlying mass, not the subterranean substrate beneath the rupture surface.
  • Therefore, claiming the mass above is stable while the mass below is unstable is physically absurd.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) Both masses are stable

Detailed Refutation:

  • If both the mass above and below the shear surface were stable, there would be no slope movement, and by definition, no landslide could occur.
  • A landslide intrinsically implies active or past failure and displacement of the slope mass. Thus, both masses cannot be stable.
Incorrect Distractor: (D) Both masses are unstable

Detailed Refutation:

  • A landslide requires a stationary reference foundation across which displacement takes place.
  • The slip surface marks the exact boundary where shear failure ceases. Below this basal boundary, the Earth's crust is stationary and stable. If the entire underlying crust were also moving, it would represent whole-lithosphere tectonic drift or subduction, not a surficial landslide.
  • Hence, claiming both masses are unstable is incorrect.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Slope Stability & Landslide Engineering
Problem 1: Infinite Slope Factor of Safety in Dry Sand

A planar landslide occurs in dry cohesionless sand ($c' = 0$) with internal friction angle $\phi' = 35^\circ$. If the slope angle is $\beta = 28^\circ$, calculate the Factor of Safety ($FS$) against sliding.

Step-by-Step Solution:

For an infinite slope in dry cohesionless material, the Factor of Safety is independent of depth:

$$FS = \frac{\tan\phi'}{\tan\beta}$$ $$\tan 35^\circ \approx 0.7002, \qquad \tan 28^\circ \approx 0.5317$$ $$FS = \frac{0.7002}{0.5317} \approx 1.317$$

Final Answer: The slope is stable with a Factor of Safety of $1.32$.

Problem 2: Influence of Groundwater Seepage on Stability

For the same slope ($\beta = 28^\circ$, $\phi' = 35^\circ$), calculate the Factor of Safety when groundwater seepage occurs parallel to the slope with the water table at the ground surface. Assume saturated unit weight $\gamma_{\text{sat}} = 20\text{ kN/m}^3$ and water unit weight $\gamma_w = 9.81\text{ kN/m}^3$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

With steady seepage parallel to the slope face, the buoyant unit weight $\gamma' = \gamma_{\text{sat}} - \gamma_w = 20 - 9.81 = 10.19\text{ kN/m}^3$ operates in the shear resistance:

$$FS = \left(\frac{\gamma'}{\gamma_{\text{sat}}}\right) \frac{\tan\phi'}{\tan\beta}$$ $$FS = \left(\frac{10.19}{20.0}\right) \times 1.317 = 0.5095 \times 1.317 \approx 0.671$$

Final Answer: The Factor of Safety drops to $0.67$ ($FS < 1.0$), causing catastrophic liquefaction sliding!

Problem 3: Critical Slide Depth for a Cohesive Soil

A clay slope with cohesion $c' = 24\text{ kPa}$, $\phi' = 0$, unit weight $\gamma = 18\text{ kN/m}^3$, and slope angle $\beta = 30^\circ$ experiences planar translation. Calculate the critical depth $z_c$ at which failure ($FS = 1.0$) initiates.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Driving shear stress along a plane at depth $z$: $\tau_d = \gamma z \sin\beta \cos\beta$. Resisting shear strength: $\tau_f = c'$.

$$FS = \frac{c'}{\gamma z \sin\beta \cos\beta} = 1.0 \implies z_c = \frac{c'}{\gamma \sin\beta \cos\beta}$$ $$\sin 30^\circ = 0.5, \qquad \cos 30^\circ = 0.8660 \implies \sin 30^\circ \cos 30^\circ = 0.4330$$ $$z_c = \frac{24\text{ kPa}}{18\text{ kN/m}^3 \times 0.4330} = \frac{24}{7.794} \approx 3.08\text{ m}$$

Final Answer: The critical depth of the rupture surface is $3.08\text{ m}$.

Problem 4: Remediation by Toe Unloading vs. Toe Weighting

Explain why excavating the toe of a landslide increases instability, while dumping rockfill at the toe stabilizes the slope.

Step-by-Step Solution:

In a rotational slump, the failure surface curves upward at the toe. The mass of soil at the toe creates a restoring moment about the center of rotation: $M_R = W_{\text{toe}} \cdot x_{\text{toe}}$. Excavating the toe removes this stabilizing resisting moment, reducing the overall Factor of Safety and triggering sliding. Conversely, adding a rockfill berm (counterweight) at the toe increases the restoring moment, driving $FS > 1.5$.

Problem 5: Pore Water Pressure Ratio ($r_u$) Calculation

Define the pore pressure ratio $r_u$ and calculate its value at a slip surface depth $z = 8\text{ m}$ in soil with $\gamma = 19.6\text{ kN/m}^3$ when the pore water pressure is $u = 45\text{ kPa}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

The pore pressure ratio is defined as the ratio of pore water pressure to total overburden vertical stress:

$$r_u = \frac{u}{\gamma z}$$ $$r_u = \frac{45\text{ kPa}}{19.6\text{ kN/m}^3 \times 8\text{ m}} = \frac{45}{156.8} \approx 0.287$$

Final Answer: The pore pressure ratio is $r_u = 0.29$.

Problem 6: Seismic Pseudostatic Coefficient ($k_h$)

During an earthquake with peak horizontal acceleration $a_h = 0.25g$, calculate the pseudostatic seismic driving force on a landslide block of mass $M = 50,000\text{ metric tons}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$F_{eq} = k_h W = \left(\frac{a_h}{g}\right) M g = 0.25 \times (50,000 \times 10^3\text{ kg}) \times 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$$ $$F_{eq} = 0.25 \times 4.905 \times 10^8\text{ N} = 1.226 \times 10^8\text{ N} = 122.6\text{ MN}$$

Final Answer: The earthquake exerts an additional downslope horizontal inertial force of $122.6\text{ Meganewtons}$.

Problem 7: Rock Slope Planar Failure Daylighting Rule

State the structural kinematic daylighting condition required for a planar slide to occur on a rock slope with face dip $\alpha_f$ and joint plane dip $\alpha_p$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

For planar rock slope failure to be kinematically possible (Markland's test):

  1. The joint plane must strike approximately parallel to the slope face (within $\pm 20^\circ$).
  2. The joint dip must be less than the slope face dip ($\alpha_p < \alpha_f$), meaning the joint daylights on the slope face.
  3. The joint dip must exceed the friction angle of the joint surface ($\alpha_p > \phi$).
  4. Combined kinematic criterion: $\phi < \alpha_p < \alpha_f$.
Problem 8: Landslide Early Warning via Inclinometers

How does a borehole inclinometer detect the exact depth of the landslide surface of rupture prior to catastrophic ground collapse?

Step-by-Step Solution:

A grooved casing is anchored deep into the stable bedrock below the suspected slide zone. An inclinometer probe measures casing tilt profile with depth over time. Above the rupture surface, the moving unstable mass displaces laterally, producing a sharp, discrete step (shear kink) in the cumulative displacement profile at the exact elevation of the slip plane. Below this depth, displacement remains zero, proving that the underlying substratum is stable.
