1. The Four Fundamental Resolutions of Remote Sensing

The capability of an imaging sensor to capture and discriminate ground features is governed by the Four Fundamental Resolutions of remote sensing (Lillesand et al., 2015; Jensen, 2007):

  • (A) Spatial Resolution: The measure of the smallest angular or linear separation between two objects that can be resolved by the sensor, dictating the clarity, sharpness, and spatial detail of ground objects. It is quantitatively represented by the Instantaneous Field of View (IFOV) and the Ground Sampling Distance (GSD).
  • (B) Spectral Resolution: The number and width of spectral bands in the electromagnetic spectrum recorded by the sensor (e.g., panchromatic vs. multispectral vs. hyperspectral). It governs the ability to discriminate materials by their absorption signatures.
  • (C) Temporal Resolution: The revisit time or frequency at which a satellite sensor images the exact same geographic location (e.g., 16 days for Landsat, 5 days for Sentinel-2, 15 minutes for INSAT-3D).
  • (D) Radiometric Resolution: The sensitivity of the detector to subtle differences in radiant energy flux, expressed as quantization bit depth (e.g., 8-bit = 256 gray levels; 12-bit = 4096 levels; 14-bit = 16,384 levels).
Ground Sampling Distance (GSD) & IFOV Geometry
$$D = H' \times \beta = H' \times \frac{d}{f}$$

Where $D$ is ground spatial resolution (GSD), $H'$ is flying height above ground level, $\beta$ is instantaneous field of view in radians, $d$ is physical detector element dimension, and $f$ is focal length of the optics.

2. Physical Determinants of Ground Object Clarity

The visual "clarity" or geometric sharpness of an object in satellite imagery depends strictly on whether the object's spatial dimensions exceed the sensor's ground resolution element:

  • Sub-Pixel Mixing: If an object is smaller than the spatial resolution cell (e.g., a $5\text{ m}$ road inside a $30\text{ m}$ Landsat pixel), its radiance is averaged with surrounding soil and vegetation, losing all geometric clarity (a "mixed pixel").
  • Nyquist Sampling Criterion: To reliably detect and identify a geometric target (such as a building or fault trace), the spatial resolution must be at least twice as fine as the target dimension ($GSD \le \frac{1}{2} L_{\text{object}}$).
  • Diffraction-Limited Resolution: By the Rayleigh optical criterion, angular resolution $\theta = 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D_{\text{aperture}}}$. Achieving sharper ground clarity requires larger telescope apertures ($D_{\text{aperture}}$) or lower orbital altitudes ($H$).
"Spatial resolution is an indication of how well a sensor can resolve small objects on the ground. Images where only large features are distinguishable have coarse or low spatial resolution; images where small objects can be identified have fine or high spatial resolution." — Thomas M. Lillesand et al., Remote Sensing and Image Interpretation (7th Ed., 2015, p. 32)
3. The Engineering Trade-off: Spatial vs. Spectral vs. Temporal

In satellite sensor design, physics imposes a fundamental trade-off governed by data rate and optical signal-to-noise ratio ($SNR$):

  • Fine spatial resolution (e.g., WorldView-3 at $0.3\text{ m}$) requires collecting photons from a tiny ground area, forcing bands to be spectrally broad (panchromatic) to capture sufficient photons.
  • Fine spectral resolution (e.g., AVIRIS hyperspectral with 224 narrow $10\text{ nm}$ bands) requires larger spatial pixels ($20\text{ m}$) to gather sufficient photons per band.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 14)
Q14. Clarity of ground object will depend upon :
(A) Spatial Resolution  ✔
(B) Spectral Resolution
(C) Temporal Resolution
(D) Radiometric Resolution
✔
Correct Answer: (A) Spatial Resolution
The geometric clarity, boundary definition, and spatial sharpness of ground objects in remote sensing imagery are directly governed by the sensor's Spatial Resolution (ground pixel footprint size / GSD).
(A) Spatial Resolution Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Spatial resolution specifies the smallest physical dimension on the ground that can be resolved as an independent image pixel. Finer spatial resolution (e.g., $0.5\text{ m}$ vs. $30\text{ m}$) yields crisp, sharp object boundaries, allowing fine features like roads, buildings, drainage rills, and fault scarps to be identified with complete visual clarity.
(B) Spectral Resolution Incorrect (Governs Compositional Discrimination, Not Geometric Clarity)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Spectral resolution refers to the bandwidth and number of spectral channels. High spectral resolution allows an analyst to differentiate between subtle rock types or minerals (e.g., kaolinite vs. illite), but does not make a blurred spatial shape appear geometrically sharp.
(C) Temporal Resolution Incorrect (Governs Revisit Frequency)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Temporal resolution is the revisit frequency of the satellite (e.g., daily vs. every 16 days). It controls how frequently a dynamic phenomenon (flood, crop growth) can be monitored over time, but has zero influence on the geometric spatial clarity of a single image.
(D) Radiometric Resolution Incorrect (Governs Gray-Level Brightness Sensitivity)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Radiometric resolution measures the detector's sensitivity to subtle brightness variations (bit depth: 8-bit, 12-bit). While higher bit depth prevents saturation in bright clouds or dark shadows, it cannot resolve features smaller than the spatial pixel footprint.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): An aerial mapping camera with a focal length $f = 152.4\text{ mm}$ and a CCD pixel pitch $d = 6.0\ \mu\text{m}$ flies at an altitude $H' = 1524\text{ m}$ above the terrain. Calculate the theoretical Ground Sampling Distance (GSD).
    ✔ Worked Answer: $\text{GSD} = 0.060\text{ m}$ ($6.0\text{ cm}$).
    Calculation:
    $$\text{Scale Factor } S_f = \frac{H'}{f} = \frac{1524\text{ m}}{0.1524\text{ m}} = 10,000$$
    $$\text{GSD} = d \times S_f = (6.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}) \times 10,000 = 0.060\text{ m} = 6.0\text{ cm}$$
  • Practice Q2: What is a mixed pixel (mixel), and how does it degrade image clarity?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A pixel covering more than one land-cover class whose recorded digital number is an area-weighted composite average.
    Explanation: Coarse spatial resolution forces multiple disparate surfaces (e.g., $30\%$ water, $40\%$ asphalt, $30\%$ grass) into a single pixel, obscuring object boundaries.
  • Practice Q3: What process is used in digital image processing to fuse high-spatial-resolution panchromatic data with lower-spatial-resolution multispectral data?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Pan-sharpening (e.g., Gram-Schmidt, IHS, or Wavelet transform fusion).
    Explanation: Pan-sharpening replaces the low-resolution intensity component of multispectral imagery with high-resolution panchromatic data, producing sharp color imagery.
  • Practice Q4: By what factor does image file data volume increase if the spatial resolution (GSD) of an area is improved by a factor of 2 (e.g., from $30\text{ m}$ to $15\text{ m}$)?
    ✔ Worked Answer: Increases by a factor of 4 ($2^2 = 4$).
    Explanation: Halving linear pixel dimension doubles pixel count along both the X and Y axes ($2 \times 2 = 4$), quadrupling the total number of pixels.
  • Practice Q5: How is Modulation Transfer Function (MTF) used to define spatial image clarity?
    ✔ Correct Answer: It measures the ratio of output image contrast to input target contrast as a function of spatial frequency (line pairs per mm).
    Explanation: MTF quantifies how well an optical sensor transfers contrast from the ground to the image; an MTF value $\ge 0.10$ at the Nyquist frequency is required for acceptable visual clarity.
  • Practice Q6: What satellite currently holds the record for the highest commercial optical spatial resolution in orbit?
    ✔ Correct Answer: WorldView-3 / WorldView-Legion ($0.30\text{ m}$ panchromatic GSD).
    Explanation: Operating at $617\text{ km}$ altitude with a $1.1\text{ m}$ diameter telescope, WorldView-3 resolves individual manhole covers and sidewalk curbs.
  • Practice Q7: Why can linear features (like high-voltage power transmission lines or railway tracks) sometimes be detected in imagery even when their physical width is much smaller than the GSD?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because high radiometric contrast against background terrain alters the digital number of the entire pixel, forming a recognizable linear trend.
    Explanation: While the object cannot be resolved geometrically, extreme contrast allows sub-pixel detection of continuous linear features.
  • Practice Q8: What is the difference between Point Spread Function (PSF) and Instantaneous Field of View (IFOV)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: IFOV is the geometric angle of the detector; PSF describes the optical blur (spatial irradiance distribution) of a point source on the focal plane.
    Explanation: Atmospheric turbulence and lens diffraction spread a point source of light over adjacent detector elements according to the PSF.
