1. Working Principle, Elastic Mechanics & Derivation of the Brazilian Formula

In engineering geology and experimental rock mechanics, the Brazilian Test (also known as the Splitting Tensile Test or Indirect Tensile Test) is the universally standardized laboratory method used to determine the Tensile Strength ($\sigma_t$) of intact rock (Bangar, p. 187; ISRM, 1978; ASTM D3967).

The test was originally conceived by Brazilian engineer Fernando L.L. Carneiro (1943) for concrete and subsequently adapted for rock core specimens. It circumvents the severe experimental difficulties of direct uniaxial pull testing (such as core necking, clamping stress concentrations, and epoxy debonding) by applying a compressive line load across the diameter of a circular disc.

Figure 126.1 — Brazilian Test Configuration and Theoretical Stress Distribution: A circular rock disc of diameter $D$ and thickness $t$ compressed diametrally by load $P$. Biaxial elastic stress analysis demonstrates a remarkably uniform horizontal tensile stress ($\sigma_x = -\sigma_t$) across approximately $75\%$ of the vertical diameter, causing clean vertical splitting along the loading axis.

Elastic Theory of the Diametrally Loaded Disc

According to the classical theory of elasticity (Timoshenko & Goodier; Hondros, 1959), when a circular disc of diameter $D$ and axial thickness $t$ is subjected to diametral concentrated line loads $P$ applied at the top and bottom:

  • Along the vertical loading diameter ($y$-axis), the vertical normal stress $\sigma_y$ is highly compressive: $$\sigma_y = -\frac{2 P}{\pi D t} \left[\frac{D^2}{y(D - y)} - 1\right]$$
  • Simultaneously, the horizontal normal stress $\sigma_x$ across the vertical diameter is tensile and nearly constant over the central portion of the disc: $$\sigma_x = \frac{2 P}{\pi D t} = \text{constant}$$

Because rock is dramatically weaker in tension than in compression ($\sigma_c \approx 10\text{ to } 20 \times \sigma_t$), tensile failure initiates at the center of the disc and propagates outward toward both loaded edges, cleanly cleaving the specimen in half along the vertical diameter.

$$\text{Brazilian Tensile Strength: } \sigma_t = \frac{2 P}{\pi D t} \approx 0.6366 \frac{P}{D t}$$ $$\text{where: } P = \text{Peak compressive load at failure (N)}, \quad D = \text{Disc diameter (mm)}, \quad t = \text{Disc thickness (mm)}$$

2. Standard ISRM & ASTM D3967 Testing Protocols: Dimensions & Jaws

Standard Specimen Dimensions and Geometry

  • Disc Shape: Circular disc diamond-sawn from cylindrical drill core ($NX$ core, diameter $D \approx 54\text{ mm}$, or minimum diameter $\ge 47\text{ mm}$).
  • Thickness-to-Diameter Ratio ($t / D$): The thickness $t$ must be approximately equal to the radius ($t \approx 0.5 D$). Specifically, ASTM D3967 mandates: $$0.2 \le \frac{t}{D} \le 0.75 \quad (\text{ideally } t / D \approx 0.50)$$ If $t / D < 0.2$, the disc behaves as a thin membrane prone to buckling. If $t / D > 0.75$, 3D end-effects and shear wedge crushing dominate before tensile splitting can initiate.
  • Curved Loading Jaws: To prevent premature crushing of mineral grains immediately beneath the sharp line contacts, the ISRM mandates the use of curved steel loading platens with a radius of curvature $1.5 \times$ the disc radius, distributing contact stress over an arc of $10^\circ \text{ to } 15^\circ$. Alternatively, cushioning strips of blotting paper or cardboard ($0.5\text{ mm}$ thick) are inserted.
  • Loading Rate: Applied continuously at a constant rate such that failure occurs within $1\text{ to } 10\text{ minutes}$ (stress rate of $0.05\text{ to } 0.1\text{ MPa/s}$).
3. Direct vs. Indirect Tensile Testing & Practical Engineering Importance

Why the Brazilian Test Superseded Direct Tension Testing

Direct tensile testing of rock requires machining dog-bone specimens or gluing heavy steel end-caps with epoxy resins. It suffers from severe practical failures:

  • Epoxy shear debonding under high humidity or high rock strength.
  • Bending moments induced by slight eccentricities in the tensile pull rods, causing non-uniform premature edge cracking.
  • High machining costs and fragility of weak, fractured, or foliated rock cores.

The Brazilian test provides an economical, rapid, and highly reproducible indirect measurement using standard core slices without complex machining.

Geotechnical and Mining Significance

  • Underground Excavation Roof Stability: Mine roofs span open roadways and delaminate under gravity-induced flexural tensile stresses.
  • Hydraulic Fracturing: In petroleum engineering and geothermal stimulation, borehole fluid pressure must overcome the in-situ minimum principal stress plus the tensile strength of the rock ($\Delta P \ge 3\sigma_3 - \sigma_1 + \sigma_t - P_p$).
  • Explosive Blasting Fragmentation: Shockwaves from detonation reflect from free rock faces as tensile stress waves, spalling the rock because of its extreme tensile weakness.

Question 126
The ‘Brazilian test’ is used to determine :
  • (A) Tensile strength
  • (B) Compressive strength
  • (C) Shear strength
  • (D) Modulus of elasticity
Correct Answer: (A) Tensile strength — The Brazilian test is the universally recognized indirect diametral compression method for determining the tensile strength ($\sigma_t$) of rocks and concrete.
Forensic Analysis of Options
Option (A): Tensile strength — Fully Verified & Correct

The Brazilian test is defined in standard textbooks (Bangar, p. 187; Jaeger & Cook) and international standards (ISRM, 1978; ASTM D3967) as the standard indirect method for evaluating the tensile strength ($\sigma_t$) of intact rock core discs. By applying compressive loads across the diameter, an internal uniform tensile stress is created normal to the diameter ($\sigma_t = \frac{2 P}{\pi D t}$), triggering vertical tensile splitting.

Option (B): Compressive strength — Incorrect Test Method

Compressive strength (UCS) is determined by the Uniaxial Compression Test (ISRM, 1979; ASTM D7012) on cylindrical rock specimens with an aspect ratio of $L/D = 2.0 - 2.5$, where axial load is applied perpendicular to the planar ends. Although the Brazilian test applies a compressive load externally to the disc, its internal failure mechanism and calibrated output measure tensile strength, not compressive strength.

Option (C): Shear strength — Incorrect Test Method

Shear strength is evaluated using Direct Shear Box testing (IS: 13047), Triaxial Compression testing (Hoek cell), or Punch Shear testing. The Brazilian test generates pure tensile splitting along the central axis with zero shear stress.

Option (D): Modulus of elasticity — Incorrect Property

Modulus of elasticity ($E$) is determined from axial and lateral strain gauges (or LVDTs) mounted on cylindrical specimens during uniaxial or triaxial compression testing ($E = \Delta \sigma / \Delta \varepsilon$). It is a deformability modulus, not measured by the Brazilian splitting test.

8 Advanced Practice Problems & Fully Worked Solutions
Problem 1: Standard Brazilian Tensile Strength Calculation
A Brazilian test is performed on an $NX$ core disc of fresh granite with diameter $D = 54.0\text{ mm}$ and thickness $t = 27.0\text{ mm}$. The digital testing machine records a peak diametral splitting load of $P = 21.6\text{ kN}$. Calculate the tensile strength ($\sigma_t$) of the granite in $\text{MPa}$.
Solution:
1. Use the standard Brazilian tensile strength formula: $$\sigma_t = \frac{2 P}{\pi D t}$$ 2. Substitute given values ($P = 21,600\text{ N}, D = 54.0\text{ mm}, t = 27.0\text{ mm}$): $$\sigma_t = \frac{2 \times 21,600}{\pi \times 54.0 \times 27.0} = \frac{43,200}{\pi \times 1458.0} = \frac{43,200}{4580.44} = 9.431\text{ N/mm}^2 = 9.43\text{ MPa}$$ The Brazilian tensile strength of the granite is $9.43\text{ MPa}$.
Problem 2: Ratio of Compressive Strength to Tensile Strength
In a geotechnical laboratory program for a tunnel project, basalt core specimens yield a mean Unconfined Compressive Strength of $\sigma_c = 145.0\text{ MPa}$ and a Brazilian tensile strength of $\sigma_t = 11.6\text{ MPa}$. (a) Calculate the ratio $\sigma_c / \sigma_t$. (b) Assess whether this ratio falls within the classical theoretical and empirical range for brittle crystalline rocks.
Solution:
1. Calculate the ratio: $$\frac{\sigma_c}{\sigma_t} = \frac{145.0\text{ MPa}}{11.6\text{ MPa}} = 12.50$$ 2. Theoretical Assessment: Griffith's classical crack theory predicts an ideal ratio of $\sigma_c / \sigma_t = 8.0$ for open 2D Griffith cracks. In real crystalline rocks with closed microcracks and internal friction, empirical ratios consistently range between $10.0\text{ and } 20.0$. Thus, $12.50$ is a typical, highly representative value.
Problem 3: Required Peak Load for Limestone Core Disc
A limestone disc has diameter $D = 50.0\text{ mm}$ and thickness $t = 25.0\text{ mm}$. The rock is known to have a tensile strength of $\sigma_t = 6.5\text{ MPa}$. What peak diametral compressive load $P$ is expected to cause tensile splitting?
Solution:
1. Rearrange the Brazilian formula for $P$: $$P = \frac{\pi D t \sigma_t}{2}$$ 2. Substitute values: $$P = \frac{\pi \times 50.0\text{ mm} \times 25.0\text{ mm} \times 6.5\text{ N/mm}^2}{2} = \frac{\pi \times 1250 \times 6.5}{2} = \frac{25,525.44}{2} = 12,762.7\text{ N} \approx 12.76\text{ kN}$$
Problem 4: Hydraulic Fracturing Breakdown Pressure Calculation
A borehole at depth $z = 1200\text{ m}$ is subjected to hydraulic fracturing. The in-situ horizontal stresses are $\sigma_h = 24.0\text{ MPa}$ and $\sigma_H = 34.0\text{ MPa}$, and pore-water pressure is $P_p = 12.0\text{ MPa}$. The Brazilian tensile strength of the sandstone formation is $\sigma_t = 5.5\text{ MPa}$. Using the Hubbert-Willis breakdown equation: $$P_b = 3 \sigma_h - \sigma_H + \sigma_t - P_p$$ Calculate the borehole fluid breakdown pressure ($P_b$) required to initiate a vertical hydraulic fracture.
Solution:
1. Substitute given values into the Hubbert-Willis equation: $$P_b = 3(24.0) - 34.0 + 5.5 - 12.0$$ $$P_b = 72.0 - 34.0 + 5.5 - 12.0 = 31.5\text{ MPa}$$ The formation will fracture when fluid injection pressure reaches $31.5\text{ MPa}$.
Problem 5: Anisotropic Tensile Strength in Metamorphic Foliation
A foliated biotite schist core is tested using the Brazilian method in two configurations: (1) Diametral load applied perpendicular to foliation planes (inducing tensile splitting across foliation): measured $\sigma_{t\perp} = 8.4\text{ MPa}$; (2) Diametral load applied parallel to foliation planes (foliation planes aligned with the vertical splitting line): measured $\sigma_{t\parallel} = 2.1\text{ MPa}$. Calculate the Tensile Anisotropy Index ($I_{t} = \sigma_{t\perp} / \sigma_{t\parallel}$) and discuss the structural failure mode.
Solution:
1. Calculate Tensile Anisotropy Index: $$I_{t} = \frac{\sigma_{t\perp}}{\sigma_{t\parallel}} = \frac{8.4\text{ MPa}}{2.1\text{ MPa}} = 4.0$$ 2. Discussion: When foliation is aligned parallel to the tensile splitting axis, the weak van der Waals bonds between parallel aligned biotite sheets split open at only $25\%$ of the force required to fracture across intact quartz-feldspar crystals, demonstrating extreme mechanical directionality.
Problem 6: Stress State at the Center of a Brazilian Disc
For a disc of diameter $D = 54\text{ mm}$ and thickness $t = 27\text{ mm}$ under load $P = 18.0\text{ kN}$, calculate: (a) the principal tensile stress $\sigma_1$ at the center, and (b) the vertical compressive stress $\sigma_3$ at the center (where $y = D/2$).
Solution:
1. Center point coordinates: $x = 0, y = D/2$. 2. Horizontal tensile stress $\sigma_x$: $$\sigma_x = \frac{2 P}{\pi D t} = \frac{2 \times 18,000}{\pi \times 54 \times 27} = \frac{36,000}{4580.44} = +7.86\text{ MPa (Tension)}$$ 3. Vertical compressive stress $\sigma_y$: $$\sigma_y = -\frac{2 P}{\pi D t} \left[\frac{D^2}{(D/2)(D/2)} - 1\right] = -\sigma_x [4 - 1] = -3 \sigma_x$$ $$\sigma_y = -3 \times 7.86\text{ MPa} = -23.58\text{ MPa (Compression)}$$ Key Insight: At the center of the Brazilian disc, the vertical compressive stress is exactly three times the horizontal tensile stress ($\sigma_y = -3 \sigma_x$).
Problem 7: Thickness-to-Diameter Ratio ($t/D$) Validity Check
A student pre-cuts four rock core discs of diameter $D = 50\text{ mm}$ with varying thicknesses: Disc 1 ($t = 8\text{ mm}$), Disc 2 ($t = 15\text{ mm}$), Disc 3 ($t = 25\text{ mm}$), Disc 4 ($t = 45\text{ mm}$). Assess each disc against the ASTM D3967 geometric criteria ($0.2 \le t/D \le 0.75$) and identify which discs are valid.
Solution:
1. Calculate $t / D$ for each disc:
  • Disc 1: $t/D = 8 / 50 = 0.16 < 0.20$ → Invalid (too thin; prone to buckling and premature edge chipping).
  • Disc 2: $t/D = 15 / 50 = 0.30$ → Valid ($0.20 \le 0.30 \le 0.75$).
  • Disc 3: $t/D = 25 / 50 = 0.50$ → Optimal / Valid (ideal $t/D \approx 0.50$ recommended by ISRM).
  • Disc 4: $t/D = 45 / 50 = 0.90 > 0.75$ → Invalid (too thick; 3D end-effects and shear crushing dominate).
Problem 8: Griffith Failure Criterion with Brazilian and UCS Data
According to Griffith's classical failure criterion for brittle materials, when $\sigma_1 + 3\sigma_3 > 0$, failure occurs when: $$(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)^2 = 8 \sigma_t (\sigma_1 + \sigma_3)$$ If a rock has a Brazilian tensile strength $\sigma_t = 8.0\text{ MPa}$, predict its theoretical Uniaxial Compressive Strength ($\sigma_c$) where $\sigma_1 = \sigma_c$ and $\sigma_3 = 0$.
Solution:
1. Substitute $\sigma_1 = \sigma_c$ and $\sigma_3 = 0$ into Griffith's criterion: $$(\sigma_c - 0)^2 = 8 \sigma_t (\sigma_c + 0)$$ $$\sigma_c^2 = 8 \sigma_t \sigma_c$$ 2. Divide both sides by $\sigma_c$ ($\sigma_c \ne 0$): $$\sigma_c = 8 \sigma_t$$ 3. Calculate $\sigma_c$: $$\sigma_c = 8 \times 8.0\text{ MPa} = 64.0\text{ MPa}$$ Geological Note: Griffith's open-crack theory demonstrates why intact rock compressive strength is naturally an order of magnitude higher than tensile strength.
