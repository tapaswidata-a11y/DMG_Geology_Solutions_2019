1. Physics of Electromagnetic Scattering in the Atmosphere

Before solar electromagnetic radiation reaches the Earth's surface or a satellite sensor, it interacts with gaseous molecules and suspended aerosols in the atmosphere. This interaction consists of absorption (conversion of radiant energy into thermal/kinetic energy) and scattering (unpredictable redirection of electromagnetic energy by particles). Atmospheric scattering is strictly governed by the ratio of particle diameter ($d$) to radiation wavelength ($\lambda$):

  • Rayleigh Scattering ($d \ll \lambda$): Molecular scattering caused by atmospheric gas molecules ($\text{N}_2, \text{O}_2$) whose effective diameter is much smaller than the wavelength of incident radiation ($d < 0.1\lambda$).
  • Mie Scattering ($d \approx \lambda$): Aerosol scattering caused by smoke, dust, pollen, and water vapor whose diameter approximately equals the radiation wavelength ($0.1\lambda \le d \le 10\lambda$).
  • Non-Selective Scattering ($d \gg \lambda$): Caused by large water droplets, cloud droplets, and ice crystals ($d > 10\lambda$), scattering all visible wavelengths equally and producing white clouds/fog.
Rayleigh's Inverse Fourth-Power Scattering Law (Lord Rayleigh, 1871)
$$I_{\text{scattered}} \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4} \quad \text{or} \quad \sigma_R = \frac{8\pi^3 (n^2 - 1)^2}{3 N^2 \lambda^4}$$

Where $I$ is scattered radiance, $\lambda$ is wavelength, $n$ is refractive index, and $N$ is molecular density. Scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of wavelength.

Figure 11.1: Rayleigh Scattering Wavelength Dependence ($\lambda^{-4}$). The steep non-linear surge in scattering efficiency in the ultraviolet and blue spectrum ($0.40 - 0.45\ \mu\text{m}$) compared to the red and near-infrared spectrum ($0.70 - 1.0\ \mu\text{m}$).
2. The Optical Mechanism: Why the Sky is Blue & Sunsets are Red

Applying Rayleigh's Law to the visible solar spectrum explains both daytime blue sky and sunset redness:

  • Blue Sky at Midday: Blue light has a wavelength of $\approx 0.40\ \mu\text{m}$, while red light has a wavelength of $\approx 0.70\ \mu\text{m}$. Comparing their scattering ratios: $$\frac{I_{\text{blue}}}{I_{\text{red}}} = \left(\frac{\lambda_{\text{red}}}{\lambda_{\text{blue}}}\right)^4 \approx \left(\frac{0.70}{0.40}\right)^4 = (1.75)^4 \approx 9.38$$ Blue photons are scattered by nitrogen and oxygen molecules roughly 9 to 10 times more efficiently than red photons. When an observer looks in any direction away from the direct solar disk, scattered blue photons enter the eye from all angles across the celestial dome.
  • Red Sunsets & Sunrises: At sunrise and sunset, solar rays travel through an atmospheric optical path length (air mass) up to 12 times thicker than at midday. Virtually all short blue and green wavelengths are scattered out of the direct line of sight, leaving only unscattered longer red and orange wavelengths to reach the observer.
"Rayleigh scattering is the primary cause of the blue color of the clear sky. Because scattering intensity varies inversely with the fourth power of wavelength, blue light ($\approx 0.4\ \mu\text{m}$) is scattered almost 10 times more intensely than red light ($\approx 0.7\ \mu\text{m}$) by atmospheric gas molecules." — Lillesand, Kiefer & Chipman, Remote Sensing and Image Interpretation (7th Ed., 2015, p. 10)
3. Impact on Optical Remote Sensing: Atmospheric Path Radiance ($L_p$)

In satellite remote sensing, Rayleigh scattering introduces unwanted noise known as atmospheric path radiance ($L_p$) or "haze":

  • Contrast Reduction in Blue Band: Solar radiation scattered into the sensor's field of view without ever reaching the ground adds an additive offset ($L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{surface}}\tau + L_p$), severely degrading image contrast in Landsat Band 1 (blue).
  • Haze Reduction Filters: In aerial photography and multispectral imaging, minus-blue filters (yellow filters, cut-off $< 0.50\ \mu\text{m}$) are deployed to eliminate Rayleigh haze.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 11)
Q11. What is responsible for the blue colour of the sky ?
(A) Mie Scattering
(B) Non-Selective Scattering
(C) Rayleigh Scattering  ✔
(D) Water droplets present in the sky
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Rayleigh Scattering
The blue color of the clear sky is caused by Rayleigh scattering of sunlight by nitrogen and oxygen gas molecules whose diameters are much smaller than visible wavelengths ($d \ll \lambda$). Scattering intensity scales inversely with $\lambda^4$, dispersing blue light ($\sim 0.4\ \mu\text{m}$) nearly 10 times more intensely than red light.
(A) Mie Scattering Incorrect (Aerosol Scattering Generating White/Grey Haze)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Mie scattering occurs when atmospheric particles (smoke, dust, pollen) have diameters roughly equal to incident wavelengths ($d \approx \lambda$). Its wavelength dependence is much weaker ($\propto \lambda^{-1}$ to $\lambda^0$), scattering all colors across the visible spectrum and causing polluted or humid skies to appear milky-white or hazy rather than vivid blue.
(B) Non-Selective Scattering Incorrect (Equal Wavelength Scattering Generating Clouds/Fog)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Non-selective scattering is caused by particles much larger than visible light ($d > 10\lambda$, such as cloud water droplets $> 10\ \mu\text{m}$). It scatters all visible wavelengths with identical efficiency, which is why clouds and dense fog appear brilliantly white.
(C) Rayleigh Scattering Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: First mathematically demonstrated by Lord Rayleigh in 1871, molecular scattering by atmospheric gases varies as $\lambda^{-4}$. Because blue photons have the shortest wavelengths in the dominant solar emission band, they undergo intense diffuse lateral scattering throughout the upper atmosphere, flooding the daytime sky with blue radiance.
(D) Water droplets present in the sky Incorrect (Liquid Droplets Cause Clouds and Rainbows, Not Blue Sky)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Liquid water droplets in the sky cause clouds, fog, and rain (via non-selective scattering) or rainbows (via refraction and internal reflection). A completely dry, clean atmosphere of pure nitrogen and oxygen produces the deepest, most saturated blue sky.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical Flux): Using Rayleigh's $\lambda^{-4}$ scattering law, compare the scattering intensity of violet light ($\lambda_1 = 0.40\ \mu\text{m}$) to near-infrared radiation ($\lambda_2 = 0.80\ \mu\text{m}$).
    ✔ Worked Answer: Violet light is scattered $16\text{ times}$ more intensely than NIR radiation ($2^4 = 16$).
    Derivation:
    $$\frac{I_1}{I_2} = \left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^4 = \left(\frac{0.80}{0.40}\right)^4 = 2^4 = 16$$ This shows why infrared imagery penetrates atmospheric haze with exceptional clarity compared to visible photography.
  • Practice Q2: What optical filter is universally attached to aerial cameras to penetrate atmospheric Rayleigh haze?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A "minus-blue" (haze) filter (e.g., Wratten 12 yellow filter) that absorbs wavelengths below $0.50\ \mu\text{m}$.
    Explanation: Because Rayleigh scattering dominates in the violet and blue wavelengths ($< 0.50\ \mu\text{m}$), blocking these wavelengths eliminates scattered airlight, maximizing surface ground contrast.
  • Practice Q3: Why is the sky on the Moon or in deep space completely black even in broad daylight?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Moon lacks an atmosphere; in the absence of gaseous molecules, zero Rayleigh scattering occurs.
    Explanation: Without gas molecules to scatter sunlight into the shadows, solar rays travel in straight vacuum paths; shadows appear pitch-black and the daytime sky remains star-filled black.
  • Practice Q4: In dark object subtraction (DOS) atmospheric correction of satellite imagery, how is path radiance ($L_p$) estimated?
    ✔ Correct Answer: By extracting the minimum digital number (DN) recorded over completely dark targets (deep clear water or shadowed terrain).
    Explanation: Deep clear water has virtually zero natural reflectance in the NIR and visible; any positive signal recorded by the satellite is assumed to be additive atmospheric path radiance ($L_p$) caused by Rayleigh and Mie scattering.
  • Practice Q5: Why do large volcanic eruptions (such as Mt. Pinatubo in 1991) cause brilliant, persistent purple sunsets worldwide?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Injection of sub-micron sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere enhances forward Mie and Rayleigh scattering of red and blue light simultaneously.
    Explanation: Stratospheric aerosol droplets scatter unattenuated blue light from high altitudes while transmitted red light passes through the troposphere, combining visually to create brilliant purple and lavender twilights.
  • Practice Q6: What is the phase function characteristic of pure Rayleigh scattering?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Symmetrical forward and backward scattering ($P(\theta) \propto 1 + \cos^2\theta$).
    Explanation: Unlike Mie scattering, which is heavily concentrated in the forward direction, Rayleigh scattering disperses radiation equally in both forward and backward directions with half the intensity at right angles ($90^\circ$).
  • Practice Q7: Why does Rayleigh scattering polarize scattered sky light at $90^\circ$ relative to the incident sun rays?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Dipole oscillations of gas molecules can only oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation, producing $100\%$ linear polarization at right angles.
    Explanation: This polarization enables polarizing filters to darken blue skies in photography and allows desert insects (e.g., honeybees) to navigate by celestial polarization patterns.
  • Practice Q8: Why does Non-Selective scattering affect radar wavelengths ($> 1\text{ cm}$) differently than optical waves?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Cloud droplets ($10\ \mu\text{m}$) are much smaller than radar wavelengths, meaning clouds behave as Rayleigh scatterers for radar, allowing microwaves to penetrate clouds without attenuation.
    Explanation: For optical waves ($0.5\ \mu\text{m}$), cloud droplets ($10\ \mu\text{m}$) are massive ($d \gg \lambda$, non-selective). But for C-band radar ($\lambda = 5.6\text{ cm}$), cloud droplets are tiny ($d \ll \lambda$), rendering clouds transparent to radar sensors.
