Q25: Fold Classification: Parallel (Concentric) Folds & Curvature Evolution with Depth

Exam: Geology Paper-II (2021)  |  Authoritative Reference: John G. Ramsay (Folding & Fracturing of Rocks, 1967); Ramsay & Huber (Vol. 2, pp. 308-320); Fossen (Ch. 10, pp. 225-230)

Part I: Theoretical Foundation & Concept Masterclass

25.1 Ramsay's Isogon Classification of Folds

In structural geology (John G. Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks; Ramsay & Huber, The Techniques of Modern Structural Geology, Vol. 2, pp. 308–320; Fossen, Ch. 10, pp. 225–230), folds are classified based on the behavior of dip isogons and variations in orthogonal layer thickness ($t_\alpha$):

Ramsay Fold Class Layer Thickness Condition Geometric Evolution with Depth & Structural Signatures
Class 1B: Parallel Fold (Concentric) Orthogonal thickness is constant throughout: $t_\alpha = t_0 = \text{const}$ Anticlines become sharper and more cuspidate with depth; synclines become broader and flatter with depth. Terminates downward in core space problems, decollements, and cuspate detachment faults.
Class 2: Similar Fold Axial-planar thickness is constant: $T_\alpha = T_0$; orthogonal thickness thins on limbs ($t_\alpha = t_0 \cos \alpha$) Fold profile remains identical and invariant with depth; no change in curvature or sharpness; no space problem in the core.
Class 1A Limbs thicker than hinge ($t_\alpha > t_0$) Convergent dip isogons, rare in nature.
Class 1C Limbs thinner than Class 1B but thicker than Class 2 Weakly modified buckling fold.
Class 3 Extreme limb thinning ($t_\alpha < t_0 \cos \alpha$) Divergent dip isogons, typical of incompetent layers between competent competent Class 1B beds.

25.2 Mathematical Proof of Curvature Evolution in Concentric Folds

In concentric circular folding of a multilayer with constant orthogonal bed thickness $t$:

For an anticline with outer radius of curvature $R_1$, the radius of curvature of the inner underlying layer is:

$$R_2 = R_1 - t$$

As depth increases, $R$ decreases towards zero ($R \to 0$). Because curvature is the inverse of radius ($\kappa = 1/R$), the curvature $\kappa \to \infty$, forcing the anticlinal core to become infinitely sharp, pinched, and cuspidate until space runs out at depth $z = R_1$.

Conversely, for the adjacent syncline, the center of curvature lies above ground, so going downward into depth increases the radius ($R_{\text{depth}} = R_{\text{surface}} + t$), causing curvature $\kappa \to 0$, making the syncline progressively broader, open, and flatter with depth!

Part II: Official Question & Critical Option Dissection

Q25. A fold in which the anticlines become sharper and synclines become broader with depth is known as :
(A) Parallel fold
(B) Similar fold
(C) Recumbent fold
(D) Box fold
VERIFIED ANSWER: OPTION (A)

Correct Answer: (A) Parallel fold — A fold in which constant layer thickness causes anticlines to become sharper and synclines to become broader with depth is strictly defined as a Parallel (or Concentric) fold (Ramsay Class 1B).

Option-by-Option Critical Dissection

  • (A) Parallel fold — CORRECT: Constant orthogonal thickness preserves layer geometry, mathematically forcing anticlinal cores to sharpen and synclinal cores to broaden with depth.
  • (B) Similar fold — INCORRECT: In similar folds (Class 2), the fold shape and sharpness are strictly invariant and identical at all depths.
  • (C) Recumbent fold — INCORRECT: Recumbent describes a spatial orientation where the axial plane is sub-horizontal (dip $< 10^\circ$), regardless of curvature evolution with depth.
  • (D) Box fold — INCORRECT: A box fold (conjugate fold) has two distinct axial planes and a broad, flat crest.

Structural Core Space Problem Practice

Practice Problem 25: The Space Problem at the Core of Parallel Folds

What structural features commonly develop in sedimentary basins to accommodate the severe "space problem" in the sharp core of a parallel anticline?

Answer:

Because concentric beds cannot penetrate into a negative radius of curvature, the core experiences extreme compressive jamming, accommodated by:
1. Detachment / Decollement thrusting: The competent folded stack detaches from the underlying flat basement.
2. Out-of-the-core thrust faults: Reverse faults propagate upwards out of the tight hinge.
3. Core flowage of incompetent material: Flow of ductile salt, shale, or gypsum into the anticlinal core (forming salt or shale diapirs).
