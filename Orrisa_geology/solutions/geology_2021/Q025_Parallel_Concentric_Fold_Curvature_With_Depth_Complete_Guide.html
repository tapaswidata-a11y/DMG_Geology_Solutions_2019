1. Ramsay's Dip Isogon Classification & Parallel (Concentric) Folds

In 1967, John G. Ramsay revolutionized structural geology by establishing a rigorous mathematical classification of folds based on dip isogons (lines connecting points of equal dip on adjacent surfaces of a folded layer) and the variation of orthogonal layer thickness ($t_\alpha$) relative to limb dip ($\alpha$):

  • Class 1: Dip isogons converge toward the fold core. Orthogonal thickness $t_\alpha$ is either greater on limbs than at the hinge (Class 1A), constant everywhere (Class 1B), or thinner on limbs (Class 1C).
  • Class 1B — The Parallel (Concentric) Fold: A fold where the orthogonal layer thickness remains strictly constant throughout the fold: $$t_\alpha = t_0 = \text{constant}$$ Where $t_0$ is orthogonal thickness at the hinge. In profile, individual bedding surfaces form concentric circular arcs sharing a common center of curvature.
  • Class 2 — Similar Folds: Dip isogons are strictly parallel. Axial planar thickness $T_\alpha = T_0 = \text{constant}$; orthogonal thickness thins on limbs ($t_\alpha = t_0 \cos\alpha$). Fold shape is identical on all layers with depth.
  • Class 3: Dip isogons diverge toward the core; extreme thinning on limbs.
Figure 25.1 (Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, Fig. 12.10): Ramsay's dip isogon classification. Class 1B defines Parallel (Concentric) folds where orthogonal layer thickness ($t$) is invariant across the entire profile.
2. The Space Problem: Why Anticlines Sharpen & Synclines Broaden Downward

Because concentric folded beds maintain constant orthogonal thickness ($t_0$) like layers of cardboard or thick plywood bent together, circular arc geometry imposes a severe geometric constraint with depth:

  • Radius of Curvature with Depth ($R$): For an anticline, the center of curvature lies below the fold. As one moves downward into the core of the anticline, the radius of curvature decreases by the layer thickness on each successive layer ($R_{n} = R_0 - n \cdot t_0$).
  • Anticlines Sharpen Downward: Because radius of curvature $R \to 0$ downward, the anticline becomes progressively tighter, narrower, and sharper at depth until the layers can no longer fit into the core (the cusp singularity).
  • Synclines Broaden Downward: Conversely, for a syncline, the center of curvature lies above the ground surface. Moving downward away from the center of curvature increases the radius ($R_{n} = R_0 + n \cdot t_0$), causing the syncline to become progressively broader and more open with depth.
Concentric Fold Curvature Relationship with Depth
$$\kappa_{\text{anticline}}(z) = \frac{1}{R_0 - z} \to \infty \quad \text{(Curvature sharpens as depth } z \to R_0\text{)}$$ $$\kappa_{\text{syncline}}(z) = \frac{1}{R_0 + z} \to 0 \quad \text{(Curvature broadens/flattens downward)}$$
"Parallel or Concentric Folds: These are folds in which the thickness of the beds remains uniform throughout. As a consequence of uniform thickness, the anticlines become sharper (tighter) downward while the synclines become broader with depth. Ultimately, a depth is reached where the anticline cannot continue downward without fracturing or crumpling. This is known as the space problem in parallel folds." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Folds, pp. 193–194, Fig. 7.11)
Figure 25.2 (K.M. Bangar, Fig. 7.11): Cross-section of Parallel (Concentric) folds illustrating the geometric consequence of constant thickness: anticlines sharpen and pinch out downward while synclines broaden downward, generating the core space problem.
3. Refutation of Similar, Supratenuous, and Chevron Folds

Candidates must clearly discriminate alternative fold morphologies:

  • (B) Similar fold: Layers have variable orthogonal thickness (thick at hinges, thin on limbs) such that curvature is identical on all layers with depth. Anticlines and synclines never sharpen or broaden downward in a similar fold.
  • (C) Supratenuous fold: Non-tectonic compaction folds formed over buried hills or reefs, where beds are thinner over the anticlinal crest and thicker in adjacent basins due to differential sedimentary compaction.
  • (D) Chevron fold: Folds characterized by straight planar limbs and sharp, angular, knife-edge hinges (accordion folds).

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 25)
Q25. A fold in which the anticlines become sharper and synclines become broader with depth is known as :
(A) Parallel fold  ✔
(B) Similar fold
(C) Supratenuous fold
(D) Chevron fold
✔
Correct Answer: (A) Parallel fold
A Parallel fold (Class 1B Concentric Fold) maintains constant orthogonal layer thickness ($t_\alpha = t_0$) throughout. As an inescapable geometric consequence of concentric arc curvature, anticlines become sharper and synclines become broader with depth (Bangar, p. 193; Ramsay, 1967).
(A) Parallel fold Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: In a parallel (concentric) fold, every bed maintains identical orthogonal thickness everywhere. Because concentric circular arcs have a common center of curvature, the radius of curvature of anticlinal beds decreases downward toward the center of curvature, forcing anticlines to become progressively tighter and sharper at depth. Conversely, synclines move away from their center of curvature and broaden downward.
(B) Similar fold Incorrect (Curvature Invariant with Depth)
Why this distractor is incorrect: In a similar fold (Ramsay Class 2), beds thin on the limbs ($t_\alpha = t_0 \cos\alpha$) and thicken at the hinge, maintaining an identical cross-sectional shape and identical curvature on every layer with depth. Anticlines and synclines in similar folds never change curvature with depth.
(C) Supratenuous fold Incorrect (Sedimentary Differential Compaction Fold)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A supratenuous fold is formed by syn-depositional differential compaction over an unyielding buried topographic ridge (such as a granite tor or fossil reef). Beds thin over the anticlinal crest and thicken into flanking troughs; it does not display concentric curvature deepening.
(D) Chevron fold Incorrect (Straight-Limbed Angular Folds)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Chevron folds (accordion folds) possess straight, planar limbs meeting at narrow, acute, angular hinges. While they may belong to Class 1B, their defining criterion is angularity, not the systematic downward broadening of synclines.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: How do rocks in the core of a parallel anticline resolve the "space problem" when the radius of curvature approaches zero ($R \to 0$)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Through ductile flow of incompetent layers, disharmonic folding, decollement detachment, or core thrust faulting (out-of-the-syncline thrusts).
    Explanation: Because rigid beds cannot occupy zero volume, the concentric fold cannot continue downward indefinitely; a basal detachment (decollement) separates the folded competent layers from undeformed basement below.
  • Practice Q2: What primary deformation mechanism produces Class 1B parallel folds in competent sedimentary sequences?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Flexural Slip (slip along bedding planes like bending a deck of cards).
    Explanation: In flexural slip folding, individual layers slide past one another along pre-existing weak bedding planes without altering internal orthogonal thickness ($t_\alpha = \text{const}$).
  • Practice Q3: Where does the amount of flexural slip between adjacent beds reach a maximum in a parallel fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: At the inflection points on the limbs (where layer dip is steepest). Slip is zero at the hinge line.
    Explanation: Relative displacement between beds ($S = t \cdot \alpha$) is proportional to limb dip ($\alpha$). At the hinge ($\alpha = 0$), shear slip is zero.
  • Practice Q4: In a Similar Fold (Class 2), what remains constant across the entire profile of a folded layer?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Thickness measured parallel to the axial plane ($T_\alpha = \text{constant}$).
    Explanation: Similar folds form by heterogeneous simple shear or passive flow parallel to the axial plane, resulting in uniform axial planar thickness everywhere.
  • Practice Q5: What metamorphic and structural environment favors the development of Similar Folds (Class 2) over Parallel Folds (Class 1B)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: High-grade regional metamorphism and ductile shearing in the middle to lower crust (incompetent rocks under high temperature).
    Explanation: High temperature lowers rock viscosity, promoting passive ductile flow and axial planar cleavage rather than bedding-plane slip.
  • Practice Q6: What is a disharmonic fold, and why is it intimately associated with parallel folds?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fold where soft, incompetent layers (shale, halite) deform into complex, irregular wrinkles between uniformly curved competent layers.
    Explanation: Incompetent beds squeeze out of high-pressure anticlinal hinges and flow into low-pressure voids to resolve the space problem.
  • Practice Q7 (Numerical): A competent limestone bed of thickness $t_0 = 10\text{ m}$ is folded into a concentric anticline whose outer surface has a radius of curvature $R_{\text{outer}} = 50\text{ m}$. After how many successive $10\text{ m}$ beds will the anticlinal core encounter the geometric cusp singularity ($R = 0$)?
    ✔ Worked Answer: Exactly 5 beds ($50\text{ m} / 10\text{ m} = 5$).
    Calculation:
    $$R_n = R_{\text{outer}} - n \cdot t_0 = 50 - n(10) = 0 \implies n = 5$$ Below the 5th layer, concentric folding is mathematically impossible without core disruption.
  • Practice Q8: What is a ptygmatic fold, and what Ramsay class does it belong to?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A tortuously contorted, pucker-folded granitic or quartzose vein enclosed in a ductile metamorphic schist/gneiss; Class 1B to Class 1C.
    Explanation: Buckling of a high-viscosity competent vein embedded in a low-viscosity incompetent host matrix produces ptygmatic meanders.
