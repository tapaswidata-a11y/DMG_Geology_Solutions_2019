PART I
Theoretical Foundation & Scientific Principles

1. The Aeolian Dynamic System Triad (Kocurek & Lancaster, 1999)

Modern process sedimentology and aeolian geomorphology abandon simplistic climatic assumptions that sand seas (ergs) require "hot desert" climates. Instead, comprehensive physical models (Kocurek & Lancaster 1999; Pye & Tsoar 2009; Boggs 2011) demonstrate that all aeolian accumulation, dune construction, and sand transport are strictly governed by a Dynamic Triad of Interacting Parameters:

  • (i) Transport Capacity ($C$): The potential maximum kinetic mass of sediment that wind can move per unit time through a specified cross-section. Transport capacity is a direct physical function of aerodynamic shear velocity ($u_*$) and wind power, governed by Bagnold's (1941) cubic sand-flux equation.
  • (ii) Sediment Supply ($G$): The total volume, bulk inventory, and grain-size distribution of sand-sized material ($0.063 - 2.0\text{ mm}$) provided to the basin by primary external source engines—such as braided glacial outwash plains, dry ephemeral wadis, paleolacustrine strandlines, or deeply weathered crystalline granites.
  • (iv) Sediment Availability ($A$): The fraction or proportion of existing sediment supply that is physically, aerodynamically, and mechanically free to be entrained by the wind. Availability is restricted by extrinsic moisture content, evaporite salt crusts, biogenic soil crusts, vegetation cover, groundwater table capillary fringe, and coarse gravel lag armoring.

2. Bagnold's Aerodynamic Physics: Fluid vs. Impact Thresholds

Brigadier Ralph Alger Bagnold (1941) laid the empirical and theoretical foundations for particle transport by wind in The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes. The boundary shear stress ($\tau_0 = \rho_a u_*^2$) exerted by wind on the ground drives three distinct transport modes:

  • Saltation (70–80% of total load): Sand grains ($0.1 - 0.5\text{ mm}$) leap into the air in asymmetric trajectories, rising steeply at $50^\circ$ and descending at shallow angles ($10^\circ - 15^\circ$), imparting kinetic energy upon impact.
  • Surface Creep / Reptation (20–25% of total load): Coarser sand grains and granules ($0.5 - 2.0\text{ mm}$) pushed, rolled, and nudged forward by the ballistic impacts of returning saltating grains.
  • Suspension (< 5% of total load): Fine silts and dust ($< 0.063\text{ mm}$) lifted high into the planetary boundary layer by turbulent eddies, traveling hundreds to thousands of kilometers as dust storms (loess deposits).
Bagnold's Fluid Threshold Friction Velocity ($u_{*t}$)
$$u_{*t} = A \sqrt{\frac{\rho_s - \rho_a}{\rho_a} g d}$$

Where $A \approx 0.1$ for fluid threshold, $\rho_s$ is quartz density ($2650\text{ kg/m}^3$), $\rho_a$ is air density ($1.22\text{ kg/m}^3$), $g$ is gravity, and $d$ is grain diameter. For $0.25\text{ mm}$ sand, minimum entrainment threshold occurs around $u_* \approx 0.2\text{ m/s}$.

Figure 3.2 (Sam Boggs Jr., 5th Ed., Fig. 2.4): Mechanics of sediment transport by fluid flow. Illustrates the tripartite division of bedload into traction (rolling/creep), saltation, and suspended load.
3. Textbooks on Wind Action & Scientific Refutation of "Hot Deserts"

Why is condition (iii) Hot desertic conditions categorically excluded from the primary physical controls? Because temperature is not an aerodynamic parameter. Aeolian processes are exceptionally active in hyper-arid cold polar and sub-polar deserts:

  • Periglacial & Polar Ergs: Massive active dune fields occur in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica ($-30^\circ\text{C}$ to $-50^\circ\text{C}$), the Kobuk Valley of sub-arctic Alaska, and the periglacial outwash sandurs of Iceland and Greenland.
  • Extraterrestrial Aeolian Systems: Gigantic active dune systems cover extensive regions of Mars (where mean surface temperatures are $-60^\circ\text{C}$) and Saturn's moon Titan ($-179^\circ\text{C}$ with methane/ethane winds).
"Wind is an active agent of erosion, transportation and deposition only in arid and semi-arid regions where rainfall is scanty, vegetation is scarce or absent, and loose dry materials are easily picked up... Temperature extremes assist mechanical disintegration, but the movement of material is purely a mechanical operation of wind velocity and sediment supply." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 2: Geological Work of Wind, pp. 28–29)

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 3)
Q3. Aeolian activity is primarily controlled by: (i) Transport capacity, (ii) Sediment supply, (iii) Hot desertic conditions, (iv) Sediment availability. Choose the correct set of answer from the following options :
(A) (i), (ii), (iii)
(B) (i), (ii), (iv)  ✔
(C) (i), (iii), (iv)
(D) (i), (ii), (iii), (iv)
✔
Correct Answer: (B) (i), (ii), (iv)
In modern aeolian process sedimentology (Kocurek & Lancaster 1999; Boggs 2011), the primary controls on aeolian system dynamics form a fundamental triad: (i) Transport capacity, (ii) Sediment supply, and (iv) Sediment availability. "Hot desertic conditions" is a classic distractor because aeolian transport operates identically in frigid polar, sub-arctic, and extraterrestrial environments.
(A) (i), (ii), (iii) Incorrect (Omits Availability & Includes Climatological Distractor)
Why this option is incorrect: This option omits Sediment availability—a disastrous oversight because an abundant sediment supply cannot be transported if it is locked beneath a high water table, salt crust, or lag armor. Furthermore, it erroneously includes "hot desertic conditions", which is scientifically invalid.
(B) (i), (ii), (iv) Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Statements (i), (ii), and (iv) comprise the universally acknowledged Aeolian Dynamic Triad established by Kocurek & Lancaster (1999). Transport capacity dictates the kinetic wind energy; sediment supply dictates the total reservoir of sand produced by weathering/fluvial systems; and sediment availability dictates how much of that reservoir can actually be lifted into the air stream without being anchored by moisture or surface crusts.
(C) (i), (iii), (iv) Incorrect (Omits Fundamental Sediment Supply)
Why this option is incorrect: This option fails to include Sediment supply. Without a primary source supplying sand-sized siliciclastic grains into a sedimentary basin, strong winds can only generate bare bedrock deflation surfaces (hamadas) or gravel lag pavements, completely preventing dune formation.
(D) (i), (ii), (iii), (iv) Incorrect (Popular Superficially All-Inclusive Trap)
Why this option is incorrect: This is the universal "all-of-the-above" trap designed to lure candidates who conflate casual layperson associations (linking sand dunes strictly to hot Sahara-like deserts) with rigorous physics. Because high-latitude cold dunes (e.g., Great Kobuk, Alaska; Lake Athabasca, Canada; Victoria Valley, Antarctica) and Martian ergs experience intense aeolian activity under freezing conditions, thermal heat is definitively not a primary control.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical Flux): According to Bagnold's sand flux formulation, mass transport rate per unit width of channel ($q$) varies with the cube of shear velocity ($q = C \cdot \frac{\rho_a}{g} u_*^3$). If a desert gale increases the aerodynamic friction velocity ($u_*$) from $0.30\text{ m/s}$ to $0.60\text{ m/s}$ (a 2-fold increase), by what factor does the sand transport rate increase?
    ✔ Worked Answer: Increases by a factor of $8$ ($2^3 = 8$, an $800\%$ increase).
    Derivation:
    $$\frac{q_2}{q_1} = \left(\frac{u_{*2}}{u_{*1}}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{0.60}{0.30}\right)^3 = 2^3 = 8$$ This cubic dependency demonstrates why high-magnitude, low-frequency storm events perform the vast majority of geological work in aeolian environments.
  • Practice Q2: What is the difference between Bagnold's fluid threshold and impact threshold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Fluid threshold is the wind velocity required to initiate grain motion purely by fluid drag; impact threshold is the lower velocity required to sustain movement once saltating grains begin impacting the bed.
    Explanation: Because the kinetic energy transferred by returning saltating grains dislodges stationary bed particles mechanically, the impact threshold friction velocity ($u_{*ti}$) is approximately $80\%$ of the initial fluid threshold velocity ($u_{*t}$).
  • Practice Q3: Why does the threshold friction velocity curve for entrainment of dry sediment display a minimum around grain sizes of $0.08 - 0.10\text{ mm}$ (fine sand), increasing for both coarser gravel AND ultra-fine clays/silts?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Coarse grains require more wind power due to gravitational mass; ultra-fine clay/silt particles are held by strong inter-particle electrostatic (van der Waals) cohesion and hide within the laminar sublayer.
    Explanation: Particles smaller than $0.05\text{ mm}$ do not protrude into the turbulent boundary layer and are bound tightly by cohesive surface forces, making pure clay beds extremely resistant to wind erosion unless impacted by saltating sand grains.
  • Practice Q4: In an aeolian system, how does a rising regional groundwater table influence sand accumulation?
    ✔ Correct Answer: It acts as an absolute baseline of deflation (Stokes surface) by drastically reducing sediment availability through capillary moisture.
    Explanation: Wet sand cannot be entrained because surface tension of capillary water creates meniscus bonding forces hundreds of times greater than aerodynamic lift. The water table thus acts as an accumulation baseline, preserving underlying aeolian strata in the rock record.
  • Practice Q5: Desert pavement (known as reg in the western Sahara or serir in the eastern Sahara) is primarily formed by which physical process?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Deflation (removal of fine sand and dust) leaving behind a concentrated armor of interlocking coarse gravel and pebbles, augmented by dust infiltration beneath the stones.
    Explanation: Winnowing of sand-sized grains exposes coarse clasts. Modern research shows that once formed, the pebble mosaic traps windblown dust, which washes beneath the stones, progressively lifting the pavement upward on a layer of fine vesicular silt.
  • Practice Q6: What dune morphology develops under a strictly unidirectional wind regime in an area with a limited sediment supply moving across a hard, non-erodible substrate?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Barchan dunes (crescentic dunes with horns pointing downwind).
    Explanation: Barchans are classic indicators of supply-limited conditions under a persistent one-directional wind. If sediment supply increases dramatically, individual barchans coalesce laterally into transverse dune ridges.
  • Practice Q7: Star dunes (radially symmetrical pyramidal sand mounds with multiple slip faces) require which specific meteorological wind regime?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A complex, multi-directional or multidirectional seasonal wind regime with high wind variability.
    Explanation: Because winds blow from three or more distinct directions throughout the year, sand is not transported across large regional distances; instead, it accumulates vertically in place, creating the highest dunes on Earth (up to $300 - 500\text{ m}$ tall in the Namib and Badain Jaran deserts).
  • Practice Q8: What is the aerodynamic roughness length ($z_0$) over a flat desert sand sheet, and how does it relate to the logarithmic wind profile equation $u_z = \frac{u_*}{\kappa} \ln\left(\frac{z}{z_0}\right)$?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $z_0 \approx d / 30$ (approximately $1/30$th of the mean grain diameter $d$), representing the theoretical height above the bed where velocity extrapolates to zero.
    Explanation: In von Kármán's logarithmic law of the wall, $\kappa \approx 0.40$ is the von Kármán constant. When saltation begins, moving grains extract momentum from the air, causing the effective aerodynamic roughness length ($z_0'$) to increase significantly to several grain diameters.
