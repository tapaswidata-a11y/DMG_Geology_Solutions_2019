1. Andersonian Fault Mechanics & Theoretical Dip Angle

In structural geology and geodynamics, thrust faults and reverse faults are contractional dip-slip fault systems in which the hanging wall moves upward relative to the footwall, accommodating horizontal crustal shortening ($\Delta L < 0$) and vertical thickening. The orientation and dip angle ($\theta$) of these faults are governed by E.M. Anderson's (1905, 1951) dynamic theory of faulting and the Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion:

  • Principal Stress Orientation: In a contractional tectonic regime (such as collisional orogenic belts like the Himalayas or Appalachian foreland), the maximum compressive principal stress ($\sigma_1$) is horizontal, intermediate stress ($\sigma_2$) is horizontal and parallel to fault strike, and the minimum principal stress ($\sigma_3$) is vertical (overburden weight).
  • Theoretical Andersonian Failure Angle: According to Coulomb fracture mechanics, shear rupture planes form at an angle $\theta$ relative to $\sigma_1$: $$\theta = 45^\circ - \frac{\phi}{2}$$ Where $\phi$ is the angle of internal friction of typical crustal rocks ($\phi \approx 30^\circ$). Because $\sigma_1$ is horizontal, the theoretical dip of a newly initiated thrust fault is: $$\theta = 45^\circ - \frac{30^\circ}{2} = 45^\circ - 15^\circ = 30^\circ$$ Thus, Andersonian mechanics predicts that contractional shear planes naturally initiate with gentle dips around $30^\circ$, universally satisfying the criterion $< 45^\circ$.
Figure 21.1 (Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, 2nd Ed., Fig. 9.2): Anderson's three tectonic fault regimes. In the contractional thrust regime, $\sigma_1$ is horizontal and $\sigma_3$ is vertical, generating shallow-dipping conjugate shear fractures dipping at $\approx 30^\circ$ ($< 45^\circ$).
2. Geometric Demarcation: Thrust Fault ($< 45^\circ$) vs. Reverse Fault ($> 45^\circ$)

In both classical and modern structural geology syllabi (Bangar, p. 202; Billings, 1972; Fossen, 2016), the dip angle of $45^\circ$ represents the formal dividing boundary between contractional fault species:

Contractional Fault Type Dip Angle Range ($\theta$) Tectonic Architecture & Kinematics Classic Geological Examples
Thrust Fault (Sensu Stricto) $< 45^\circ$ (typically $10^\circ - 35^\circ$) Low-angle contraction; large horizontal transport ($> 10 - 100\text{ km}$); forms nappes, klippes, and tectonic windows (fensters) Main Boundary Thrust (MBT), Main Central Thrust (MCT) in Himalayas; Moine Thrust (Scotland)
Low-Angle Overthrust / Decollement $< 10^\circ - 15^\circ$ (sub-horizontal) Basal detachment gliding along weak evaporite/shale horizons (thin-skinned fold-and-thrust belts) Main Frontal Thrust (MFT) / Siwalik detachment
High-Angle Reverse Fault $> 45^\circ$ (typically $55^\circ - 75^\circ$) Steep contractional slip; dominantly vertical stratigraphic offset; often reactivated steep normal faults (tectonic inversion) Laramide basement-cored uplifts (Rocky Mountains, USA)
"Thrust Fault: A reverse fault in which the fault plane dips at an angle less than 45° (usually between 10° and 30°) is termed a thrust fault or overthrust. In such faults, older rocks are pushed horizontally over younger rocks across large distances." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Folds and Faults, p. 202)
Figure 21.2 (Haakon Fossen, Fig. 9.2c): Cross-sectional geometry of contractional faults. Shows low-angle thrust faults ($\theta < 45^\circ$) with extensive horizontal displacement placing older strata over younger strata.
3. Regional Tectonic Manifestations: Nappes, Klippen & Fensters

Because thrust faults dip at shallow angles ($< 45^\circ$), prolonged erosion of the displaced thrust sheet (allochthon) uncovers complex geometric outcrop patterns:

  • Nappe: A colossal, sheet-like allochthonous rock mass displaced horizontally tens to hundreds of kilometers along a low-angle thrust surface.
  • Klippe (Plural: Klippen): An isolated, erosional remnant of the thrust sheet stranded atop younger autochthonous footwall rocks.
  • Fenster (Tectonic Window): An erosional hole or window gouged through the hanging wall sheet exposing the underlying younger autochthonous rocks beneath.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 21)
Q21. Thrust faults are reverse faults that dip at :
(A) > 45°
(B) < 45°  ✔
(C) 45°
(D) 90°
✔
Correct Answer: (B) < 45°
In standard structural geology (Bangar, p. 202; Billings; Fossen), a contractional fault where the hanging wall moves upward is formally classified as a thrust fault when the fault plane dips at an inclination less than 45° ($\theta < 45^\circ$). Contractional faults dipping $> 45^\circ$ are termed reverse faults.
(A) > 45° Incorrect (Defines High-Angle Reverse Faults)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Contractional faults dipping at angles greater than 45° ($\theta > 45^\circ$) are specifically designated as high-angle reverse faults. They are typically produced by tectonic inversion of older steep normal faults or deep basement upthrusts, producing high vertical throw with minimal horizontal displacement.
(B) < 45° Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: The universal geometric criterion that separates a thrust fault from a generic reverse fault is a dip angle $< 45^\circ$. In nature and Andersonian failure models ($\theta = 45^\circ - \phi/2 \approx 30^\circ$), most thrusts initiate at $25^\circ - 35^\circ$ and often flatten into bedding-parallel decollements dipping $< 15^\circ$.
(C) 45° Incorrect (Arbitrary Division Boundary, Not a Range)
Why this distractor is incorrect: 45° is the geometric boundary dividing low-angle thrusts ($< 45^\circ$) from high-angle reverse faults ($> 45^\circ$). A thrust fault does not dip exclusively at exactly 45°.
(D) 90° Incorrect (Vertical Fault / Strike-Slip Fault)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A dip of 90° represents a vertical fault. In Andersonian mechanics, vertical faults with horizontal displacement are strike-slip (wrench) faults ($\sigma_2$ vertical).

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A thrust fault dips at an angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ into a collisional mountain belt. Geodetic and seismic measurements indicate a net dip-slip displacement (slip) of $S = 5.0\text{ km}$. Calculate the horizontal crustal shortening ($\Delta L$) and vertical tectonic throw ($h$) produced by this fault.
    ✔ Worked Answer: Horizontal Shortening $\Delta L = 4.33\text{ km}$; Vertical Throw $h = 2.50\text{ km}$.
    Calculation:
    $$\text{Shortening } \Delta L = S \cdot \cos\theta = 5.0 \cdot \cos 30^\circ = 5.0 \times 0.8660 = 4.33\text{ km}$$
    $$\text{Vertical Throw } h = S \cdot \sin\theta = 5.0 \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 5.0 \times 0.5000 = 2.50\text{ km}$$
  • Practice Q2: What is a blind thrust fault, and why does it represent a grave seismic hazard in urban foreland regions?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A thrust fault that terminates underground without breaking the surface, creating an anticline fold (fault-propagation fold) above it.
    Explanation: Because the fault plane does not rupture the surface, it lacks a recognizable surface fault scarp, obscuring active seismic accumulation until a catastrophic earthquake occurs (e.g., 1994 Northridge, California earthquake).
  • Practice Q3: What stratigraphic diagnostic rule is universally violated when a borehole penetrates an active thrust fault?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Steno's Law of Superposition; the borehole encounters older rocks overlying younger rocks, and repeats stratigraphic sections.
    Explanation: Low-angle thrusting transports deeply buried older strata up and over younger rocks in the footwall, causing vertical repetition of layers.
  • Practice Q4: In Himalayan tectonics, name the major regional low-angle thrust fault separating the Lesser Himalayan sedimentary sequence from the Higher Himalayan Crystallines.
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Main Central Thrust (MCT).
    Explanation: The MCT is an iconic ductile shear zone and low-angle thrust dipping gently to the north, along which high-grade metamorphic rocks were thrust over low-grade Lesser Himalayan rocks.
  • Practice Q5: What is a duplex in thrust belt kinematics?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A stack of imbricate thrust slices (horses) bounded below by a floor thrust (sole thrust) and above by a roof thrust.
    Explanation: Duplexes accommodate massive crustal shortening by packing multiple lenticular thrust sheets into an overlapping shingles-like structural stack.
  • Practice Q6: What is the difference between a ramp and a flat in thin-skinned fold-and-thrust belts?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A flat is a bedding-parallel thrust segment in weak shale/salt; a ramp is a steeper segment cutting obliquely across competent sandstone/limestone.
    Explanation: Faults propagate easily along weak bedding planes (flats, $\theta \approx 0^\circ - 5^\circ$) and step upward through strong layers along ramps ($\theta \approx 30^\circ$), generating fault-bend folds.
  • Practice Q7: How does high pore fluid pressure ($u$) facilitate low-angle thrusting under Hubbert and Rubey's (1959) overthrust mechanism?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Pore fluid pressure reduces effective normal stress ($\sigma_n' = \sigma_n - u$), reducing frictional resistance along the fault plane to near zero.
    Explanation: Trapped high fluid pressures float the thrust sheet, allowing colossal nappes to glide horizontally across hundreds of kilometers without shattering.
  • Practice Q8: A tectonic window (fenster) is an erosional hole that exposes:
    ✔ Correct Answer: Autochthonous (younger) footwall rocks surrounded by allochthonous (older) hanging wall rocks.
    Explanation: Deep stream incision cuts through the allochthonous thrust sheet, opening a window to view the younger, overridden rocks underneath.
