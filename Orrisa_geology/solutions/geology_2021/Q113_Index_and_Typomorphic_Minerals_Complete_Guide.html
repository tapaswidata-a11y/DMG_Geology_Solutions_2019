1. Definitions, Historical Genesis & Concepts: Index Minerals and Typomorphic Minerals

In metamorphic geology and petrogenesis (Winter, 2014, pp. 420–428; Turner & Verhoogen, 1960; Bucher & Grapes, 2011), the reconstruction of temperature and pressure regimes relies on minerals whose chemical stability fields are restricted to specific thermodynamic boundaries:

  1. Index Minerals: First introduced by George Barrow (1893, 1912) in the Scottish Highlands, an index mineral is a specific metamorphic mineral whose first appearance (entry isograd) across a metamorphic terrane marks the beginning of a new, higher metamorphic zone or grade under prograde conditions (e.g., Chlorite $\rightarrow$ Biotite $\rightarrow$ Garnet $\rightarrow$ Staurolite $\rightarrow$ Kyanite $\rightarrow$ Sillimanite).
  2. Typomorphic Minerals: Formulated in the foundational metamorphic facies theory of Pentti Eskola (1915, 1920) and V.M. Goldschmidt, a typomorphic mineral (from Greek typos = type + morphe = form) is a mineral whose occurrence is strictly limited to and diagnostic of a particular metamorphic grade, facies, or $P\text{-}T$ field. For instance, glaucophane is typomorphic of the blueschist facies; omphacite is typomorphic of the eclogite facies; sillimanite is typomorphic of the upper amphibolite to granulite facies.

Comparison of Index Minerals vs. Typomorphic Minerals

While both terms designate minerals characteristic of a specific metamorphic grade, they emphasize distinct methodological perspectives:

  • Index Mineral: An operational field-mapping concept. Geologists map the isograd line connecting the first points of macroscopic emergence of the mineral in pelitic rocks.
  • Typomorphic Mineral: A rigorous thermodynamic and facies concept. It represents a phase that crystallizes exclusively under the restricted $P\text{-}T\text{-}X$ conditions of that particular facies, vanishing at higher or lower grades.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 113: Minerals that are characteristic for a certain metamorphic grade are called :
(A) Typomorphic minerals
(B) Barrovian minerals
(C) Index minerals
(D) Both (A) and (C) are correct
Verified Correct Answer: (D) Both (A) and (C) are correct
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Evaluated — Typomorphic Minerals

Partially Correct but Incomplete: In Pentti Eskola's metamorphic facies theory and classic European petrology (Turner & Verhoogen, 1960), minerals whose occurrence is restricted to and diagnostic of a specific metamorphic grade or facies are explicitly designated as typomorphic minerals. However, selecting (A) alone fails to recognize that "Index minerals" is an equally valid, widely recognized term for this exact concept.

Option (B) Refuted — Barrovian Minerals

Incorrect: "Barrovian minerals" is an informal colloquial term referring strictly to George Barrow's specific sequence of six pelitic minerals in the Scottish Highlands (chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite). It is not the formal scientific definition for minerals characteristic of a metamorphic grade in general (which applies across all metamorphic facies, Buchan series, and mafic/carbonate protoliths).

Option (C) Evaluated — Index Minerals

Partially Correct but Incomplete: In British and American geological literature (Winter, 2014, p. 420; Bangar, p. 85), minerals whose appearance marks a specific metamorphic grade are formally called index minerals. However, because "typomorphic minerals" is also canonically correct, choosing (C) alone misses the unified composite option.

Option (D) Confirmed — Both (A) and (C) are Correct

Correct: Both Typomorphic minerals (Eskola's facies concept) and Index minerals (Barrow's zonal concept) are standard, authoritative geological designations for minerals that are characteristic of a specific metamorphic grade. Therefore, Option (D) Both (A) and (C) are correct is the comprehensive, official verified answer.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

Explain why quartz is never used as an index mineral or typomorphic mineral in metamorphic petrology, despite being the most abundant mineral in pelitic schists and gneisses.

Solution: An index or typomorphic mineral must have a restricted thermodynamic stability field with sharp, well-defined entry or exit reactions in $P\text{-}T$ space. Low-quartz is chemically stable from surface weathering temperatures ($25^\circ\text{C}$) up to $573^\circ\text{C}$ (where it inverts to high-quartz), remaining stable up to $870^\circ\text{C}$ (tridymite) across all crustal pressures. Because quartz persists unchanged from un-metamorphosed shale through slate, phyllite, schist, and granulite, its mere presence provides zero diagnostic information regarding metamorphic grade.

Practice Problem 2

What is an "isograd" and how is it related to the concept of index minerals on a regional geological map?

Solution: An isograd (introduced by C.E. Tilley in 1924) is a mapped line of equal metamorphic grade on a geological map representing the first appearance (entry isograd) of a specific index mineral in rocks of appropriate bulk composition (e.g., the "garnet isograd" marks the locus of points where almandine garnet first crystallizes from chlorite and muscovite). An isograd physically represents a continuous thermodynamic reaction boundary in $P\text{-}T$ space.

Practice Problem 3

State the typomorphic mineral pair that definitively defines the Eclogite facies in metamorphosed basaltic crust.

Solution: The Eclogite facies is defined by the coexistence of two typomorphic minerals: 1. Omphacite: A sodic clinopyroxene solid solution between diopside ($\text{CaMgSi}_2\text{O}_6$) and jadeite ($\text{NaAlSi}_2\text{O}_6$). 2. Pyrope-rich Garnet: A magnesium-rich garnet ($\text{(Mg,Fe,Ca)}_3\text{Al}_2\text{Si}_3\text{O}_{12}$). Crucially, plagioclase feldspar is completely absent in eclogite, having broken down under extreme pressure ($P > 12\text{--}15\text{ kbar}$) into omphacite and quartz.

Practice Problem 4

Why can staurolite serve as an index mineral only in "pelitic" rocks (aluminous shales) and not in metabasites or impure limestones?

Solution: Metamorphic mineral assemblages are governed simultaneously by physical conditions ($P, T$) and bulk rock chemical composition ($X_{bulk}$). Staurolite ($\text{Fe}_2\text{Al}_9\text{O}_6(\text{SiO}_4)_4(\text{O,OH})_2$) is an ultra-aluminous, iron-rich silicate. It can crystallize only in rocks possessing high $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ and $\text{FeO}$ relative to $\text{MgO}, \text{CaO}$, and $\text{K}_2\text{O}$—a chemical signature unique to pelitic shales. Metabasites are rich in $\text{Ca, Mg}$, and poor in excess $\text{Al}$, crystallizing hornblende and plagioclase instead.

Practice Problem 5

What is a "bathozone" and how does it relate to index minerals?

Solution: A bathozone (Carmichael, 1978) is a metamorphic pressure zone delineated by the invariant intersections and relative stability order of index minerals in $P\text{-}T$ space (specifically the $\text{Al}_2\text{SiO}_5$ polymorphs and their reactions with staurolite, muscovite, and garnet). For example, Carmichael divided metamorphic crust into six bathozones (Bathozone 1 to 6), where higher bathozones indicate progressively deeper crustal burial during regional metamorphism.

Practice Problem 6

In low-pressure / high-temperature metamorphism (Buchan / Abukuma type), what index mineral sequence replaces the Barrovian Staurolite-Kyanite sequence?

Solution: Under Buchan low-pressure conditions ($P < 3.5\text{ kbar}$), high geothermal gradients bypass the stability fields of staurolite and kyanite. Instead, the prograde index sequence is: $$\text{Biotite} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{Cordierite}} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{Andalusite}} \rightarrow \text{Sillimanite} \pm \text{K-feldspar}$$ Here, cordierite and andalusite serve as the diagnostic low-pressure typomorphic index minerals.

Practice Problem 7

How does the presence of "chloritoid" vs. "staurolite" differentiate lower-grade from medium-grade pelites?

Solution: Chloritoid ($\text{(Fe,Mg)}_2\text{Al}_4\text{O}_2(\text{SiO}_4)_2(\text{OH})_4$) is a typomorphic index mineral of the upper greenschist to epidote-amphibolite facies ($T \approx 350\text{--}500^\circ\text{C}$). At $T \approx 500\text{--}550^\circ\text{C}$, chloritoid breaks down via terminal dehydration: $\text{Chloritoid} + \text{Quartz} \rightarrow \text{Staurolite} + \text{Almandine Garnet} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Thus, chloritoid marks lower grades, and its replacement by staurolite marks the transition into the amphibolite facies.

Practice Problem 8

What is a "relict mineral" and why must petrographers distinguish relict minerals from true typomorphic index minerals?

Solution: A relict mineral (or porphyroclast) is a metastable mineral crystal inherited from an earlier, lower-grade metamorphic episode (or original igneous/sedimentary protolith) that failed to react during peak metamorphism due to sluggish reaction kinetics (e.g., detrital quartz cores, or relict kyanite armored inside garnet during retrograde sillimanite growth). If a relict mineral is mistaken for a coexisting typomorphic phase, it leads to erroneous overestimates of phase equilibrium variance and invalid $P\text{-}T$ calculations.
