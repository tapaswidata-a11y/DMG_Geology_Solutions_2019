1. The Solid-State Physics of Trapped-Charge Geochronology

Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL) dating is a revolutionary trapped-charge geochronological technique that determines the elapsed time since ubiquitous mineral grains (principally quartz, $\text{SiO}_2$, and potassium feldspar, $\text{KAlSi}_3\text{O}_8$) were last exposed to sunlight or heat. The physical mechanism operates through solid-state defect crystal lattices:

  • Ionizing Ambient Radiation: Natural sediments contain trace concentrations of radioactive isotopes ($^{238}\text{U}$, $^{235}\text{U}$, $^{232}\text{Th}$, their decay daughters, and $^{40}\text{K}$), accompanied by penetrating cosmic rays. This flux emits $\alpha, \beta$, and $\gamma$ radiation, steadily ionizing surrounding mineral grains.
  • Metastable Trapping: Ionization excites electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. Most electrons recombine instantly, but a fraction become trapped in crystal lattice defects (such as oxygen vacancies / E' centers and titanium/aluminum substitution sites). These trapped electrons occupy metastable energy states with lifetimes exceeding millions of years at ambient Earth temperatures.
  • Stimulation & Luminescence Emission: When stimulated in the laboratory with specific wavelengths of light (blue light at $470\text{ nm}$ for quartz, or infrared at $870\text{ nm}$ for feldspar), the trapped electrons are evicted from their traps, drop into luminescence centers, and emit photons of higher energy (UV light around $375\text{ nm}$). The emitted photon flux is directly proportional to the radiation dose absorbed.
The Fundamental Luminescence Age Equation (Aitken, 1998)
$$\text{OSL Age (years)} = \frac{D_e}{\dot{D}} = \frac{\text{Equivalent Paleodose (Grays, Gy)}}{\text{Environmental Dose Rate (Gy/year or Gy/ka)}}$$

Where $D_e$ is the total ionizing radiation dose accumulated by the mineral since burial, and $\dot{D}$ is the annual dose rate emitted by radioactive decay in the host sediment and cosmic radiation.

Figure 6.1: Physical Mechanism of OSL Dating. The cyclic luminescence clock: (1) Daylight bleaching during subaerial transport zeroes the trapped electrons; (2) Burial under darkness accumulates trapped charge at dose rate $\dot{D}$; (3) Laboratory optical stimulation releases stored luminescence measuring paleodose $D_e$.
2. The Zeroing Event: Optical Bleaching vs. Thermal Firing

For a trapped-charge clock to measure the true geological or archaeological age, the stored charge in the crystal traps must be completely evacuated (zeroed) at the starting event of interest ($t = 0$):

  • (i) Naturally Occurring Sediments (Optical Bleaching): During subaerial transport by wind (aeolian dunes, loess, sand sheets), rivers (alluvial plains, bars), or waves (coastal beaches, barrier islands), mineral grains are exposed to sunlight. Natural daylight is so potent that just a few seconds to minutes of direct sunlight exposure completely bleaches the light-sensitive quartz traps to zero. Upon subsequent burial by overlying strata, darkness ensues, and the luminescence clock begins ticking.
  • (ii) Man-Made Materials (Thermal Zeroing): When ancient humans fired archaeological ceramic vessels, pottery, bricks, or burnt clay hearths in kilns at temperatures exceeding $450 - 500^\circ\text{C}$, the thermal energy completely evacuated all trapped electrons, resetting the luminescence clock to zero at the exact moment of manufacture.
"Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating determines the time since quartz or feldspar grains were last exposed to daylight or heat. It is widely applied to naturally occurring sediments such as aeolian sands, loess, fluvial deposits, and coastal dunes, as well as heated archaeological materials like pottery and burnt flints." — E.J. Rhodes, Optically Stimulated Luminescence Dating of Sediments over the Past 200,000 Years (Quaternary Science Reviews, 2011, p. 771)
3. Crystal Saturation: Why Intrusive Igneous & Metamorphic Rocks Fail

Why are (iii) Intrusive igneous rocks (granites, gabbros) and (iv) Metasedimentary rocks (quartzites, schists) fundamentally unsuitable for determining geological crystallization or metamorphic ages via OSL?

  • Trapping Saturation Limit ($D_0$): Mineral defect traps have a finite capacity. Under continuous natural background radiation ($\sim 2 - 4\text{ mGy/yr}$), the electron traps in quartz become completely filled and reach asymptotic saturation at doses around $100 - 300\text{ Gy}$ (corresponding to an absolute dating ceiling of $\sim 150,000 - 300,000\text{ years}$).
  • Millions of Years of Radiation: Intrusive igneous rocks crystallized deep in the crust millions of years ago (e.g., Archean or Proterozoic granites). Their traps reached 100% saturation tens of millions of years ago, rendering them completely incapable of measuring geological formation age.
  • Absence of Sunlight Zeroing: Intrusive plutons crystallize kilometers underground without ever seeing daylight. Because there was no optical bleaching event during crystallization, the OSL clock was never zeroed.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 6)
Q6. Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL) dating methods are useful for determining the age of: (i) Naturally occurring sediments, (ii) Man-made materials like pottery, (iii) Intrusive igneous rocks, (iv) Metasedimentary rocks. Choose the correct set of answer from the following options :
(A) (i), (ii) and (iii)
(B) (i) and (ii)  ✔
(C) (iii) and (iv)
(D) (i) and (iii)
✔
Correct Answer: (B) (i) and (ii)
OSL is specifically designed to date materials whose electron traps were zeroed by daylight exposure or kiln firing prior to burial—namely (i) naturally occurring sediments (aeolian, fluvial, coastal sands) and (ii) man-made materials like pottery (fired ceramics, burnt clay). Intrusive igneous and metamorphic rocks are saturated and cannot be OSL-dated for geological age.
(A) (i), (ii) and (iii) Incorrect (Wrongly Includes Intrusive Rocks)
Why this option is incorrect: This option includes (iii) Intrusive igneous rocks. Deep-seated plutonic rocks like granite or diorite never experience daylight exposure during emplacement and crystallize over millions of years, causing their electron traps to be completely saturated with radiation.
(B) (i) and (ii) Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Statements (i) and (ii) are the precise, classic domains of luminescence dating. Exposure to sunlight during sedimentary transport bleaches quartz/feldspar in naturally occurring sediments (i), while the intense heat of kiln firing ($> 500^\circ\text{C}$) thermal-bleaches archaeological ceramics (ii), providing the requisite zero baseline ($t = 0$).
(C) (iii) and (iv) Incorrect (Exclusively Contains Invalid Saturated Bedrock)
Why this option is incorrect: Both intrusive igneous rocks and metasedimentary rocks possess geological ages measured in millions or billions of years (Phanerozoic, Proterozoic, Archean), far exceeding the Quaternary saturation limit ($\sim 300\text{ ka}$) of luminescence dating.
(D) (i) and (iii) Incorrect (Omits Archaeological Pottery & Includes Intrusive Rocks)
Why this option is incorrect: This option wrongly omits man-made pottery (which was historically the very first material dated by thermoluminescence by Martin Aitken in the 1960s) and erroneously includes intrusive igneous rocks.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical Age Calculation): In an OSL laboratory, a purified quartz aliquot from an alluvial terrace in the Mahanadi Basin yields an equivalent paleodose $D_e = 64.8\text{ Gy}$ using the SAR protocol. High-resolution gamma spectrometry and in-situ dosimetry measure an annual environmental dose rate $\dot{D} = 2.40\text{ Gy/ka}$ ($2.40\text{ mGy/yr}$). Calculate the depositional age of the terrace sand.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $27,000\text{ years}$ ($27.0\text{ ka}$; Late Pleistocene / Marine Isotope Stage 2).
    Calculation:
    $$\text{OSL Age} = \frac{D_e}{\dot{D}} = \frac{64.8\text{ Gy}}{2.40\text{ Gy/ka}} = 27.0\text{ ka} = 27,000\text{ years}$$ This dates the burial of the sand during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) aggradation phase.
  • Practice Q2: What is the meaning and danger of partial bleaching in sediment luminescence dating, and which sedimentary environment is most susceptible?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Incomplete zeroing of electron traps prior to burial; highly prevalent in turbid, deep fluvial and subaqueous debris-flow environments.
    Explanation: Water absorbs the UV/blue spectrum rapidly, and turbid suspended sediment shields grains from sunlight. Unbleached residual trapped charge remains, causing the calculated OSL age to be significantly older than the true burial event (age overestimation).
  • Practice Q3: What revolutionary analytical protocol, developed by A.S. Murray and A.G. Wintle in 2000, transformed OSL dating into a high-precision technique?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Single-Aliquot Regenerative-dose (SAR) protocol.
    Explanation: The SAR protocol measures the natural luminescence and multiple laboratory regeneration doses on a single aliquot, using a small test dose after each cycle to monitor and mathematically correct for luminescence sensitivity changes.
  • Practice Q4: Why is Infrared Stimulated Luminescence (IRSL) specifically applied to potassium feldspar rather than quartz?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Quartz traps cannot be evicted by low-energy infrared photons ($870\text{ nm}$), whereas K-feldspar contains an infrared resonance excitation trap with an internal dose rate from radioactive $^{40}\text{K}$.
    Explanation: K-feldspar is sensitive to IR stimulation, allowing dating in red-light safe conditions without exciting quartz. However, feldspars suffer from anomalous fading (quantum mechanical tunneling of electrons), which must be corrected.
  • Practice Q5: How do cosmic rays contribute to the total annual environmental dose rate ($\dot{D}$)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Through penetrating muons and neutrons; the cosmic dose rate decreases exponentially with burial depth below the ground surface.
    Explanation: At the surface, cosmic radiation contributes $\sim 0.30\text{ mGy/yr}$. With increasing overburden depth, absorption by overlying sediment attenuates the flux, approaching a low asymptotic value beyond $10\text{ meters}$ depth.
  • Practice Q6: What is the physical basis of luminescence thermometry or rock surface exposure dating?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Measuring the depth-penetration profile of daylight bleaching into the outer millimeters of an exposed rock boulder surface.
    Explanation: Sunlight penetrates several millimeters into transparent quartz grains on rock surfaces, creating a bleaching front that advances inward over time, allowing direct dating of rock-art, glacial erratic exposure, or masonry construction.
  • Practice Q7: Why must OSL sediment samples be collected strictly inside opaque, light-tight tubes (e.g., black PVC or steel tubes hammered into trench walls)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because any exposure to ambient daylight during excavation instantly bleaches the quartz grains, permanently destroying the trapped paleodose record.
    Explanation: Sampling tubes are sealed light-tight in the field and opened only inside darkrooms illuminated by dim amber/red safelights.
  • Practice Q8: Why does moisture content (interstitial pore water) affect the calculated luminescence age of a buried sand deposit?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Water absorbs ionizing radiation more effectively than solid mineral grains, reducing the effective annual dose rate ($\dot{D}$).
    Explanation: Higher moisture levels absorb gamma and beta rays before they reach the quartz grains. If water content is underestimated, $\dot{D}$ will be overestimated, resulting in an erroneously young calculated age ($Age = D_e / \dot{D}$).
