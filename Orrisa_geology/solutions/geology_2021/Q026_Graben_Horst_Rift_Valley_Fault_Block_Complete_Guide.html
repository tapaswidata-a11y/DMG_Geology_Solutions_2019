1. Extensional Tectonic Mechanics & Graben-Horst Geometry

In continental rift systems and regions undergoing horizontal crustal extension ($\Delta L > 0$), the maximum principal stress is vertical ($\sigma_1 = \text{overburden weight}$), while minimum principal stress is horizontal ($\sigma_3 = \text{extensional axis}$). Under this stress regime, conjugate normal faults dipping at $\approx 60^\circ$ toward each other partition the brittle upper crust into alternating elevated and depressed fault blocks:

  • Graben (German for "trench" or "ditch"): A downthrown, depressed structural block bounded on both sides by conjugate normal faults dipping toward each other. A graben forms an elongated, fault-bounded structural depression or rift valley.
  • Horst (German for "eyrie" or "nest"): An upthrown, elevated crustal block bounded on both sides by conjugate normal faults dipping away from each other. Horsts form linear mountain ranges or plateaus flanking rift basins.
  • Half-Graben: An asymmetrical extensional basin bounded by a major master normal fault (border fault) on one side and a gentle roll-over flexure on the other, characteristic of modern continental rifts.
Figure 26.1 (Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, Fig. 9.5): Extensional tectonic block diagram showing a central downthrown Graben bounded by conjugate normal faults, flanked by elevated Horsts.
2. Global & Indian Archetypes: Rhine Graben, East African Rift & Narmada

Grabens and rift valleys host some of Earth's most spectacular geological and physiographic features:

  • The Rhine Graben (Western Europe): The classic European type-locality, where a $300\text{ km}$ long, $35\text{ km}$ wide graben floor (the Rhine Valley) dropped down between the elevated Vosges Mountains horst in France and the Black Forest (Schwarzwald) horst in Germany.
  • The East African Rift System (EARS): A grand active continental divergent boundary where Africa is splitting into the Nubian and Somalian plates, forming giant grabens occupied by deep rift lakes (Lake Tanganyika, Lake Malawi).
  • The Narmada-Son Graben (Central India): A major fault-bounded rift basin where the Narmada River flows westward within a depressed crustal graben bounded by the elevated Vindhyan Range horst to the north and the Satpura Range horst to the south.
"Graben and Horst: A Graben or Rift Valley is formed when a central block is displaced downward between two parallel normal faults that dip towards each other... A Horst or Block Mountain is formed when the central block remains elevated while the bordering blocks are downthrown, or when the central block is pushed upward between faults that dip away from each other." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Folds and Faults, p. 204, Fig. 7.30)
Figure 26.2 (K.M. Bangar, Fig. 7.30 & 7.31): Classical cross-sectional diagrams of a Graben (rift valley) and Horst (block mountain) with relative fault displacements.
3. Refutation of Horst, Strike Valley, and Dip Valley

Understanding geomorphic vs. structural terms eliminates distractors:

  • Horst (Option A): An elevated, upthrown crustal block (block mountain), the polar opposite of a depressed valley.
  • Strike Valley (Option B): An erosional valley carved by a river flowing parallel to the strike of soft sedimentary formations (subsequent stream), not a fault-bounded structural downthrow.
  • Dip Valley (Option D): An erosional valley carved down the dip slope of inclined strata by a consequent or resequent stream.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 26)
Q26. A valley formed by the downward displacement of a fault bounded block is known as :
(A) Horst
(B) Strike valley
(C) Graben  ✔
(D) Dip valley
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Graben
An elongate, fault-bounded structural valley or trench formed by the downward displacement of a crustal block between conjugate normal faults is formally designated as a Graben (or Rift Valley).
(A) Horst Incorrect (Upthrown Elevated Block Mountain)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A horst is an upthrown, elevated crustal block standing prominent between down-dropped blocks. It forms a block mountain or plateau (such as the Vosges, Black Forest, or Satpura range), the exact morphological opposite of a downthrown valley.
(B) Strike valley Incorrect (Fluvial Erosional Feature Along Strike)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A strike valley is an erosional landscape feature excavated by a subsequent stream eroding soft rock beds parallel to regional strike. It is produced by fluvial differential erosion, not tectonic block faulting.
(C) Graben Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Derived from the German word for trench, a graben is the standard geological term for an elongate crustal valley produced by the downward displacement of a rock block bounded by parallel normal faults dipping toward each other. When developed on a continental scale, it forms a rift valley.
(D) Dip valley Incorrect (Erosional Valley Down Structural Dip)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A dip valley is carved by a river flowing in the direction of stratal dip across a homoclinal ridge or cuesta. It is an erosional fluvial feature, not a fault-bounded downthrown block.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: What is a half-graben, and what structural feature separates it from a full graben?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An asymmetrical rift basin bounded by a major planar or listric normal fault on only ONE side, with a flexural rollover on the other.
    Explanation: Most continental rifts are composed of chains of linked half-grabens with alternating polarity, rather than perfectly symmetrical grabens bounded by two opposing faults.
  • Practice Q2: What is a listric normal fault, and what happens to the hanging wall block as it slips downward?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A normal fault with a curved, concave-upward fault plane that flattens with depth; hanging wall block rotates backward creating a rollover anticline.
    Explanation: Slip along a curved fault surface creates extension and rollover, forming crestal collapse grabens that trap petroleum in extensional basins.
  • Practice Q3: In the Basin and Range Province of western North America, what landforms represent the horsts and grabens?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The linear mountain ranges are horsts; the intervening flat desert basins (playas) are grabens and half-grabens.
    Explanation: Crustal extension across Nevada and Utah over the past 20 million years has doubled the width of the crust, creating alternating horst ranges and graben basins.
  • Practice Q4: In Indian tectonics, the Narmada and Tapti river valleys flow westward across peninsular India through which structural feature?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Narmada-Son-Damodar Graben System (SONATA Zone).
    Explanation: While most peninsular rivers flow eastward to the Bay of Bengal, the Narmada and Tapti are structurally confined within ancient, reactivated Proterozoic-Cretaceous grabens.
  • Practice Q5: What is an accommodation zone in a continental rift system?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A complex structural transfer zone connecting two half-grabens that have opposing fault dip polarities.
    Explanation: Accommodation zones transfer extensional strain between overlapping normal fault segments via oblique faults and strike-slip tear faults.
  • Practice Q6: What sedimentary rock deposit is characteristically deposited along the border fault of an active graben?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Coarse alluvial fan fanglomerates and talus breccias.
    Explanation: Rapid uplift of the horst footwall generates steep fault scarps that shed coarse, poorly sorted fanglomerates directly into the graben basin.
  • Practice Q7: What is a pull-apart basin, and how does it relate to strike-slip faulting?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A rhomboidal graben-like extensional basin formed at a releasing bend or en echelon stepover along a strike-slip fault.
    Explanation: Left-stepping dextral faults or right-stepping sinistral faults pull crust apart, creating deep rhombic basins (e.g., the Dead Sea basin).
  • Practice Q8: Why do continental rifts and grabens exhibit positive Bouguer gravity anomalies despite being filled with low-density sediments?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Due to crustal thinning and passive upwelling of high-density asthenospheric mantle beneath the rift axis.
    Explanation: Lithospheric necking pulls the dense mantle closer to the surface, generating an overriding positive regional Bouguer gravity signature.
