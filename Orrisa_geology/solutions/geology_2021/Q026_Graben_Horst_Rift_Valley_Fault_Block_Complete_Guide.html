Q26: Graben, Horst & Continental Rift Valley Tectonics

Exam: Geology Paper-II (2021)  |  Authoritative Reference: Van der Pluijm & Marshak (Earth Structure, Ch. 16, pp. 412-420); Haakon Fossen (Structural Geology, 2nd Ed., Ch. 17)

Part I: Theoretical Foundation & Concept Masterclass

26.1 Extensional Fault Systems & Graben Geometry

In structural geology and tectonics (Van der Pluijm & Marshak, Earth Structure, 2nd Ed., Ch. 16; Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, 2nd Ed., Ch. 17), crustal extension produces conjugate pairs of normal faults. When regional tensional stress (where maximum principal stress $\sigma_1$ is vertical and minimum principal stress $\sigma_3$ is horizontal) acts on brittle lithosphere, characteristic fault-block morphologies develop:

Morphotectonic Feature Kinematic & Geometric Definition Structural Displacement Global & Indian Examples
Graben (Rift Valley) A depressed, elongate crustal valley bounded on both flanks by inward-dipping conjugate normal faults. Downthrown central block (hanging wall of both faults) relative to flanks. East African Rift, Rhine Graben, Narmada-Tapi Rift (SONATA).
Horst An elevated, elongate crustal ridge bounded by outward-dipping normal faults. Upthrown central block (footwall of both faults) relative to flanking grabens. Vosges and Black Forest (flanking Rhine Graben), Satpura Range.
Half-Graben An asymmetric depression tilted towards a single major master listric border fault. Hanging-wall block undergoes rollover anticlinal deformation and tilt. Basin and Range Province, East African lake basins.
Fault Escarpment A steep topographic scarp formed directly by differential vertical slip on a single fault plane. Linear step in topography along a single fault trace. Wasatch fault scarp (Utah).

26.2 Continental Rifting & Lithospheric Attenuation Mechanics

Active and passive rifting mechanisms lead to lithospheric thinning ($eta$-factor, McKenzie 1978):

$$eta = rac{t_0}{t_1}$$

Where $t_0$ is initial crustal thickness and $t_1$ is thinned post-rift thickness. Subsidence of the central graben floor is accommodated initially by fault-controlled mechanical crustal stretching (syn-rift phase), followed by regional thermal cooling subsidence (post-rift sag phase).

Part II: Official Question & Critical Option Dissection

Q26. A valley formed by the downward displacement of a fault bounded block is known as :
(A) Nappe
(B) Fault escarpment
(C) Graben
(D) All of these
VERIFIED ANSWER: OPTION (C)

Correct Answer: (C) Graben — A valley formed by the downward displacement of a crustal block bounded on either side by normal faults is universally designated in structural geology as a Graben (or Rift Valley).

Option-by-Option Critical Dissection

  • (A) Nappe — INCORRECT: A nappe is a massive, allochthonous rock sheet or recumbent fold that has moved tens to hundreds of kilometers horizontally along a low-angle thrust fault in a contractional orogen (e.g., Swiss Alps or Himalayas), entirely unrelated to extensional downthrown valleys.
  • (B) Fault escarpment — INCORRECT: A fault escarpment is simply the cliff-like surface slope formed by vertical offset along an individual fault plane; it is not a fault-bounded downthrown valley.
  • (C) Graben — CORRECT: From the German word for "ditch" or "trench", a graben is defined precisely as an elongate valley formed by the downward subsidence of a central block bounded by normal faults.
  • (D) All of these — INCORRECT: Erroneous combination of contractional (nappe), geomorphic (escarpment), and extensional graben terms.

High-Yield Concept Check & Numerical Application

Practice Problem 26: Graben Subsidence from Conjugate Normal Faulting

A symmetrical continental graben is bounded by two conjugate planar normal faults each dipping at $\delta = 60^\circ$. If horizontal crustal extension produces a net heave of $H = 2.4\text{ km}$ across the basin ($1.2\text{ km}$ heave per fault), calculate the vertical structural throw (subsidence of the graben floor) accommodated by each fault.

Solution:

For a planar normal fault with dip angle $\delta$, the relationship between vertical throw ($T$) and horizontal heave ($H_f$) is:

$$T = H_f \times \tan(\delta)$$

Given $H_f = 1.2\text{ km}$ and $\delta = 60^\circ$ ($ an 60^\circ = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$):

$$T = 1.2 \times 1.732 = 2.08\text{ km}$$

Thus, each border fault accommodates approximately $2.08\text{ km}$ of vertical graben subsidence.
