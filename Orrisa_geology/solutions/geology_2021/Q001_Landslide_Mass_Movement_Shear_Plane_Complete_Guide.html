Q1: Slope Stability, Mass Movement vs. Landslide & Shear Plane Mechanics

Exam: Geology Paper-II (2021)  |  Authoritative Reference: K.M. Bangar (Engineering Geology, Ch. 12, pp. 245-258); Cruden & Varnes (1996, TRB Sp. Rep. 247)

Part I: Theoretical Foundation & Concept Masterclass

1.1 Fundamental Distinction: Mass Wasting vs. True Landslides

In classical geomorphology and engineering geology (Varnes, 1978; Cruden & Varnes, 1996; Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology, Ch. 12), the downslope displacement of earth materials is categorized under the comprehensive umbrella of mass movement (mass wasting).

However, engineering geology establishes a critical mechanical demarcation based on the existence of a discrete shear surface (slip plane):

Classification Criteria Mass Movement (Sensu Lato) Landslide (Sensu Stricto) Creep Avalanche
Shear Plane May be absent, diffuse, internal, or discrete Discrete, well-defined shear failure plane No discrete shear plane; continuous internal viscous strain Turbulent fluid-like flow, cushion of compressed air
Displacement Mechanics Falls, topples, slides, spreads, flows Rigid or semi-rigid shear slide (rotational or translational) Slow, imperceptible seasonal ratchet strain Extremely rapid, airborne turbulent granular flow
Mohr-Coulomb Failure Variable $\tau_f = c' + \sigma_n' \tan \phi'$ localized on basal surface Low-stress distributed shear strain $(\dot{\gamma})$ Fluidized granular dispersion

1.2 Kinematic Classifications of Landslides (Varnes, 1978)

A true landslide occurs when the shear stress $(\tau)$ along a potential failure surface exceeds the shear strength $(\tau_f)$ of the rock or soil mass. The two primary categories defined by the geometry of the shear plane are:

  • Rotational Slides (Slumps): The shear plane is concave upward (spoon-shaped). The movement involves backward tilting of the slip block towards the scarp.
  • Translational Slides: The shear plane is approximately planar or undulating, often controlled by structural discontinuities (bedding planes, foliation, joints, or fault surfaces).

Part II: Official Question & Critical Option Dissection

Q1. The generic term describing those downward movements of slope-forming material as a result of shear failure occurring along a well-defined shear plane is :
(A) Mass movement
(B) Landslide
(C) Creep
(D) Avalanche
VERIFIED ANSWER: OPTION (B)

Correct Answer: (B) Landslide — Downward movement of slope-forming material resulting from shear failure along a well-defined shear plane is strictly defined as a Landslide.

Option-by-Option Critical Dissection

  • (A) Mass movement — INCORRECT (Distractor / Over-generalization): Mass movement is the broad generic term that includes falls, topples, slides, flows, and creep. It is not restricted to movements with a well-defined shear plane (e.g. mudflows and creep lack a distinct shear plane).
  • (B) Landslide — CORRECT: In international engineering geology terminology (Varnes 1978; IAEG Commission on Landslides), a landslide is explicitly defined as shear failure localized along a recognizable, well-defined shear plane or zone of rupture.
  • (C) Creep — INCORRECT: Soil creep or rock creep is an extremely slow, imperceptible, continuous deformation occurring throughout the entire thickness of the soil without a discrete shear failure surface.
  • (D) Avalanche — INCORRECT: An avalanche is a rapid, turbulent downrush of snow, ice, or shattered rock debris supported by dynamic flow or air cushioning rather than sliding on a stationary shear plane.

High-Yield Concept Check & Numerical Applications

Practice Problem 1: Factor of Safety on a Planar Slip Surface

A translational landslide occurs along a planar bedding plane inclined at $\theta = 30^\circ$. The cohesive strength $c' = 15\text{ kPa}$, effective normal stress $\sigma_n' = 80\text{ kPa}$, and internal friction angle $\phi' = 25^\circ$. If the driving shear stress along the slip plane is $\tau_d = 45\text{ kPa}$, calculate the Factor of Safety ($FS$) and determine slope stability.

Solution:

The resisting shear strength along the shear plane is given by the Mohr-Coulomb criterion:

$$\tau_f = c' + \sigma_n' \tan \phi' = 15 + 80 \tan(25^\circ) = 15 + 80(0.4663) = 15 + 37.3 = 52.3\text{ kPa}$$

The Factor of Safety ($FS$) is the ratio of resisting shear strength to driving shear stress:

$$FS = \frac{\tau_f}{\tau_d} = \frac{52.3}{45.0} \approx 1.16$$

Since $FS > 1.0$, the slope is conditionally stable, but vulnerable to failure if pore water pressure reduces $\sigma_n'$.
