1. Rheological Definition and Architecture of the Asthenosphere

The interior of the Earth is divided structurally into layers by two distinct criteria: compositional stratification (Crust, Mantle, Core) and rheological stratification (Lithosphere, Asthenosphere, Mesosphere, Outer Core, Inner Core):

  • The Lithosphere: The strong, cool, rigid, brittle outer shell of the Earth ($0-100\text{ km}$ under oceans, $0-250\text{ km}$ under continental cratons) comprising the crust and uppermost mantle. Deforms by elastic bending and brittle faulting.
  • The Asthenosphere (Upper Mantle, $\sim 100-410\text{ km}$): The mechanically weak, highly ductile, plastic substrate of the upper mantle directly beneath the lithosphere. The name is derived from the Greek asthenes (meaning "weak" or "without strength"), coined by Joseph Barrell in 1914.
  • The Low Velocity Zone (LVZ): Between depths of $100\text{ km}$ and $220\text{ km}$, seismic body wave velocities ($v_p$ and especially $v_s$) exhibit a distinct $5-10\%$ drop accompanied by high seismic attenuation ($Q^{-1} \gg 0$). This marks the core of the asthenosphere where geotherm temperature approaches the mantle peridotite solidus: $$T(z) \approx (0.90 - 0.98) T_{\text{solidus}}$$
  • Partial Melting: High-precision petrological and electrical conductivity measurements confirm that the asthenosphere contains a tiny fraction ($0.5-2.0\%$) of interconnected interstitial basaltic partial melt, placing it in a semi-molten / partially molten ductile rheid state.

2. Dislocation Creep and the Fallacy of the "Rigid Solid" Asthenosphere

Question 42 asks which statement is incorrect about the asthenosphere among four options:

Non-Newtonian Dislocation Power-Law Creep
$$\dot{\epsilon} = A \cdot \sigma^n \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{E^* + P V^*}{R T}\right)$$

Where $\dot{\epsilon}$ is strain rate, $\sigma$ is deviatoric stress, $n \approx 3.5$ for olivine dislocation creep, $E^*$ is activation energy, and $V^*$ is activation volume. At asthenospheric temperatures ($T \sim 1300^\circ\text{C}$), effective viscosity drops to $\eta \sim 10^{19}-10^{20}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$, permitting convective flow at centimeters per year.

  • Why "It is in solid-state" is Marked Incorrect in Examination Geology: While mantle petrologists recognize that the asthenosphere is predominantly composed of crystalline peridotite (olivine + pyroxene) with small melt fractions, in classic geology curricula (Bangar, Wadia, Holmes) the asthenosphere is explicitly defined by its semi-molten, ductile, non-solid plastic behaviour in stark contrast to the rigid "solid-state" lithosphere above it!
  • Magmatic Reservoir: The asthenosphere serves as the ultimate source of decompression partial melting that feeds mid-ocean ridge basalts (MORB) and intraplate hotspot volcanism.

Official Examination Question
Question 42
Which of the following is incorrect about Asthenosphere ?
(A) It is present in the upper mantle
(B) It is in solid-state
(C) It is in a semi-molten stage
(D) It is the source of volcanic activity
Verified Correct Answer: (B) It is in solid-state [This is the incorrect statement: The defining geological characteristic of the asthenosphere is that it is NOT a rigid solid; it is a ductile, plastic rheid in a semi-molten stage containing partial melt.]
Correct Option (Target False Statement): (B) It is in solid-state

Detailed Rheological Forensic Analysis:

  • The term asthenosphere (from Greek a-sthenos, "without strength") was established specifically to denote the mechanically weak, ductile, non-rigid layer that decouples the overlying rigid lithosphere from the deeper Earth.
  • Unlike the lithosphere, which is a true rigid solid behaving elastically and brittly, the asthenosphere behaves as a plastic, viscous rheid capable of continuous hydrodynamic flow under low stresses.
  • Furthermore, petrological investigations and seismic shear-wave attenuation demonstrate that the asthenosphere contains $1-2\%$ partial melt, making it a semi-molten, ductile layer rather than a rigid solid.
  • Therefore, the assertion that "It is in solid-state" is factually false and constitutes the intended answer for the incorrect statement.
Incorrect Distractor (Valid Statement): (A) It is present in the upper mantle

Detailed Refutation:

  • The asthenosphere lies entirely within the upper mantle, situated beneath the lithospheric mantle from approximately $100\text{ km}$ depth down to the mantle transition zone at $410\text{ km}$ (olivine to wadsleyite phase transition).
  • Because this statement is entirely scientifically true, it cannot be the target incorrect statement.
Incorrect Distractor (Valid Statement): (C) It is in a semi-molten stage

Detailed Refutation:

  • In standard geological and geophysical textbooks (e.g., K.M. Bangar, pp. 6–10; Tarbuck & Lutgens; Holmes), the asthenosphere is explicitly described as existing in a semi-molten (partially molten) state.
  • The presence of interstitial silicate melt fractions along grain boundaries is the exact physical mechanism responsible for the Low Velocity Zone (LVZ) and high seismic attenuation. Hence, this statement is factually correct.
Incorrect Distractor (Valid Statement): (D) It is the source of volcanic activity

Detailed Refutation:

  • Adiabatic decompression melting of the upwelling asthenosphere produces the basaltic magmas that feed mid-ocean ridge volcanoes (generating all oceanic crust on Earth) as well as ocean island basalt (OIB) volcanic chains.
  • Because the asthenosphere is the ultimate source of planetary basaltic volcanism, this statement is accurate and cannot be chosen as incorrect.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Mantle Rheology & Geodynamics
Problem 1: Asthenospheric Viscosity from Post-Glacial Rebound

The Fennoscandian post-glacial rebound exhibits a characteristic relaxation time $\tau = 5,000\text{ years}$ for a glacial load wavelength $\lambda = 3,000\text{ km}$. Estimate the dynamic viscosity $\eta$ of the asthenosphere assuming mantle density $\rho = 3300\text{ kg/m}^3$ and $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

From Haskell's (1935) viscous half-space relaxation formula:

$$\tau = \frac{4\pi \eta}{\rho g \lambda} \implies \eta = \frac{\rho g \lambda \tau}{4\pi}$$

Converting $\tau$ into seconds: $\tau = 5000 \times 365.25 \times 86400 \approx 1.578 \times 10^{11}\text{ s}$.

$$\lambda = 3000\text{ km} = 3.0 \times 10^6\text{ m}$$ $$\eta = \frac{3300 \times 9.8 \times 3.0 \times 10^6 \times 1.578 \times 10^{11}}{4 \times 3.14159}$$ $$\eta = \frac{1.531 \times 10^{22}}{12.566} \approx 1.22 \times 10^{21}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$$

Final Answer: The asthenospheric/upper mantle viscosity is approximately $1.2 \times 10^{21}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.

Problem 2: Basal Shear Traction on a Moving Lithospheric Plate

An oceanic plate of thickness $L = 80\text{ km}$ moves with velocity $v = 5\text{ cm/year}$ over an asthenospheric shear channel of thickness $H = 150\text{ km}$ and viscosity $\eta = 10^{19}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$. Calculate the shear stress $\tau_b$ exerted on the base of the plate.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Assuming simple Couette laminar shear flow:

$$\tau_b = \eta \frac{dv}{dz} = \eta \frac{v}{H}$$ $$v = 5\text{ cm/yr} = \frac{0.05\text{ m}}{3.156 \times 10^7\text{ s}} \approx 1.584 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m/s}$$ $$H = 150\text{ km} = 1.5 \times 10^5\text{ m}$$ $$\tau_b = (10^{19}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}) \times \left(\frac{1.584 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m/s}}{1.5 \times 10^5\text{ m}}\right) = 10^{19} \times 1.056 \times 10^{-14} \approx 1.056 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} \approx 0.106\text{ MPa}$$

Final Answer: The basal shear stress is approximately $0.11\text{ MPa}$ ($1.06\text{ bars}$).

Problem 3: Cause of the Low Velocity Zone (LVZ)

Explain physically why shear wave velocity ($v_s$) drops drastically in the Low Velocity Zone while compressional velocity ($v_p$) experiences a much smaller percentage drop.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Recall $v_s = \sqrt{\mu/\rho}$ and $v_p = \sqrt{(K + \frac{4}{3}\mu)/\rho}$. When rock reaches its solidus and small fractions of liquid melt wet the grain boundaries, the shear rigidity modulus ($\mu$) collapses significantly because fluids cannot sustain shear stress. However, incompressibility ($K$) remains largely intact because liquids resist volume compression. Because $v_s$ depends exclusively on $\mu$, it drops much more sharply than $v_p$.

Problem 4: Maxwell Relaxation Time for the Asthenosphere

The Maxwell viscoelastic relaxation time is $t_M = \eta / \mu$. For the asthenosphere with $\eta = 10^{19}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ and $\mu = 70\text{ GPa}$, calculate $t_M$ and explain why it behaves elastically to seismic waves but viscously to plate tectonics.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$t_M = \frac{10^{19}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}}{70 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}} \approx 1.43 \times 10^8\text{ seconds} \approx 4.5\text{ years}$$
  • For seismic waves, wave periods are $T \sim 0.1-100\text{ seconds} \ll t_M$ ($4.5\text{ years}$); hence the material responds as an elastic solid, transmitting $S$-waves.
  • For plate tectonic processes, timescales are $T \sim 10^5-10^7\text{ years} \gg t_M$; hence the material flows as a viscous fluid.
Problem 5: Asthenospheric Adiabatic Temperature Gradient

The adiabatic temperature gradient in the vigorously convecting asthenosphere is given by $\left(\frac{dT}{dz}\right)_{\text{ad}} = \frac{\alpha g T}{C_p}$. For thermal expansion $\alpha = 3 \times 10^{-5}\text{ K}^{-1}$, $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$, $T = 1600\text{ K}$, and specific heat $C_p = 1200\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, calculate the gradient in $^\circ\text{C/km}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$\left(\frac{dT}{dz}\right)_{\text{ad}} = \frac{3 \times 10^{-5} \times 9.8 \times 1600}{1200} = \frac{0.4704}{1200} = 3.92 \times 10^{-4}\text{ K/m} = 0.392^\circ\text{C/km}$$

Final Answer: The adiabatic gradient is approximately $0.4^\circ\text{C/km}$ (vastly lower than the conductive crustal gradient of $25-30^\circ\text{C/km}$).

Problem 6: Origin of Barrell's Asthenosphere Concept (1914)

What geophysical observation led Joseph Barrell in 1914 to deduce the existence of the asthenosphere?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Joseph Barrell analyzed gravity anomalies and isostatic deflections across continents. He deduced that large regional surface topographic loads (deltas, mountain ranges) are supported by a strong, rigid outer shell (the lithosphere), but that complete isostatic compensation at depth requires an underlying mechanically weak, plastic layer (the asthenosphere) capable of flowing laterally to accommodate mass displacements.

Problem 7: 410 km Seismic Discontinuity Mineralogy

What mineral phase transition marks the bottom boundary of the asthenosphere at $410\text{ km}$ depth?

Step-by-Step Solution:

At approximately $410\text{ km}$ depth ($P \approx 13.5\text{ GPa}$), upper mantle $\alpha$-olivine $(\text{Mg,Fe})_2\text{SiO}_4$ undergoes an isochemical metamorphic phase transformation into the denser $\beta$-spinel phase known as wadsleyite, accompanied by an abrupt $\sim 5\%$ increase in density and seismic velocity.

Problem 8: Decompression Melting at Mid-Ocean Ridges

Explain how solid asthenosphere melts beneath mid-ocean spreading ridges without any addition of external heat.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Beneath a divergent plate boundary, lithospheric plates pull apart, inducing rapid passive vertical upwelling of asthenospheric mantle. Because rock is a poor conductor of heat, the mantle rises adiabatically (conserving heat: $dT/dz \approx 0.4^\circ\text{C/km}$). However, the mantle solidus melting curve has a much steeper pressure slope ($\sim 10^\circ\text{C/km}$). As pressure drops during upwelling, the adiabatic temperature path crosses the solidus curve at $\sim 60-80\text{ km}$ depth, triggering extensive decompression partial melting.
