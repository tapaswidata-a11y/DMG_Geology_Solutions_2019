PART I
Theoretical Foundation & Scientific Principles

1. Kinematic Classification of Faults by Net Slip Direction

In structural geology (Billings, 1972; Fossen, 2016; Bangar, p. 201), faults are classified kinematically according to the orientation of the net-slip vector (the actual vector displacement connecting two formerly contiguous points on opposite sides of the fault plane):

  • Dip-Slip Fault: A fault in which the relative movement of the blocks is parallel to the dip direction of the fault plane (the net slip vector lies parallel to the true dip line, meaning rake of slickenlines is $\approx 90^\circ$). Dip-slip faults are subdivided based on the sense of displacement:
    • Normal Fault: Hanging wall moves downward relative to footwall (extensional regime).
    • Reverse / Thrust Fault: Hanging wall moves upward relative to footwall (contractional regime).
  • Strike-Slip Fault (Wrench / Transcurrent Fault): A fault in which movement is strictly parallel to the strike line of the fault plane (horizontal displacement, rake $\approx 0^\circ$). Subdivided into Dextral (right-lateral) and Sinistral (left-lateral).
  • Oblique-Slip Fault: A fault combining significant components of both strike-slip and dip-slip motion ($0^\circ < \text{rake} < 90^\circ$, typically $15^\circ < \text{rake} < 75^\circ$).
  • Hinge Fault (Pivotal / Rotational Fault): A fault where displacement increases progressively away from a stationary pivot point.

2. Geometric Anatomy: Throw, Heave, and Net Slip

On a dip-slip fault dipping at angle $\theta$, the net dip slip ($S$) is geometrically resolved into two orthogonal components:

Mathematical Resolution of Dip Slip
$$\text{Vertical Throw } (h) = S \cdot \sin\theta$$ $$\text{Horizontal Heave } (l) = S \cdot \cos\theta$$

Where $S$ is dip slip, $h$ is throw (vertical stratigraphic displacement), and $l$ is heave (horizontal separation). For normal faults, heave represents crustal extension; for reverse faults, heave represents crustal shortening.

"Classification of Faults Based on Direction of Slip: 1. Dip-slip fault: In this type, the relative movement of the blocks is essentially parallel to the dip of the fault plane. It includes normal faults and reverse faults. 2. Strike-slip fault: Movement is parallel to the strike. 3. Oblique-slip fault: Movement is oblique to both dip and strike." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Folds and Faults, p. 201, Fig. 7.24)
Figure 28.2 (K.M. Bangar, Fig. 7.24): Dip-slip fault mechanics, illustrating displacement strictly parallel to the fault plane's dip inclination.
3. Refutation of Strike-Slip, Oblique-Slip, and Hinge Faults

Candidates must clearly differentiate alternative slip geometries:

  • (A) Strike-slip fault: Movement is parallel to the strike (horizontal line), not the dip.
  • (C) Oblique-slip fault: Movement is at an oblique angle, possessing both strike and dip components simultaneously.
  • (D) Hinge fault: Movement involves rotation around an axis perpendicular to the fault plane, where slip is not uniform or purely parallel to dip.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 28)
Q28. A fault in which the movement is parallel to the dip of the fault is called :
(A) Strike-slip fault
(B) Dip-slip fault  ✔
(C) Oblique-slip fault
(D) Hinge fault
✔
Correct Answer: (B) Dip-slip fault
A fault in which the relative movement of the displaced blocks is strictly parallel to the dip of the fault plane is universally defined as a Dip-slip fault (Bangar, p. 201; Fossen, p. 166).
(A) Strike-slip fault Incorrect (Displacement Parallel to Strike)
Why this distractor is incorrect: In a strike-slip fault (transcurrent/wrench fault), the displacement vector is horizontal and parallel to the strike line of the fault plane (e.g., San Andreas Fault). It has zero dip-slip component.
(B) Dip-slip fault Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: By standard international definition, a dip-slip fault is one in which the net slip is oriented parallel to the fault dip. It encompasses normal faults (hanging wall down) and reverse/thrust faults (hanging wall up).
(C) Oblique-slip fault Incorrect (Combines Strike and Dip Slip Simultaneously)
Why this distractor is incorrect: In an oblique-slip fault, the net displacement vector is neither parallel to strike nor parallel to dip, but cuts diagonally across the fault plane ($0^\circ < \text{rake} < 90^\circ$).
(D) Hinge fault Incorrect (Rotational Displacement around a Pivot)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A hinge (pivotal) fault involves rotational displacement around an axis perpendicular to the fault, such that displacement varies from zero at the pivot to a maximum at the opposite end.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A normal dip-slip fault dips at an angle $\theta = 60^\circ$. Geological mapping shows a total net dip-slip $S = 200\text{ m}$. Calculate the vertical throw ($h$) and horizontal heave ($l$) of the fault.
    ✔ Worked Answer: Throw $h = 173.2\text{ m}$; Heave $l = 100.0\text{ m}$.
    Calculation:
    $$\text{Throw } h = S \cdot \sin(60^\circ) = 200 \times 0.8660 = 173.2\text{ m}$$
    $$\text{Heave } l = S \cdot \cos(60^\circ) = 200 \times 0.5000 = 100.0\text{ m}$$
  • Practice Q2: On a vertical fault plane ($\text{dip} = 90^\circ$), if the hanging wall moves purely vertically downward, is the fault classified as a dip-slip fault?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Yes; movement is strictly parallel to the vertical dip line.
    Explanation: Because the slip vector is aligned with the vertical dip vector, it is kinematically a dip-slip fault with $90^\circ$ throw and zero heave.
  • Practice Q3: What field diagnostic feature on a fault surface directly proves that slip was purely dip-slip rather than strike-slip?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Slickenlines (striations) and mineral step fibres that plunge down the steepest line of dip with a rake of $90^\circ$.
    Explanation: Slickenlines record the instantaneous velocity vector of fault movement; a rake of $90^\circ$ demonstrates pure dip-slip kinematics.
  • Practice Q4: In oil exploration, what is the geometric difference between stratigraphic throw and vertical throw across a dip-slip fault?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Vertical throw is measured vertically; stratigraphic throw is measured perpendicular to bedding planes.
    Explanation: Stratigraphic throw ($\text{ST} = \text{Throw} \cdot \cos\delta \pm \text{Heave} \cdot \sin\delta$) directly determines the thickness of missing or repeated strata in a well.
  • Practice Q5: How does an oblique-slip fault differ from a pure dip-slip fault in terms of net slip formulation?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Net slip requires vector summation of both dip-slip ($S_d$) and strike-slip ($S_s$) components: $S_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{S_d^2 + S_s^2}$.
    Explanation: For pure dip-slip faults, $S_s = 0$, so $S_{\text{net}} = S_d$.
  • Practice Q6: What term describes a strike-slip fault that terminates into a series of imbricate contractional thrust faults at its end?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A horse-tailing termination or transpressional flower structure (positive flower structure).
    Explanation: Lateral strike-slip motion decelerates by transferring displacement into contractional thrust splays.
  • Practice Q7: What is a growth fault, and what kinematic class does it belong to?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A syn-sedimentary normal dip-slip fault where sediment thickness increases abruptly across the fault into the downthrown hanging wall.
    Explanation: Growth faults slip continuously during deltaic sedimentation (e.g., Gulf of Mexico and Niger Delta), trapping thick hydrocarbon reservoirs.
  • Practice Q8: Why does drilling through a normal dip-slip fault result in a "missing section" of strata in a vertical wellbore?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because horizontal crustal extension (heave) pulls the stratigraphic layers apart, creating a horizontal gap.
    Explanation: Normal fault heave separates strata laterally; a vertical wellbore passing through the fault plane passes from hanging wall into footwall, missing the intervening stratigraphic section.
