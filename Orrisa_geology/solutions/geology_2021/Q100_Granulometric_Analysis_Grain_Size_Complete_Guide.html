1. Definition, Etymology & Experimental Framework of Granulometric Analysis

In physical sedimentology, soil mechanics, and sedimentary petrology (Boggs, 2014, pp. 55–75; Folk & Ward, 1957; Bangar, p. 67), granulometric analysis (also termed granulometry, mechanical grain-size analysis, or particle-size distribution analysis) is the rigorous scientific procedure used to quantitatively determine the size frequency distribution and weight percentage of individual particle-size fractions comprising a sediment or disaggregated clastic rock sample.

The term is derived from the Latin granulum ("small grain or seed") and the Greek metron ("measure"). It provides the primary quantitative baseline for reconstructing ancient hydrodynamic transport mechanisms, depositional energy regimes, paleocurrent dynamics, and geotechnical engineering classifications.

Standard Laboratory Methodologies in Granulometric Analysis

Because sedimentary particles span a gigantic physical size range—from boulders ($> 256\text{ mm}$) to sub-micron colloidal clays ($< 0.001\text{ mm}$)—no single analytical instrument can measure the entire spectrum. Granulometric analysis employs integrated complementary methods:

  • Sieve Analysis (Dry and Wet Sieving): Applied to the coarse sand and gravel fraction ($> 0.0625\text{ mm}$ or $< 4\phi$). A known dry mass of disaggregated sediment is shaken through a nested stack of standardized brass wire-mesh sieves (U.S. Standard / ASTM / Tyler mesh) arranged in order of decreasing aperture on a mechanical Ro-Tap shaker. The sediment retained on each sieve is weighed to $0.01\text{ g}$ precision.
  • Sedimentation Analysis (Pipette and Hydrometer Methods): Applied to the fine silt and clay fraction ($< 0.0625\text{ mm}$ or $> 4\phi$). Governed by Stokes' Law, which dictates the terminal settling velocity ($v$) of spherical particles through a viscous fluid: $$v = \frac{(\rho_s - \rho_f) g d^2}{18 \mu}$$ where $\rho_s$ is particle density ($2.65\text{ g/cm}^3$ for quartz), $\rho_f$ is fluid density, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity of water, and $d$ is particle diameter. By withdrawing aliquots at fixed depths and times (pipette method) or measuring suspension density (hydrometer), particle diameters are calculated.
  • Modern Automated Optical Methods: Laser diffraction particle size analyzers (Fraunhofer diffraction / Mie scattering), digital dynamic image analyzers, and automated settling tubes.
2. Folk and Ward (1957) Graphic Statistical Parameters & Environmental Signatures

Mathematical Derivation of Graphic Percentile Metrics

The data obtained from granulometric analysis are plotted as a cumulative frequency curve on semi-logarithmic or probability paper, from which critical phi-percentiles ($\phi = -\log_2 d_{\text{mm}}$) are extracted:

Statistical Parameter Folk & Ward (1957) Formula Physical & Geological Meaning
Graphic Mean ($M_z$) $$M_z = \frac{\phi_{16} + \phi_{50} + \phi_{84}}{3}$$ Overall average grain size of the sediment; reflects peak hydrodynamic competence of the depositing fluid.
Inclusive Graphic Standard Deviation ($\sigma_I$) $$\sigma_I = \frac{\phi_{84} - \phi_{16}}{4} + \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{6.6}$$ Sorting (uniformity of grain sizes); reflects the constancy and efficiency of fluid velocity fluctuations.
Inclusive Graphic Skewness ($Sk_I$) $$Sk_I = \frac{\phi_{16} + \phi_{84} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{84} - \phi_{16})} + \frac{\phi_5 + \phi_{95} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{95} - \phi_5)}$$ Asymmetry of the distribution. Positive ($> 0$): excess fine tail (river channels). Negative ($< 0$): excess coarse tail (beach surf zones winnowed of fines).
Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$) $$K_G = \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{2.44(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})}$$ Peakedness of the frequency curve relative to normal Gaussian bell curve ($K_G = 1.0$ is Mesokurtic). Measures mixing of bimodalities.

Folk's Sorting Classification Scale ($\sigma_I$)

$\sigma_I < 0.35\phi$: Very well sorted | $0.35\text{--}0.50\phi$: Well sorted | $0.50\text{--}0.71\phi$: Moderately well sorted | $0.71\text{--}1.00\phi$: Moderately sorted | $1.00\text{--}2.00\phi$: Poorly sorted | $> 2.00\phi$: Very poorly sorted.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 100: An analysis that helps to determine grain size and percentage of the individual size fractions in the specific sediment sample is called :
(A) Granulometric analysis
(B) Gravimetric analysis
(C) Sedimentation analysis
(D) Both (A) and (B) are correct
Verified Correct Answer: (A) Granulometric analysis
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Confirmed — Granulometric Analysis

Correct: By universal scientific, engineering, and sedimentological definition (Boggs, 2014, p. 55; Bangar, p. 67; Folk, 1980), granulometric analysis is the exact and comprehensive geological term for the laboratory determination of particle sizes and the relative weight percentages of individual size fractions in a sediment or soil sample. It encompasses all grain sizes (gravel, sand, silt, and clay) across both sieving and sedimentation procedures.

Option (B) Refuted — Gravimetric Analysis

Incorrect: Gravimetric analysis is a classical quantitative analytical chemistry technique that determines the mass or concentration of an analyte based on measuring the mass of a solid precipitate (e.g., precipitating dissolved sulfate as insoluble barium sulfate $\text{BaSO}_4$ and weighing it). Gravimetric analysis measures chemical elemental mass, not the physical grain-size distribution or size fractions of sediment particles.

Option (C) Refuted — Sedimentation Analysis

Incorrect: While sedimentation analysis (pipette/hydrometer method based on Stokes' Law) is a recognized laboratory technique, it is strictly one specific sub-method restricted exclusively to measuring the fine silt-and-clay fraction ($< 0.0625\text{ mm}$). It cannot determine the grain size of gravels or coarse sands, which settle too rapidly for laminar Stokes' flow. Therefore, it does not represent the overarching, comprehensive analysis for the entire sediment sample; that universal discipline is granulometric analysis.

Option (D) Refuted — Both (A) and (B) are Correct

Incorrect: Because gravimetric analysis is a chemical technique having nothing to do with determining physical grain-size distributions, option (D) is entirely fallacious. The single, unequivocally verified correct answer is (A) Granulometric analysis.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A $200.0\text{ g}$ dry sediment sample is processed through a granulometric sieve stack. The weights retained are: Gravel ($> 2\text{ mm}$) = $20.0\text{ g}$; Sand ($2.0\text{ to } 0.0625\text{ mm}$) = $150.0\text{ g}$; Mud ($< 0.0625\text{ mm}$) in pan = $30.0\text{ g}$. Calculate the percentage fractions and classify the sediment on a Folk (1954) ternary diagram.

Solution: - Gravel fraction: $\frac{20.0}{200.0} \times 100 = 10.0\%$ - Sand fraction: $\frac{150.0}{200.0} \times 100 = 75.0\%$ - Mud fraction: $\frac{30.0}{200.0} \times 100 = 15.0\%$ - Sand : Mud ratio = $75 : 15 = 5 : 1$. On Folk's Gravel-Sand-Mud (G-S-M) ternary classification diagram, a sediment containing between $5\%\text{ and } 30\%$ gravel with a sand-to-mud ratio $> 9:1$ is a "gravelly sand", but here the sand:mud ratio is $5:1$ (between $1:1$ and $9:1$), classifying it formally as a gravelly muddy sand.

Practice Problem 2

From the cumulative frequency curve of an estuarine sand sample, the following phi-percentile values are read: $\phi_5 = 1.2$, $\phi_{16} = 1.8$, $\phi_{50} = 2.5$, $\phi_{84} = 3.2$, $\phi_{95} = 4.0$. Calculate Folk and Ward's Inclusive Graphic Standard Deviation ($\sigma_I$) and interpret the sorting.

Solution: Using Folk and Ward's formula: $$\sigma_I = \frac{\phi_{84} - \phi_{16}}{4} + \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{6.6}$$ $$\sigma_I = \frac{3.2 - 1.8}{4} + \frac{4.0 - 1.2}{6.6} = \frac{1.4}{4} + \frac{2.8}{6.6} = 0.350 + 0.424 = 0.774\phi$$ According to Folk's sorting scale, $\sigma_I$ between $0.71\phi$ and $1.00\phi$ is classified as Moderately Sorted.

Practice Problem 3

Using the percentile values from Problem 2, calculate Folk and Ward's Inclusive Graphic Skewness ($Sk_I$) and interpret its physical meaning.

Solution: The formula for Inclusive Graphic Skewness is: $$Sk_I = \frac{\phi_{16} + \phi_{84} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{84} - \phi_{16})} + \frac{\phi_5 + \phi_{95} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{95} - \phi_5)}$$ Substituting values: $$Sk_I = \frac{1.8 + 3.2 - 2(2.5)}{2(3.2 - 1.8)} + \frac{1.2 + 4.0 - 2(2.5)}{2(4.0 - 1.2)}$$ $$Sk_I = \frac{5.0 - 5.0}{2(1.4)} + \frac{5.2 - 5.0}{2(2.8)} = 0 + \frac{0.2}{5.6} \approx +0.036$$ A skewness between $-0.10$ and $+0.10$ is classified as Near-Symmetrical, indicating that the grain size distribution closely approximates a normal, symmetrical Gaussian bell curve without an excessive tail of coarse or fine grains.

Practice Problem 4

Calculate the terminal settling velocity ($v$) of a quartz sphere ($d = 0.02\text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}$, fine silt) falling through water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ according to Stokes' Law. Given: $\rho_s = 2650\text{ kg/m}^3$, $\rho_f = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$, dynamic viscosity of water $\mu = 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.

Solution: Stokes' Law formula: $$v = \frac{(\rho_s - \rho_f) g d^2}{18 \mu}$$ Substituting values: $$v = \frac{(2650 - 1000) \times 9.81 \times (2 \times 10^{-5})^2}{18 \times (1.002 \times 10^{-3})}$$ $$v = \frac{1650 \times 9.81 \times 4 \times 10^{-10}}{1.8036 \times 10^{-2}} = \frac{6.4746 \times 10^{-6}}{1.8036 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 3.59 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m/s} = 0.359\text{ mm/s}$$ The fine silt grain settles at approximately $0.36\text{ mm/s}$ (or $1.29\text{ m/hour}$).

Practice Problem 5

Why does Stokes' Law fail when applied to coarse sand particles ($d > 0.2\text{ mm}$) settling in water?

Solution: Stokes' Law strictly assumes pure laminar flow around the settling sphere with a Reynolds number: $$Re = \frac{\rho_f v d}{\mu} < 0.5\text{ to } 1.0$$ For particles coarser than $\sim 0.1\text{--}0.2\text{ mm}$, settling velocities are sufficiently high that flow separation occurs behind the particle, shedding turbulent vortices and creating form drag (inertial drag) that exceeds viscous skin friction. Under turbulent drag, settling velocity becomes proportional to $\sqrt{d}$ rather than $d^2$, rendering Stokes' Law invalid.

Practice Problem 6

What is a Passega C-M diagram in sedimentology and how are the parameters $C$ and $M$ obtained from granulometric analysis?

Solution: A Passega C-M diagram (Passega, 1957, 1964) is a bivariate plot used to determine the hydrodynamic mechanism of sediment deposition (graded suspension, uniform suspension, rolled traction, or pelagic settling). The parameters are: - $C$ (One-percentile grain size, $\phi_1$ in microns): Represents the coarsest fraction transported by the current, monitoring peak turbulence competence. - $M$ (Median grain size, $\phi_{50}$ in microns): Represents the average hydrodynamic energy of the depositing fluid.

Practice Problem 7

Why do beach foreshore sands characteristically exhibit negative skewness ($Sk_I < 0$), whereas river channel sands exhibit positive skewness ($Sk_I > 0$)?

Solution: - Beach Foreshore Sands ($Sk_I < 0$, Negative / Coarse-skewed): Continuous swash and backwash wave currents relentlessly winnow away fine silt and clay particles into offshore deep water, leaving a lag of coarse particles (excess coarse tail, negative phi skewness). - River Channel Sands ($Sk_I > 0$, Positive / Fine-skewed): Fluvial river currents experience periodic drops in discharge and velocity, causing fine suspension mud and silt to settle into intergranular pore spaces among bedload sand grains, adding an excess tail of fine grains (positive phi skewness).

Practice Problem 8

Explain why the Wentworth phi ($\phi$) logarithmic transformation ($\phi = -\log_2 d_{\text{mm}}$) is universally preferred over metric millimeter scales in sedimentary granulometry.

Solution: The Wentworth phi transformation provides two crucial advantages: 1. Natural Logarithmic Scaling: Natural sediments cover several orders of magnitude in diameter ($0.001\text{ mm to } 1000\text{ mm}$). Arithmetic millimeter plots severely bunch together the fine sand, silt, and clay fractions. Using a $\log_2$ scale expands these fine intervals equally. 2. Gaussian Normalization: Most natural hydrodynamic sorting processes generate grain-size distributions that follow a log-normal distribution. Transforming grain diameter to $-\log_2 d$ converts a skewed log-normal curve into a symmetrical Gaussian bell curve, enabling straightforward statistical analysis (mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis).
