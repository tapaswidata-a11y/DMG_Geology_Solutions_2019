  • (B) The instrument measures the distance of rebound of a controlled impact on a rock surface.
  • (C) The distance of rebound (R) gives an absolute measure of surface hardness or strength.

2. Detailed Geological Masterclass: The Schmidt Rebound Hammer (ISRM Standards)

The Schmidt Hammer (Type L or Type N) is a portable, non-destructive mechanical apparatus widely employed in engineering geology and geotechnical site characterization:

  • Operating Principle: A spring-loaded mass impacts a plunger pressed against the rock or concrete surface. Upon impact, the mass rebounds. The rebound distance ($R$) is recorded on a linear scale from 10 to 100 (Statements A and B are TRUE).
  • Relative Empirical Index (NOT an Absolute Measure):
    • The rebound value $R$ does NOT provide an absolute measure of rock strength or surface hardness (Statement C is FALSE, hence the correct answer).
    • $R$ is purely an empirical, dimensionless relative index. To deduce compressive strength or modulus, $R$ must be multiplied by rock bulk density and referenced against empirical correlation charts (e.g., Deere & Miller, 1966).
    • $R$ is highly sensitive to surface roughness, weathering rinds, moisture content, hammer inclination (vertical, horizontal, overhead), and core specimen clamping.
  • Correlations: Extensive empirical studies (ISRM, Barton & Choubey) confirm that properly calibrated Schmidt rebound values correlate strongly with Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) and dynamic Young's Modulus (Statement D is TRUE).

3. Critical Option Elimination & Exam Traps

  • Option C is the incorrect statement (hence correct answer): Rebound $R$ is an empirical relative index, never an "absolute measure".
  • Option A is true: Originally invented by Ernst Schmidt in 1948 for non-destructive concrete testing.
  • Option B is true: Measures rebound travel distance of the spring-driven hammer mass.
  • Option D is true: Widely verified correlations exist for UCS and modulus estimation.
