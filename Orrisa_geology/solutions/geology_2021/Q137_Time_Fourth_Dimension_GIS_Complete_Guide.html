1. Conceptual Foundations: Introducing the Fourth Dimension (Time) into GIS

In Geographic Information Science (GIScience), digital geoinformatics, and dynamic earth system modeling, Time ($T$) is universally recognized and designated as the Fourth Dimension in GIS (Worboys & Duckham, 2004; Langran, 1992; Bangar, p. 222).

Figure 137.1 — Spatio-Temporal Data Architecture (4D GIS): Synthesis of 3-dimensional spatial coordinate geometry $(X, Y, Z)$ with the continuous chronological axis $(T)$, forming the 4D hypercube $(X, Y, Z, T)$ necessary to model dynamic geological processes such as seismic rupture, groundwater plume advection, and open-pit mine excavation progression.

The Evolution from Static 2D/3D Maps to Dynamic 4D Systems

Traditional GIS architectures were fundamentally static:

  • 2D Spatial GIS: Modeled geographic space using horizontal planar Cartesian coordinates $(X, Y)$ (Easting, Northing).
  • 3D Spatial GIS: Integrated topographic elevation or subterranean depth $(Z)$, yielding $(X, Y, Z)$ spatial coordinates.
  • 4D Spatio-Temporal GIS: Integrates chronological Time ($T$), formulating every real-world phenomenon as a multi-dimensional function:
    $$\text{Geographic State } S = f(X, Y, Z, T)$$

Because Earth is a dynamic, continuously evolving planet, static snapshots fail to capture rates of change, causality, or cyclical periodicity. Integrating Time empowers GIS to analyze not only "where" something is, but "when" it occurred, "how fast" it changes, and "where" it will migrate in the future.

2. Dynamic Geoscience Applications Requiring 4-Dimensional Spatio-Temporal GIS

In geology, hydrogeology, and geophysics, incorporating the temporal dimension ($T$) is mandatory for modeling non-stationary processes:

  • Earthquake Seismology & Fault Rupture: Propagating seismic shear and compressional wavefronts away from a hypocenter $(X_0, Y_0, Z_0)$ at origin time $T_0$, tracking stress redistribution along fault segments over milliseconds to centuries.
  • Hydrogeology & Contaminant Transport: Advective-dispersive migration of toxic industrial leachate or heavy metals through an unconfined sandy aquifer over decades:
    $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = D_x \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial X^2} - v_x \frac{\partial C}{\partial X}$$
  • Open-Pit Mine Excavation Progression: Tracking the monthly and annual removal of overburden benches, bench volume displacement, and progressive tailings dam sedimentation.
  • Geomorphological Evolution: Quantifying coastal cliff retreat rates, deltaic progradation, and landslide creep displacement via multi-temporal satellite interferometry (InSAR).
  • Volcanology: Real-time mapping of lava flow fronts, volcanic ash cloud dispersion trajectories in the atmosphere, and ground inflation/deflation recorded by continuous GNSS networks.
3. Spatio-Temporal Data Models: Snapshot vs. Space-Time Composite

Architectures for Encoding Time in GIS Databases

Spatio-Temporal Model Structural Architecture How Time is Represented Advantages & Limitations
Snapshot Model Series of independent discrete map layers for each timestamp ($T_1, T_2, T_3$). External layer timestamp; complete state recorded at each time slice. Simple to implement; massive data redundancy because unchanging features are repeatedly duplicated.
Space-Time Composite A single decomposed polygonal mesh where overlapping historical boundaries create new polygon fragments. Each fragmented polygon carries an attribute history matrix ($T_{\text{start}}, T_{\text{end}}$). Zero data redundancy; tracks exact boundary changes; polygon count explodes over long time horizons.
Event-Based Model (ESTDM) Base map at $T_0$ plus a sequential transaction ledger recording only spatial change events. Time attached directly to discrete change events ($\Delta E_i$). Highly compact storage; requires chronological forward/backward playback to reconstruct state at any arbitrary time.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 137
Which of the following might be considered the fourth dimension in GIS ?
  • (A) Space
  • (B) Scale
  • (C) Time
  • (D) Location
Correct Answer: (C) Time — In GIScience and spatio-temporal modeling, Time ($T$) is universally recognized as the fourth dimension, complementing the three spatial dimensions $(X, Y, Z)$ to form 4D spatio-temporal systems $(X, Y, Z, T)$.
Forensic Analysis of Options
Option (A): Space — Already Accounts for Dimensions 1, 2, and 3

"Space" refers to physical geometric extent. In GIS, space is fully described by the three primary spatial dimensions: $X$ (Easting / Longitude), $Y$ (Northing / Latitude), and $Z$ (Elevation / Depth). Space cannot be the "fourth" dimension because it already constitutes the first three dimensions.

Option (B): Scale — A Dimensionless Ratio, Not a Coordinate Dimension

Scale is a dimensionless mathematical ratio ($d_m / D_g$) or representative fraction expressing the proportion between map distance and ground distance. It is an operational parameter of display and cartographic generalization, not an independent physical coordinate axis.

Option (C): Time — Fully Verified & Universal GIScience Standard

In Geographic Information Systems literature (Bangar, p. 222; Worboys & Duckham, Ch. 6; Langran):

  1. The first two dimensions are horizontal planar coordinates $(X, Y)$.
  2. The third dimension is vertical height or subterranean depth $(Z)$.
  3. The fourth dimension is chronological Time ($T$), enabling multi-temporal change detection, kinematics, and predictive time-series simulation.

Therefore, Time is universally designated as the fourth dimension in GIS.

Option (D): Location — Synonymous with Spatial Coordinates

"Location" is the geographical position of an entity, defined specifically by its spatial coordinates $(X, Y)$ or $(X, Y, Z)$. It is a synonym for spatial position, not an additional fourth dimension.

Problem 1: 4D Contaminant Plume Advection Distance Over Time
A toxic chemical leak from a tailings pond enters a sandy unconfined aquifer at time $T_0 = 0$ at coordinates $(X_0 = 1000\text{ m}, Y_0 = 2000\text{ m})$. The regional groundwater seepage velocity is $v_x = 0.40\text{ m/day}$ along the $+X$ axis and $v_y = 0.10\text{ m/day}$ along the $+Y$ axis. Calculate: (a) the 4D spatio-temporal position $(X, Y, T)$ of the plume center after $T = 365\text{ days}$, and (b) the total linear advection distance.
Solution:
1. Calculate displacement along $X$ and $Y$ at $T = 365\text{ days}$: $$\Delta X = v_x \times T = 0.40\text{ m/day} \times 365\text{ days} = 146.0\text{ meters}$$ $$\Delta Y = v_y \times T = 0.10\text{ m/day} \times 365\text{ days} = 36.5\text{ meters}$$ 2. Compute coordinates at $T = 365\text{ days}$: $$X(365) = X_0 + \Delta X = 1000 + 146.0 = 1146.0\text{ m}$$ $$Y(365) = Y_0 + \Delta Y = 2000 + 36.5 = 2036.5\text{ m}$$ $$\text{4D State: } (X = 1146.0\text{ m}, Y = 2036.5\text{ m}, Z = -15.0\text{ m}, T = 365\text{ days})$$ 3. Total linear advection distance: $$D = \sqrt{\Delta X^2 + \Delta Y^2} = \sqrt{(146.0)^2 + (36.5)^2} = \sqrt{21,316 + 1332.25} = \sqrt{22,648.25} \approx 150.49\text{ meters}$$
Problem 2: Geomorphological Shoreline Erosion Rate in Spatio-Temporal GIS
In a coastal GIS study of the Odisha coastline (Chandipur-Puri sector), a high-water shoreline polyline was digitized from satellite imagery at two distinct epochs: $T_1 = \text{January 2000}$, and $T_2 = \text{January 2020}$ ($\Delta T = 20.0\text{ years}$). At a monitoring transect, the shoreline retreated inland by $\Delta s = 140.0\text{ meters}$. Calculate: (a) the annual coastal retreat rate ($v_{\text{erosion}}$), and (b) predict the total landward retreat by the year $2050$ assuming linear steady-state erosion.
Solution:
1. Annual coastal retreat rate: $$v_{\text{erosion}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta T} = \frac{140.0\text{ m}}{20.0\text{ years}} = 7.0\text{ meters/year}$$ 2. Projected retreat from 2020 to 2050 ($\Delta T = 30.0\text{ years}$): $$\Delta s_{\text{future}} = v_{\text{erosion}} \times 30.0\text{ years} = 7.0\text{ m/year} \times 30.0\text{ years} = 210.0\text{ meters}$$
Problem 3: InSAR Subsidence Velocity along Vertical Coordinate ($Z$) Over Time ($T$)
Persistent Scatterer InSAR (PS-InSAR) measures ground deformation over an abandoned coal mining field. A scatterer point at planar location $(X = 520100, Y = 2640200)$ records cumulative vertical displacements relative to datum: At $T_0 = \text{Day } 0: \Delta Z = 0.0\text{ mm}$; At $T_1 = \text{Day } 730\text{ (2.0 years)}: \Delta Z = -96.0\text{ mm}$. Calculate the average vertical subsidence rate in millimeters per year ($\text{mm/yr}$).
Solution:
1. Calculate time difference in years: $\Delta T = 2.0\text{ years}$. 2. Vertical velocity $v_z$: $$v_z = \frac{\Delta Z}{\Delta T} = \frac{-96.0\text{ mm}}{2.0\text{ years}} = -48.0\text{ mm/year}$$ The ground surface is subsiding at an active rate of $48.0\text{ mm/year}$.
Problem 4: Geological Fault Slip Rate from Paleoseismic Trenching
In a paleoseismic GIS investigation of an active strike-slip fault, radiocarbon dating ($^{14}\text{C}$) of an offset alluvial channel determines that the channel was deposited at $T = 12,000\text{ years BP}$. GIS measurement of the horizontal piercing point offset across the fault trace reveals an offset of $\Delta X = 48.0\text{ meters}$. Calculate the long-term fault slip rate in millimeters per year ($\text{mm/yr}$).
Solution:
1. Long-term slip rate: $$v_{\text{slip}} = \frac{\Delta X}{\Delta T} = \frac{48.0\text{ m}}{12,000\text{ years}} = 0.0040\text{ m/year}$$ 2. Convert to millimeters per year: $$v_{\text{slip}} = 0.0040 \times 1000\text{ mm/yr} = 4.0\text{ mm/year}$$ The active strike-slip fault operates at a rate of $4.0\text{ mm/year}$.
Problem 5: 4D Open-Pit Mine Excavation Volume Rate Calculation
Sequential drone photogrammetric DEMs are captured over an open-cast iron ore mine at two survey epochs: Survey 1: March 31, 2021; Survey 2: September 30, 2021 ($\Delta T = 6\text{ months} = 183\text{ days}$). Volumetric DEM surface subtraction ($\text{DEM}_1 - \text{DEM}_2$) calculates a net excavated pit volume of $\Delta V = 1,464,000\text{ m}^3$. Calculate the average daily extraction rate in cubic meters per day ($\text{m}^3\text{/day}$).
Solution:
1. Daily extraction rate: $$\text{Rate} = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = \frac{1,464,000\text{ m}^3}{183\text{ days}} = 8000.0\text{ m}^3/\text{day}$$
Problem 6: Space-Time Cube Voxel Resolution
A 4D Spatio-Temporal Voxel Cube is generated to model atmospheric sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) gas plumes emanating from a volcanic caldera. The spatial domain is $20\text{ km} \times 20\text{ km} \times 5\text{ km}$ ($X \times Y \times Z$) with spatial voxel size $100\text{ m} \times 100\text{ m} \times 50\text{ m}$. The model runs for a time span of $24\text{ hours}$ with a temporal step of $\Delta T = 15\text{ minutes}$. Calculate the total number of 4D space-time voxels in the simulation.
Solution:
1. Spatial voxel count: $$N_x = \frac{20,000\text{ m}}{100\text{ m}} = 200, \quad N_y = \frac{20,000\text{ m}}{100\text{ m}} = 200, \quad N_z = \frac{5,000\text{ m}}{50\text{ m}} = 100$$ $$N_{\text{spatial}} = 200 \times 200 \times 100 = 4,000,000\text{ voxels}$$ 2. Temporal steps ($24\text{ hours} = 1440\text{ minutes}$): $$N_t = \frac{1440\text{ min}}{15\text{ min}} = 96\text{ time slices}$$ 3. Total 4D space-time voxels: $$N_{\text{4D}} = N_{\text{spatial}} \times N_t = 4,000,000 \times 96 = 384,000,000\text{ voxels (384 Million)}$$
Problem 7: Space-Time Composite Polygon Fragmentation
A forest parcel is initially a single unified polygon at $T_0$. At $T_1$, a new highway bisects the polygon into 2 pieces. At $T_2$, a powerline easement crosses the parcel perpendicular to the highway, dividing each piece into 2 again. At $T_3$, a commercial quarry is excised from one quadrant. How many space-time composite child polygons exist at $T_3$?
Solution:
1. At $T_0$: $1\text{ polygon}$. 2. At $T_1$: Bisection yields $2\text{ polygons}$. 3. At $T_2$: Perpendicular cross yields $2 \times 2 = 4\text{ polygons}$. 4. At $T_3$: Excising an interior quarry polygon divides one quadrant into two (quarry boundary + remaining doughnut/parcel) → $3 + 2 = 5\text{ composite polygons}$. Each child polygon carries the complete chronological history of changes from $T_0$ to $T_3$.
Problem 8: Seismic Rupture Wavefront Velocity
An earthquake epicenter is located at $(X_0, Y_0)$ with origin time $T_0 = 10:14:00.0\text{ UTC}$. A seismological GIS network detects the first P-wave arrival at three automated stations: Station 1 ($D_1 = 30.0\text{ km}$): $T_1 = 10:14:05.0\text{ UTC}$ ($\Delta t_1 = 5.0\text{ s}$); Station 2 ($D_2 = 72.0\text{ km}$): $T_2 = 10:14:12.0\text{ UTC}$ ($\Delta t_2 = 12.0\text{ s}$); Station 3 ($D_3 = 120.0\text{ km}$): $T_3 = 10:14:20.0\text{ UTC}$ ($\Delta t_3 = 20.0\text{ s}$). Calculate the apparent crustal P-wave velocity ($V_p$) in $\text{km/s}$.
Solution:
1. Calculate velocity for each station: $$V_{p1} = \frac{30.0\text{ km}}{5.0\text{ s}} = 6.0\text{ km/s}$$ $$V_{p2} = \frac{72.0\text{ km}}{12.0\text{ s}} = 6.0\text{ km/s}$$ $$V_{p3} = \frac{120.0\text{ km}}{20.0\text{ s}} = 6.0\text{ km/s}$$ The crustal P-wave velocity is uniform at $V_p = 6.0\text{ km/s}$.
