1. The Origin of Diapsid Reptiles: The Araeoscelidia Clade

The amniote class Reptilia is divided into major evolutionary lineages based on the architecture of the temporal region of the skull. The clade Diapsida—which gave rise to lizards, snakes, sphenodontians, crocodilians, pterosaurs, dinosaurs, and birds—is defined by the presence of two temporal openings (fenestrae) on each side of the skull behind the orbit (a superior/upper temporal fenestra and an inferior/lower temporal fenestra):

  • Petrolacosaurus kansensis (Oldest Known Diapsid): Discovered in the Late Carboniferous (Pennsylvanian, Gzhelian, $\sim 305\text{ Ma}$) Garnett quarry of Kansas, USA, by H.H. Lane (described in detail by Robert R. Reisz in 1981). It is an agile, lizard-like carnivore ($\sim 40\text{ cm}$ long) with large eyes, slender gracile limbs, an elongated neck, and unequivocal upper and lower temporal fenestrae.
  • The Sister Genus Araeoscelis: The closest known relative of Petrolacosaurus in global paleontology is Araeoscelis (from the Early Permian Red Beds of Texas, described by S.W. Williston in 1910).
  • The Order Araeoscelidia: Together, Petrolacosaurus and Araeoscelis constitute the stem group Araeoscelidia (or Araeoscelida)—the most primitive, basal sister clade to all other diapsids (Eosuchia / Neodiapsida).
Figure 64.1 (Reisz 1981 / Benton 2014): Comparative skeletal anatomy of basal diapsids: Petrolacosaurus (Late Carboniferous) and its closest relative Araeoscelis (Early Permian), illustrating the elongate cervical vertebrae and gracile limbs.
2. Comparative Anatomy: Petrolacosaurus vs. Araeoscelis

While sharing a close phylogenetic sister-group relationship, Araeoscelis displays specialized dietary adaptations compared to the generalized insectivorous Petrolacosaurus:

The Basal Diapsid Sister Relationship
$$\mathbf{\text{Clade Araeoscelidia:}} \quad \mathbf{Petrolacosaurus} \text{ (Carboniferous)} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbf{Araeoscelis} \text{ (Permian)}$$
  • Skull Fenestration Evolution: Petrolacosaurus preserves the primitive complete diapsid condition with both upper and lower temporal openings wide open. In Araeoscelis, while the upper temporal fenestra remains open, the lower temporal opening has closed secondarily by expansion of the squamosal and jugal bones (euryapsid-like condition) to accommodate massive jaw-closing adductor musculature.
  • Dentition: Petrolacosaurus has slender, pointed teeth and two caniniform fangs for capturing active insects. Araeoscelis has broad, blunt, bulbous crushing teeth adapted for hard-shelled invertebrates (durophagy).
  • Postcranial Skeleton: Both genera possess uniquely elongated cervical vertebrae (neck vertebrae longer than dorsal vertebrae), slender humeri, and elongated tarsals.

Official Examination Question
Question 64
The closest known relative of Petrolacosaurus is —__.
(A) Sphenodon
(B) Araeoscelis
(C) Hylonomous
(D) Askeptosaurus
Verified Correct Answer: (B) Araeoscelis [The Early Permian genus Araeoscelis is the closest known sister taxon to the Late Carboniferous Petrolacosaurus, uniting within the basal diapsid clade Araeoscelidia.]
Correct Option: (B) Araeoscelis

Detailed Paleontological Forensic Analysis:

  • In vertebrate paleontology and amniote systematics (Benton 2014, Ch. 5, p. 120; Carroll 1988; Reisz 1981), Petrolacosaurus is universally classified in the Order Araeoscelidia.
  • The nominal genus and undisputed closest phylogenetic relative of Petrolacosaurus is Araeoscelis from the Early Permian of Texas.
  • Both genera share unique synapomorphies: distinct cervical vertebral elongation, gracile limb ratios, specialized astragalus-calcaneum contacts, and primitive diapsid cranial construction.
  • Therefore, Option (B) Araeoscelis is the precise, authoritative correct answer.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) Sphenodon

Detailed Refutation:

  • Sphenodon (the modern Tuatara of New Zealand) is a living rhynchocephalian diapsid belonging to the highly advanced crown-diapsid clade Lepidosauromorpha.
  • While it retains a complete primitive diapsid skull, it is separated from Petrolacosaurus by hundreds of millions of years of evolution and belongs to a completely different sub-clade.
Incorrect Distractor: (C) Hylonomous

Detailed Refutation:

  • Hylonomus lyelli (Late Carboniferous of Joggins, Nova Scotia) is famously the oldest known stem-reptile, but it is an anapsid (with a completely solid temporal skull roof lacking fenestrae).
  • It is not the closest relative of the diapsid Petrolacosaurus.
Incorrect Distractor: (D) Askeptosaurus

Detailed Refutation:

  • Askeptosaurus is an aquatic marine reptile belonging to the Order Thalattosauria (Triassic), specialized for marine swimming with an elongate snout and paddle limbs. It is entirely unrelated to Petrolacosaurus.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Amniote Phylogeny & Reptilian Skull Architecture
Problem 1: Functional Advantage of Temporal Fenestrae

What physiological and mechanical advantage did the evolution of temporal fenestrae provide to early reptiles over solid-skulled anapsids?

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Provided open window space for the bulky adductor mandibulae (jaw-closing) muscles to bulge outwards during contraction, enabling stronger, faster biting.
  2. Provided secure, thickened bony margins (crests and arches) for muscle attachment with minimum skull weight.
  3. Reduced unnecessary bone mass, creating a light, agile skull suitable for rapid predation.
Problem 2: The Amniotic Egg Adaptations

Name the four extraembryonic membranes of the amniotic egg that allowed early reptiles like Petrolacosaurus to conquer dry terrestrial environments.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Amnion: Encloses the embryo in a fluid-filled aquatic cushion, preventing desiccation.
  • Yolk Sac: Provides nutrient reserves for embryonic growth.
  • Allantois: Stores nitrogenous metabolic waste (uric acid) and acts as a respiratory organ.
  • Chorion: Outer membrane facilitating gas exchange ($\text{O}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$) across the porous shell.
Problem 3: Loss of the Lower Temporal Bar in Squamates

How did modern lizards modify the ancestral diapsid skull of Petrolacosaurus to achieve cranial kinesis?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Lizards lost the lower temporal bar (quadratojugal bone), liberating the quadrate bone to swing freely (streptostyly). This enables cranial kinesis, allowing the jaws to open extraordinarily wide and align upper and lower teeth to grasp struggling prey.

Problem 4: Geological Formation of Petrolacosaurus

In which rock unit was Petrolacosaurus discovered, and what unusual taphonomic setting preserved its delicate skeleton?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Preserved in the Rock Lake Shale Member (Stanton Formation) near Garnett, Kansas. The depositional environment was a stagnant, anoxic coastal lagoon or estuarine channel where fine muds buried complete skeletons away from scavengers and storm currents.

Problem 5: Secondary Euryapsid Condition in Araeoscelis

Why did earlier paleontologists mistakenly classify Araeoscelis as a "protorosaur" or euryapsid rather than a diapsid?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Because the lower temporal fenestra in Araeoscelis was secondarily closed by the widening of the squamosal and jugal bones, leaving only the upper temporal opening visible. Detailed preparation by Reisz (1981) proved that its postcranial skeleton and upper fenestra are identical to Petrolacosaurus, establishing it as a secondarily modified diapsid.

Problem 6: Synapsid vs. Diapsid Competition in the Permian

During the Late Carboniferous and Early Permian, which amniote lineage was the ecologically dominant terrestrial carnivore group: Diapsida or Synapsida?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Synapsida (the mammal-like reptiles / pelycosaurs such as Dimetrodon, Sphenacodon, Edaphosaurus) dominated terrestrial megafaunal niches ($>80\%$ of biomass). Early diapsids like Petrolacosaurus were minor, small-bodied, agile insectivorous components of the undergrowth, only achieving ecological dominance after the end-Permian extinction during the Triassic.

Problem 7: Distinction Between Captorhinids and Diapsids

How does the skull of a captorhinid stem-reptile (e.g., Captorhinus) differ from Petrolacosaurus?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Captorhinus possesses a heavy, completely unperforated anapsid skull without temporal openings, multiple rows of blunt crushing teeth on the jaw margins, and short, stocky, sprawling limbs, in contrast to the light, diapsid-fenestrated skull and gracile limbs of Petrolacosaurus.

Problem 8: Youngina and the Rise of Neodiapsids

What is the phylogenetic position of the Late Permian fossil Youngina capensis relative to Araeoscelidia?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Youngina (from the Karoo Basin of South Africa) is a primitive member of Neodiapsida. It represents the crownward evolutionary stage beyond the primitive Araeoscelidia, possessing the modernized skull structure that directly gave rise to archosaurs and lepidosaurs.
