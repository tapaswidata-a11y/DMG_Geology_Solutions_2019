1. Genetic Stream Systematics & Structural Guidance

The geometric layout of a river network—its drainage pattern—is primarily governed by regional rock structure, varying lithological hardness, joint/fault fracture networks, and tectonic slope. In genetic fluvial geomorphology (W.M. Davis, 1899; Zernitz, 1932), streams within a drainage basin are genetically categorized relative to structural strike and dip:

  • Consequent Streams: Initial streams whose flow course is directly determined by the original slope or tilt of a newly uplifted land surface.
  • Subsequent Streams: Streams that develop along lines of structural weakness, specifically excavating linear master strike valleys into belts of easily erodible, soft sedimentary formations (such as shale, slate, or soft marl) parallel to structural strike.
  • Resequent Streams: Tributary streams that flow down the regional structural dip slope in the same direction as original consequent streams, joining master subsequent streams at right angles ($90^\circ$).
  • Obsequent Streams: Short, steep tributary streams flowing down the escarpment (scarp slope) in the direction opposite to the regional structural dip, emptying orthogonally into subsequent strike streams.
"Trellis Pattern: In this type of drainage pattern, the master streams and their tributaries follow a system of mutually perpendicular or nearly right-angled courses. The master streams flow parallel to the regional strike of the beds (in soft rock belts), while short tributaries flow down the dip and anti-dip slopes, joining the main stream at right angles. It resembles a vine on a garden trellis and is typical of folded sedimentary belts." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 2: Geological Work of Rivers, pp. 61–62, Fig. 2.15)

2. Geometric Architecture: Strike Valleys and Orthogonal Intersections

When alternating resistant formations (quartzite, sandstone, limestone) and incompetent formations (shale, mudstone) are folded into synclines and anticlines or tilted into homoclinal ridges, differential erosion carves a series of parallel ridges and valleys:

  • Master Strike Streams: Long, prominent subsequent rivers occupy the soft-rock strike vales, running strictly parallel to one another along the regional strike line of the formations.
  • Orthogonal Dip & Scarp Tributaries: Short, parallel tributaries flow down the resistant dip slopes (resequent streams) and steep scarp faces (obsequent streams), intersecting the master strike streams at near-perfect right angles ($90^\circ$).
  • Water Gaps & Wind Gaps: Where transverse master rivers carve narrow gorges directly across resistant quartzite strike ridges, they form water gaps. If headward erosion and stream piracy divert the river into an adjacent strike valley, the dry, abandoned notch through the ridge is termed a wind gap.

3. Diagnostic Discrimination: Trellis vs. Rectangular vs. Parallel vs. Dendritic

Candidates must clearly differentiate trellis drainage from other geometric network patterns:

  • Rectangular Pattern: Characterized by sharp $90^\circ$ bends and right-angled junctions, but controlled by intersecting joint systems and faults in homogeneous crystalline rocks (e.g., jointed granites, basalts) or flat-lying sedimentary beds. It lacks parallel strike ridges and soft-rock vales.
  • Parallel Pattern: Characterized by straight, parallel streams running down uniform, steep regional slopes (e.g., young volcanic slopes, tilted coastal plains, or steep fault scarps). It lacks right-angled orthogonal tributaries.
  • Dendritic Pattern: Irregular, treelike branching with tributaries joining at acute angles ($< 90^\circ$), characteristic of structurally isotropic, homogeneous rocks or horizontally undeformed strata lacking directional control.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 8)
Q8. If a network of parallel streams is developed along strike and dip of the formations, the drainage pattern is referred to as :
(A) Parallel
(B) Rectangular
(C) Trellis  ✔
(D) Dendritic
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Trellis
A drainage pattern wherein master subsequent streams flow parallel to one another along the strike of incompetent beds and are joined orthogonally by short tributaries flowing down the dip and scarp slopes is universally designated a Trellis Drainage Pattern (Bangar, p. 61; Zernitz, 1932).
(A) Parallel Incorrect (Steep Slope Controlled, Lacks Strike-Dip Orthogonality)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A parallel drainage pattern develops on steep, elongated, uniformly dipping regional slopes or coastal plains. All streams flow in the same direction down the regional gradient without developing an orthogonal strike-and-dip tributary network.
(B) Rectangular Incorrect (Controlled by Joint/Fault Networks, Not Strike-Dip Formations)
Why this distractor is incorrect: While rectangular drainage also exhibits right-angled bends and tributary junctions, it is structurally controlled by intersecting orthogonal joint sets, cleavage fractures, or fault lines in homogeneous crystalline rocks (granites, basalts) or horizontal strata. It lacks parallel strike vales and dipping homoclinal ridges.
(C) Trellis Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: By standard geological definition, the trellis pattern is defined specifically by parallel master streams aligned along the strike of soft formations, intersected at right angles by short tributaries flowing down the structural dip and anti-dip slopes of resistant homoclinal ridges. It is the diagnostic fingerprint of folded sedimentary terranes (e.g., the Appalachian Valley and Ridge, Cuddapah Basin).
(D) Dendritic Incorrect (Irregular Insequent Branching in Isotropic Rocks)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Dendritic drainage resembles the branching veins of a leaf or tree roots, with tributaries joining at acute angles ($< 90^\circ$). It develops in structurally isotropic, homogeneous rocks or flat horizontal beds where geology exerts no directional control over stream orientation.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: What is the genetic classification of a tributary that flows down the steep escarpment face in a direction directly opposite to the regional structural dip?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Obsequent stream.
    Explanation: Consequent and resequent streams flow in the direction of structural dip; obsequent streams flow down the scarp face opposite to dip, discharging into the subsequent strike stream.
  • Practice Q2: Describe the mechanism of stream piracy (river capture) in a trellis drainage network.
    ✔ Correct Answer: A vigorous subsequent strike stream working headward in soft shale breaches a drainage divide and diverts a transverse consequent stream.
    Explanation: Rapid headward erosion along soft shale belts allows subsequent streams to intersect and capture transverse streams that are slowed down by cutting across hard quartzite ridges.
  • Practice Q3: What field diagnostic distinguishes an active water gap from an abandoned wind gap in folded mountain belts?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A water gap carries an active transverse river; a wind gap is a dry, elevated notch through a ridge containing river gravels but no running stream.
    Explanation: Wind gaps are former water gaps abandoned after stream piracy diverted the river upstream, leaving rounded fluvial gravels stranded high above the valley floor.
  • Practice Q4: In Horton-Strahler stream ordering, what is the typical Bifurcation Ratio ($R_b = N_u / N_{u+1}$) for a dendritic network versus a trellis network?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Dendritic basins typically yield $R_b \approx 3.0 - 5.0$; elongated trellis basins in folded belts often show anomalously high $R_b > 5.0 - 10.0$.
    Explanation: Trellis networks restrict lateral expansion, forcing dozens of short first-order streams to feed directly into a long master subsequent channel, inflating $R_b$.
  • Practice Q5: How does an antecedent stream maintain its course across a newly rising anticlinal ridge in a trellis belt?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The river's downcutting rate equals or exceeds the rate of tectonic fold uplift, incising a narrow transverse antecedent gorge.
    Explanation: Antecedent streams predate the folding and maintain their original transverse path across the rising ridges (e.g., Indus, Brahmaputra, and Sutlej cutting across the rising Himalayas).
  • Practice Q6: What drainage pattern develops on a deeply eroded circular structural salt dome or breached granite dome?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Annular drainage pattern.
    Explanation: Circular upturned sedimentary layers create concentric rings of hard and soft rock; subsequent streams trace circular concentric rings joined radially by short tributaries.
  • Practice Q7: A superimposed stream is defined as:
    ✔ Correct Answer: A stream whose drainage course was established on an overlying unconformable cover, which then incised vertically into older discordant structural rocks below.
    Explanation: Superimposition explains why rivers sometimes cut bizarre transverse gorges through buried folded mountain ranges without structural regard to the buried folds (e.g., Subarnarekha River in the Singhbhum shear zone).
  • Practice Q8: A deranged or disordered drainage pattern with erratic wandering channels and abundant lakes/swamps is diagnostic of which geologic history?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Recently glaciated continental shield terranes (Pleistocene ice sheet ablation).
    Explanation: Glacial erosion and morainic dumping completely disrupt pre-existing fluvial systems, creating irregular lakes, swamps, and aimlessly wandering streams (e.g., Canadian Shield and Finland).
