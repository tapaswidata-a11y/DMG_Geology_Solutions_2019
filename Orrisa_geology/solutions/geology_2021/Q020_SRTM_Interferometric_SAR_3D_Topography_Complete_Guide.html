1. The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM): Breakthrough in 3D Geodesy

In February 2000, NASA, the National Imagery and Mapping Agency (NIMA, now NGA), the German Aerospace Center (DLR), and the Italian Space Agency (ASI) conducted the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) aboard Space Shuttle Endeavour (Flight STS-99). SRTM accomplished an unprecedented geodetic triumph: acquiring a consistent, high-precision Three-Dimensional (3D) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) of Earth's land surface between latitudes $60^\circ\text{N}$ and $56^\circ\text{S}$, encompassing over $80\%$ of Earth's total landmass in just 11 days of flight.

  • Single-Pass Spaceborne Radar Interferometry (InSAR): Previous spaceborne radar interferometry (such as ERS-1/2) required "repeat-pass" orbits days or weeks apart, suffering severe decorrelation from wind, vegetation growth, and variable atmospheric water vapor. SRTM revolutionized radar geodesy by using Single-Pass Interferometry:
    • Primary Main Antenna: Mounted rigidly inside the space shuttle's payload bay.
    • Secondary Outboard Antenna: Deployed at the tip of a lightweight, rigid carbon-fiber truss mast extending 60 meters (197 feet) outward into space—representing the largest rigid human-made structure ever deployed in space!
  • Simultaneous Transmission and Reception: Radar pulses transmitted from the main antenna were backscattered from the Earth's terrain and received simultaneously by both the main and outboard antennas, freezing the atmosphere and surface in an identical instant of time.

2. Interferometric Phase Difference ($\Delta\phi$) & Height Derivation

The mathematical physics of extracting 3D ground topography ($z$) from dual-antenna radar interferometry relies on measuring the interferometric phase difference ($\Delta\phi$) between the two received microwave signals:

Interferometric Phase Difference & Elevation Derivation
$$\Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} (R_1 - R_2) \approx \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} B \sin(\theta - \alpha)$$ $$z = H - R_1 \cos\theta$$

Where $\lambda$ is radar wavelength (C-band: $5.6\text{ cm}$; X-band: $3.1\text{ cm}$), $B$ is physical baseline length ($60\text{ m}$), $\alpha$ is baseline tilt angle, $\theta$ is look angle, and $H$ is shuttle altitude ($233\text{ km}$). Measuring $\Delta\phi$ to within fractions of a wavelength enables vertical height determination ($z$) to meter-level accuracy.

SRTM produced two primary global elevation datasets:

  • SRTM-1 (1 Arc-Second): Nominal ground spatial resolution of $30\text{ meters}$ with an absolute vertical accuracy better than $\pm 16\text{ meters}$ (typically achieving $\pm 6 - 8\text{ m}$ in flat to moderate terrain).
  • SRTM-3 (3 Arc-Seconds): Averaged ground spatial resolution of $90\text{ meters}$, made globally accessible to researchers in 2003.
"The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) successfully collected interferometric radar data covering more than 80% of Earth's land surface... providing the first high-resolution, homogeneous 3D digital topographic map of the world." — T.G. Farr et al., The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (Reviews of Geophysics, 2007, 45, RG2004)
3. Refutation of Landsat, IKONOS & ERS as Primary Answers

Understanding the distinction between 2D imagery and native 3D topographic mission architectures:

  • (A) LANDSAT: A classic nadir-looking 2D multispectral optical imaging system. Landsat sensors (MSS, TM, ETM+, OLI) acquire 2D planar images of land reflectance ($x, y$ coordinates). They cannot measure vertical relief directly without external stereoscopic pairs or auxiliary DEMs.
  • (C) IKONOS: A commercial high-resolution 2D optical satellite ($0.8\text{ m}$ panchromatic, $4\text{ m}$ multispectral). While stereo pairs can be post-processed via optical photogrammetry into DEMs, IKONOS is an optical imager, not an inherently 3D topographic mission.
  • (D) ERS: ERS-1 and ERS-2 carried single radar antennas that required repeat-pass orbital passes (35 days apart) to perform interferometry, which suffered severe temporal decorrelation. SRTM was the definitive, dedicated mission created specifically to generate comprehensive 3D global topography.

Q20. Name the satellite providing three-dimensional topographic data :
(A) LANDSAT
(B) SRTM  ✔
(C) IKONOS
(D) ERS
✔
Correct Answer: (B) SRTM
The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) utilized single-pass dual-antenna radar interferometry (InSAR) aboard Space Shuttle Endeavour in 2000 to generate the world's premier high-resolution Three-Dimensional (3D) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) covering over 80% of Earth's landmass.
(A) LANDSAT Incorrect (Planar 2D Multispectral Optical Satellite)
Why this distractor is incorrect: The Landsat series (Landsat-1 through 9) consists of nadir-pointing optical sensors producing planar two-dimensional (2D) multispectral reflectance images ($x, y$). They do not record native 3D topographic elevation data ($z$).
(B) SRTM Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Flown in February 2000, SRTM was specifically engineered to map the Earth's topography in 3D. By deploying a 60-meter mast with an outboard radar antenna, it recorded single-pass interferometric phase shifts, creating the famous 30-meter and 90-meter near-global 3D Digital Elevation Models that serve as the universal topographic standard in geoscience and GIS.
(C) IKONOS Incorrect (High-Resolution 2D Optical Satellite)
Why this distractor is incorrect: IKONOS (launched in 1999) is an optical earth observation satellite designed for 2D high-resolution urban mapping and surveillance. It does not measure topographic heights natively via radar interferometry.
(D) ERS Incorrect (Repeat-Pass Radar with High Temporal Decorrelation)
Why this distractor is incorrect: The European Remote Sensing satellites (ERS-1/2) carried single-antenna Synthetic Aperture Radars that required repeat-pass orbits (35-day repeat cycles), making global 3D topographic mapping unfeasible due to atmospheric changes and vegetative decorrelation.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: What was the physical length of the deployable mast deployed from Space Shuttle Endeavour during SRTM?
    ✔ Correct Answer: 60 meters (197 feet).
    Explanation: The 60-meter mast separated the primary radar antenna inside the cargo bay from the outboard antenna, establishing the physical baseline ($B$) necessary to measure interferometric phase differences.
  • Practice Q2: What two radar microwave frequency bands were simultaneously flown on SRTM?
    ✔ Correct Answer: C-band ($\lambda = 5.6\text{ cm}$, by NASA/JPL) and X-band ($\lambda = 3.1\text{ cm}$, by DLR/ASI).
    Explanation: The C-band radar operated with ScanSAR mode to cover wide swaths ($225\text{ km}$), while the German/Italian X-band radar operated in high-resolution stripmap mode ($50\text{ km}$ swath).
  • Practice Q3: What is the difference between a Digital Surface Model (DSM) and a Digital Terrain Model (DTM), and which one does SRTM naturally produce?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A DSM includes vegetation canopy tops and buildings; a DTM represents bare ground elevation. SRTM radar reflects off the upper tree canopy and is inherently a DSM.
    Explanation: Because C-band microwaves ($\lambda = 5.6\text{ cm}$) scatter off forest foliage and tree branches, SRTM elevations in forested terrain are offset upward above bare Earth by a significant fraction of tree height.
  • Practice Q4: Why does SRTM data contain "voids" (no-data gaps) in steep mountainous terrain (e.g., the Himalayas)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Due to radar shadow and extreme layover where steep slopes face away from or directly toward the radar antenna.
    Explanation: Extreme geometric distortion prevents phase unwrapping in steep relief, creating voids that have since been filled in Version 3 (SRTM Plus) using ASTER GDEM and topographic maps.
  • Practice Q5: In radar interferometry, what is the altitude of ambiguity ($h_a$)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The vertical elevation change that produces exactly one full $2\pi$ ($360^\circ$) interferometric phase cycle ($h_a = \frac{\lambda R \sin\theta}{B_{\perp}}$).
    Explanation: Shorter baselines have larger altitude of ambiguity (easier to unwrap phase but lower height precision); longer baselines have smaller $h_a$ (higher height precision but difficult phase unwrapping).
  • Practice Q6: What mathematical process converts a cyclic, wrapped interferometric phase map ($-\pi$ to $+\pi$) into continuous, absolute terrain elevation?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Phase Unwrapping (e.g., Minimum Cost Flow or Branch-Cut algorithms).
    Explanation: Because radar phase is measured modulo $2\pi$, phase unwrapping integrates phase gradients across the interferogram to resolve the integer number of $2\pi$ phase cycles for every pixel.
  • Practice Q7: What is DInSAR (Differential InSAR), and what geophysical phenomena can it measure with millimeter-level precision?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Subtracting topographic phase from repeat-pass interferograms to measure surface crustal displacement from earthquakes, volcanic inflation, and land subsidence.
    Explanation: DInSAR eliminates stationary topography, isolating tiny sub-centimeter line-of-sight surface deformations between satellite overpasses.
  • Practice Q8: Why did SRTM not acquire topographic data over the polar ice caps of Greenland and Antarctica?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Space Shuttle *Endeavour* had an orbital inclination of $57.0^\circ$, physically limiting ground coverage to between $60^\circ\text{N}$ and $56^\circ\text{S}$ latitudes.
    Explanation: The shuttle's launch azimuth from Cape Canaveral constrained orbital inclination to $57^\circ$, leaving the high polar latitudes to be mapped later by polar satellites like CryoSat-2 and TanDEM-X.
