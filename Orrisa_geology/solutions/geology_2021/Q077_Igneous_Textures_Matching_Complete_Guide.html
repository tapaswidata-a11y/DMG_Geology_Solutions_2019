1. Crystallization Kinetics & Textural Evolution of Magmas

The texture of an igneous rock is the geometric arrangement, mutual spatial relationships, crystallinity, and grain size of its constituent mineral phases. Textural development is fundamentally controlled by the interplay of two kinetic parameters during magmatic cooling (Winter, 2014, p. 34):

  • Rate of Nucleation ($I$): The number of stable crystal nuclei formed per unit volume per unit time.
  • Rate of Crystal Growth ($Y$): The linear rate at which chemical components diffuse through the melt and attach to existing crystal faces ($m/\text{s}$).

Both rates are functions of undercooling ($\Delta T = T_{\text{liquidus}} - T_{\text{actual}}$):

  • Slow Cooling (Deep Plutonic Realm / Low $\Delta T$): Growth rate ($Y$) far exceeds nucleation rate ($I$). Very few nuclei form, but each grows to large macroscopic dimensions. This produces a phaneritic texture (crystals visible to the unaided eye, $>1\text{ mm}$), characteristic of deep-seated plutons such as Granite and Gabbro.
  • Rapid Cooling (Volcanic Realm / High $\Delta T$): Nucleation rate surges while diffusion slows, yielding microscopic crystals (aphanitic) or, under explosive quenching into air/water, freezing the liquid structure into volcanic glass (holohyaline).

2. Ophitic & Subophitic Textures: Hallmarks of Hypabyssal Dolerite

Ophitic texture (from the Greek ophis = serpent, referring to its mottled appearance) is the diagnostic petrographic signature of dolerite (diabase), the medium-grained hypabyssal intrusive equivalent of basalt that forms extensive dikes and sills worldwide.

Figure 77.2 — Petrographic Thin Section of Ophitic Texture in Dolerite: Authentic textbook diagram from K.M. Bangar (Principles of Engineering Geology, p. 166). Laths of euhedral-to-subhedral plagioclase feldspar (labradorite) are completely enclosed within large, optically continuous plates of clinopyroxene (augite).

Crystallization Mechanics of the Ophitic Texture

The formation of ophitic texture reflects cotectic crystallization along the plagioclase-clinopyroxene liquidus:

  1. Early Plagioclase Framework: In basaltic dikes, plagioclase begins crystallizing first or nucleates with high frequency, forming an open, interlocking network of slender, prismatic laths.
  2. Late Poikilitic Pyroxene Overgrowth: Clinopyroxene (augite) has a relatively high nucleation barrier in dry basaltic melts. Once nucleated, pyroxene grows rapidly, wrapping around and completely enclosing the pre-existing plagioclase laths. Under polarized light, the enclosing pyroxene extinguishes simultaneously over several square millimeters.
  3. Subophitic Transition: When the plagioclase laths are only partially enclosed by pyroxene grains, the texture is termed subophitic. When pyroxenes form small, granular grains clustered between plagioclase laths, it becomes intergranular.
3. Holocrystalline Plutonism (Gabbro) & Submarine Pillow Structures (Spillite)

Degree of Crystallinity: Holocrystalline Gabbro

Igneous rocks are divided based on the ratio of crystal to glass into:

  • Holocrystalline: Composed $100\%$ of crystalline mineral grains with zero interstitial amorphous glass. In the exam pairing, Gabbro—the coarse-grained mafic plutonic equivalent of basalt crystallized deep within oceanic crust (Layer 3) or continental layered intrusions—is the textbook archetype of a holocrystalline phaneritic rock.
  • Hypocrystalline (Merocrystalline): Composed of both crystals and interstitial glass (e.g., andesite, pitchstone).
  • Holohyaline: Composed entirely of amorphous volcanic glass ($>95\%$ glass, e.g., obsidian, tachylite).

Subaqueous Quenching & Pillow Structures in Spillites

Pillow structures are bulbous, rounded, or ellipsoidal sack-like masses (typically 0.2 to 2 m in diameter) formed when basaltic magma is extruded underwater into a marine or lacustrine basin:

  • Pillow Mechanics: Upon contact with cold seawater, a flexible, chilled glassy rind (tachylite) freezes instantly around the expanding lava lobe. Internal magmatic pressure inflates the lobe like a water balloon until it ruptures, budding into a daughter pillow. Gravity forces the lower surface to sag into underlying voids, creating a diagnostic convex upper surface and a downward-pointing V-shaped pedicle (a classic geopetal younging indicator!).
  • Spillite (Spilite): An altered, sodium-rich submarine basalt commonly exhibiting pillow structure. During extrusion on the seabed, low-temperature hydrothermal reaction with heated seawater induces spilitization, transforming calcic plagioclase into secondary albite while mafic phases alter to chlorite, actinolite, epidote, and calcite.

Problem 1: Crystal Size Distribution (CSD) Theory & Residence Time

In a porphyritic granite pluton, Crystal Size Distribution (CSD) analysis of plagioclase phenocrysts yields a linear slope $s = -0.12\text{ mm}^{-1}$ in a plot of $\ln(n)$ versus crystal size $L$. If the linear crystal growth rate is experimentally determined as $G = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\text{ mm/s}$, calculate the average magma residence time ($\tau$) in years ($1\text{ year} \approx 3.15 \times 10^7\text{ s}$).

Step-by-Step CSD Derivation:
In steady-state CSD theory (Marsh, 1988), the population density slope $s$ is related to growth rate $G$ and residence time $\tau$ by: $$s = -\frac{1}{G \cdot \tau}$$
Rearranging for residence time $\tau$: $$\tau = -\frac{1}{G \cdot s} = \frac{1}{(1.0 \times 10^{-10}\text{ mm/s}) \times (0.12\text{ mm}^{-1})} = \frac{1}{1.2 \times 10^{-11}\text{ s}^{-1}} \approx 8.33 \times 10^{10}\text{ seconds}$$
Converting seconds to years: $$\tau = \frac{8.33 \times 10^{10}\text{ s}}{3.1536 \times 10^7\text{ s/year}} \approx \mathbf{2,642\text{ years}}$$
Interpretation: The plagioclase phenocrysts resided in the cooling granitic magma chamber for approximately 2,640 years prior to final consolidation.
Problem 2: Petrographic Discrimination of Basaltic Textures

Compare and contrast the microscopic definitions and cooling regimes of: (a) Ophitic; (b) Subophitic; (c) Intergranular; and (d) Intersertal textures.

Textural Continuum in Mafic Rocks:
1. Ophitic: Plagioclase laths are completely enclosed poikilitically within large, optically continuous clinopyroxene plates. (Slowest cooling among hypabyssal dolerites; thick sill cores).
2. Subophitic: Plagioclase laths are only partially enclosed by pyroxene crystals, with parts of the laths projecting outside. (Moderate cooling rates; medium-width dikes).
3. Intergranular: Plagioclase laths form an interlocking lattice, and the angular interstitial spaces are completely filled by small, polygonal, granular pyroxene and iron-oxide crystals without any glass. (Rapid cooling; dike margins).
4. Intersertal: The angular wedges between plagioclase laths are occupied by brown amorphous volcanic glass (tachylite) or cryptocrystalline devitrified material. (Very rapid cooling / subaqueous flow tops).
Problem 3: Chemical Stoichiometry of the Spilitization Reaction

Submarine basalts undergo hydrothermal metasomatism (spilitization) on the ocean floor. Write the balanced chemical reaction representing the albitization of an anorthite plagioclase component in the presence of dissolved sodium and silica in seawater, producing secondary calcite.

Stoichiometric Reaction:
$$\text{CaAl}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_8\ (\text{Anorthite}) + 2\text{Na}^+ + 4\text{SiO}_{2\text{ (aq)}} + \text{CO}_3^{2-} \longrightarrow 2\text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_8\ (\text{Albite}) + \text{CaCO}_3\ (\text{Calcite})$$
Geochemical Significance: Calcic plagioclase ($\text{An}_{50\text{--}70}$) is unstable in the presence of heated, circulating seawater ($T \approx 200\text{--}350^\circ\text{C}$). Seawater donates $Na^+$ while leaching $Ca^{2+}$, transforming the basalt into a spilite dominated by albite, chlorite, epidote, and calcite amygdules.
Problem 4: Geopetal Younging Criteria in Submarine Pillow Basalts

A structural geologist mapping an overturned Archean greenstone belt encounters deformed pillow lavas. Explain how the cross-sectional geometry of individual pillows indicates the original stratigraphic "way-up" (younging direction).

Geopetal Criteria:
1. Convex Upper Surface: As hot lava buds from a feeder tube, buoyancy and surface tension cause the top surface of the pillow to expand smoothly upward, forming a broad, convex-upward dome.
2. Cuspidate V-shaped Base (Pedicle): The soft, plastic bottom of the expanding pillow sags under gravity into the triangular cusp or indentation formed between the two underlying, pre-existing pillows, producing a downward-pointing tail or "keel" (V-shaped pedicle).
3. Vesicle Zonation: Gas vesicles are concentrated near the top margin of the pillow due to buoyant upward gas migration before quenching.
Deduction: The smooth rounded curve points to stratigraphic TOP (younger); the pointed V-shaped cusp points to stratigraphic BASE (older).
Problem 5: Thermal Profiling of a Dolerite Dike Chilled Margin

A vertical dolerite dike of thickness $w = 10\text{ meters}$ intrudes cold country rock ($T_0 = 20^\circ\text{C}$). The magma enters at its liquidus temperature ($T_m = 1180^\circ\text{C}$). Using Jaeger's (1968) thermal contact solution, calculate the immediate contact temperature ($T_c$) at the dike wall and describe the resulting texture.

Thermal Contact Derivation:
Assuming equivalent thermal properties (heat capacity and thermal conductivity) for the magma and host rock, the initial contact temperature ($T_c$) immediately upon intrusion is: $$T_c = \frac{T_m + T_0}{2} = \frac{1180 + 20}{2} = \frac{1200}{2} = \mathbf{600^\circ\text{C}}$$
Textural Evolution Across the Dike:
- At the immediate wall ($x = 0$): Quenched instantly from $1180^\circ\text{C}$ to $600^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T = 580^\circ\text{C}$). Extreme undercooling produces an aphanitic to glassy chilled margin (tachylite).
- At the dike interior ($x = 5\text{ m}$): Cooling is slow, controlled by heat conduction through the country rock. Low undercooling allows complete crystal growth, developing classic coarse ophitic texture.
Problem 6: Poikilitic vs. Ophitic: Terminological Demarcation

Both poikilitic and ophitic textures describe mineral grains enclosed within larger host crystals. Explain the precise mineralogical restriction that distinguishes an ophitic texture from general poikilitic texture.

Taxonomic Distinction:
- Poikilitic Texture (General Term): A generic petrographic texture where relatively small, randomly oriented crystals of any mineral phase (chadacrysts) are enclosed within a much larger host crystal of a different mineral (oikocryst). Examples: Olivine enclosed in hornblende, or quartz enclosed in biotite.
- Ophitic Texture (Specific Restrictive Variety): A specialized variety of poikilitic texture strictly restricted to mafic rocks where the enclosed chadacrysts are specifically prismatic laths of plagioclase feldspar, and the enclosing oikocryst is specifically clinopyroxene (augite).
Problem 7: Grain Size Boundaries in Crystalline Rocks

State the internationally accepted metric grain size thresholds defining: (a) Fine-grained (aphanitic); (b) Medium-grained; (c) Coarse-grained; and (d) Pegmatitic crystalline rocks according to the IUGS Subcommission on the Systematics of Igneous Rocks.

IUGS Grain Size Scales:
- Fine-grained (Aphanitic): Average grain diameter $< 1.0\text{ mm}$ (individual crystals cannot be distinguished with the naked eye; e.g., basalt, rhyolite).
- Medium-grained: Average grain diameter between $1.0\text{ mm}$ and $5.0\text{ mm}$ (typical of hypabyssal rocks; e.g., dolerite, microgranite).
- Coarse-grained (Phaneritic): Average grain diameter between $5.0\text{ mm}$ and $30.0\text{ mm}$ (typical of plutonic rocks; e.g., granite, gabbro).
- Very Coarse / Pegmatitic: Giant crystals exceeding $> 30.0\text{ mm}$ (often $> 5\text{--}10\text{ cm}$), crystallized from volatile-rich, water-saturated residual pegmatitic melts.
Problem 8: Glass Formation and the Glass Transition Temperature ($T_g$)

Why do high-silica rhyolitic magmas readily form natural glass (obsidian) upon subaerial cooling, whereas low-silica basaltic magmas rarely form pure glass except when quenched underwater?

Melt Polymerization and Rheology:
1. Polymerization and Viscosity: Rhyolitic magma ($SiO_2 \approx 72\text{--}75\text{ wt}\%$) has a fully polymerized, three-dimensional network of bridging oxygens ($NBO/T \approx 0$), resulting in an astronomical viscosity ($\eta \approx 10^7\text{ to } 10^9\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$). During subaerial cooling, high viscosity impedes the diffusion of ions to crystal growth sites, freezing the disordered liquid structure into glass even under moderate subaerial cooling rates.
2. Basaltic Depolymerization: Basaltic magma ($SiO_2 \approx 48\text{--}50\text{ wt}\%$) is rich in network-modifying cations ($Mg^{2+}, Fe^{2+}, Ca^{2+}$) with high non-bridging oxygens ($NBO/T \approx 0.6\text{--}0.8$), giving it an extremely low viscosity ($\eta \approx 10\text{--}100\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$). Rapid ionic diffusion allows crystals to nucleate and grow rapidly; pure glass can only form when basalt is violently quenched in cold water ($dT/dt > 100^\circ\text{C/s}$).
