1. Folk and Ward (1957) Formulation of Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$)

In quantitative granulometry and statistical sedimentology (Folk & Ward, 1957; Boggs, 2014, p. 75), kurtosis (from the Greek $\kappa\upsilon\rho\tau\acute{o}\varsigma$, meaning "bulging" or "convex") measures the ratio between the sorting in the tails of a grain-size frequency curve and the sorting in the central body of the distribution.

To quantify departure from a standard Gaussian normal bell curve, Robert L. Folk and William C. Ward (1957, p. 13) derived the Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$) defined explicitly by the mathematical formula:

$$\mathbf{K_G = \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{2.44(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})}}$$

where $\phi_5, \phi_{25}, \phi_{75}, \text{and } \phi_{95}$ represent the respective 5th, 25th, 75th, and 95th percentiles determined from the cumulative weight percentage frequency curve plotted on the Wentworth phi scale ($\phi = -\log_2 d_{\text{mm}}$).

Figure 101.1 — Folk and Ward (1957) Statistical Grain-Size Formulae and Kurtosis Scale: Authentic textbook table from Sam Boggs Jr. (Principles of Sedimentology and Stratigraphy, p. 75). Contains the exact mathematical definition of Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$) and its verbal classification scale.

Mathematical Derivation of the Constant $2.44$

The denominator coefficient $2.44$ is not an empirical arbitrary number, but a rigorously derived theoretical constant:

  1. For an idealized, perfectly normal Gaussian distribution:
    • The spread of the outer $90\%$ of the distribution ($\phi_{95} - \phi_5$) encompasses exactly $3.29$ standard deviations ($2 \times 1.6449\sigma \approx 3.2897\sigma$).
    • The interquartile range ($\phi_{75} - \phi_{25}$) encompasses exactly $1.35$ standard deviations ($2 \times 0.6745\sigma \approx 1.3490\sigma$).
  2. The ratio of these two percentile spreads in a standard normal curve is: $$\frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{\phi_{75} - \phi_{25}} = \frac{3.2897\sigma}{1.3490\sigma} \approx 2.4386 \approx \mathbf{2.44}$$
  3. By dividing this ratio by $2.44$, Folk and Ward normalized the formula such that a perfectly normal Gaussian grain-size distribution yields: $$K_G = \frac{2.44}{2.44} = \mathbf{1.00}\quad (\text{Mesokurtic})$$
2. Folk and Ward Verbal Kurtosis Scale & Hydrodynamic Mixing Models

Verbal Classification Scale for Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$)

Folk and Ward (1957) and Folk (1980) established the standard verbal scale for interpreting calculated $K_G$ values:

$K_G$ Range Verbal Classification Frequency Curve Geometry Hydrodynamic / Depositional Interpretation
$< 0.67$ Very Platykurtic Flat-topped, broad, bimodal saddle Equal mixing of two distinct sub-equal grain-size populations (e.g., gravel + sand).
$0.67\text{ to } 0.90$ Platykurtic Flat, broad central peak Sub-equal mixing of two populations; tails are better sorted than the center.
$0.90\text{ to } 1.11$ Mesokurtic Standard normal bell curve ($K_G \approx 1.0$) Unimodal sediment deposited under steady, unimodal hydrodynamic conditions.
$1.11\text{ to } 1.50$ Leptokurtic Excessively peaked, narrow central body Dominant unimodal population with a minor ($5\text{--}15\%$) subordinate tail of fine or coarse grains.
$1.50\text{ to } 3.00$ Very Leptokurtic Extremely sharp, narrow central spike Central $70\text{--}80\%$ is exceptionally well-sorted, with a tiny, poorly sorted tail.
$> 3.00$ Extremely Leptokurtic Hyper-peaked; needle-like central body Extreme unimodality with minute extraneous infiltration.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 101: Formulae and verbal scales for graphic size parameters are provided by Folk and Ward (1957). As per the relation, inclusive graphic kurtosis is calculated by which formula ?
(A) KG = (phi95 - phi5) / [2.44 (phi75 - phi25)]
(B) KG = (phi95 - phi5) / [2.44 (phi75 - phi95)]
(C) KG = (phi95 - phi5) / [1.44 (phi5 - phi25)]
(D) Both (A) and (B) are correct
Verified Correct Answer: (A) KG = (phi95 - phi5) / [2.44 (phi75 - phi25)]
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Confirmed — Canonical Formula

Correct: By exact mathematical definition in Folk and Ward (1957, p. 13) and Sam Boggs Jr. (Principles of Sedimentology and Stratigraphy, p. 75), the formula for Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$) is: $$K_G = \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{2.44(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})}$$ The numerator $(\phi_{95} - \phi_5)$ measures the total spread of the distribution tails ($90\%$ of the sediment), while the denominator interquartile range $(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})$ measures the spread of the central body ($50\%$ of the sediment), scaled by the normal curve constant $2.44$.

Option (B) Refuted — Erroneous Denominator Term

Incorrect: The denominator in (B) contains the term $(\phi_{75} - \phi_{95})$. This is mathematically nonsensical: because phi percentiles increase monotonically ($\phi_5 < \phi_{25} < \phi_{75} < \phi_{95}$), subtracting a larger percentile ($\phi_{95}$) from a smaller percentile ($\phi_{75}$) yields a negative value, which would result in a negative kurtosis for all normal positive sediments. Furthermore, it completely omits the 25th percentile ($\phi_{25}$).

Option (C) Refuted — Erroneous Constant & Subtraction Order

Incorrect: Option (C) substitutes an incorrect constant ($1.44$ instead of $2.44$) and inverts the subtraction $(\phi_5 - \phi_{25})$, which also produces a negative spread. It bears no mathematical or historical validity in sedimentology.

Option (D) Refuted — Fallacious Dual Option

Incorrect: Because Option (B) is mathematically and dimensionally erroneous, Option (D) is entirely invalid. The official answer key verified by the examination authorities unambiguously affirms (A).

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A river sediment sample yields the following percentile values from its cumulative frequency curve: $\phi_5 = 0.50$, $\phi_{25} = 1.60$, $\phi_{75} = 2.40$, and $\phi_{95} = 3.90$. Calculate the Graphic Kurtosis ($K_G$) and classify it according to Folk's verbal scale.

Solution: Applying Folk and Ward's formula: $$K_G = \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{2.44(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})}$$ Substituting values: $$K_G = \frac{3.90 - 0.50}{2.44(2.40 - 1.60)} = \frac{3.40}{2.44 \times 0.80} = \frac{3.40}{1.952} \approx \mathbf{1.74}$$ According to Folk's verbal scale, a $K_G$ value between $1.50$ and $3.00$ is classified as Very Leptokurtic. This indicates an excessively peaked central body with an outlying tail of poorly sorted grains.

Practice Problem 2

Show mathematically why a bimodal sediment composed of $50\%$ coarse gravel ($\phi = -2$) and $50\%$ fine sand ($\phi = +3$) exhibits a Platykurtic ($K_G < 0.90$) distribution.

Solution: In an equal 50:50 bimodal mixture, the cumulative curve rises in two distinct steps separated by a flat terrace across the intermediate sizes. Consequently, the interquartile body ($\phi_{75} - \phi_{25}$) spans across the entire wide gap between the two modes, making the denominator $(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})$ abnormally large relative to the outer tails $(\phi_{95} - \phi_5)$. When divided by $2.44(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25})$, the resulting $K_G$ drops well below $0.90$ (often $0.55\text{--}0.65$), producing a very platykurtic (flat-topped or saddle-shaped) frequency distribution.

Practice Problem 3

What is the transformed kurtosis metric $K'_G$ proposed by Folk and Ward, and why was it introduced?

Solution: Because $K_G$ values are severely non-normal and positively skewed (ranging from $0.40$ to infinity, with normal at $1.00$), Folk and Ward introduced the Transformed Kurtosis ($K'_G$): $$K'_G = \frac{K_G}{1 + K_G}$$ This mathematical transformation normalizes the scale into a bounded range between $0.0$ and $1.0$, where a normal Gaussian curve corresponds to: $$K'_G = \frac{1.0}{1 + 1.0} = \mathbf{0.50}$$ enabling rigorous linear regression and multivariate statistical testing.

Practice Problem 4

A coastal geologist tests a beach dune sand and finds $\phi_{95} - \phi_5 = 2.44\phi$ and $\phi_{75} - \phi_{25} = 1.00\phi$. Calculate $K_G$ and identify the distribution type.

Solution: $$K_G = \frac{2.44}{2.44 \times 1.00} = \frac{2.44}{2.44} = \mathbf{1.00}$$ A $K_G$ of exactly $1.00$ corresponds to a Mesokurtic distribution. The ratio of tail sorting to central body sorting matches that of a perfectly symmetrical, standard Gaussian normal distribution.

Practice Problem 5

Why do natural sediments that have undergone prolonged sorting by wind in desert dunes frequently exhibit Leptokurtic kurtosis ($K_G > 1.11$)?

Solution: In eolian dune environments, steady wind velocities selectively transport and concentrate a very narrow, uniform grain size in the central $70\text{--}80\%$ of the sediment (the saltation population, creating an exceptionally well-sorted central body). However, occasional wind gusts roll a few oversized coarse grains (creep population) and light breezes trap a few silt dust grains, forming extended, poorly sorted tails. This contrast—an ultra-well-sorted central mode flanked by minor poorly sorted tails—forces $K_G$ into the leptokurtic field.

Practice Problem 6

Compare the percentile coverage of the Folk & Ward (1957) kurtosis formula with the earlier Trask (1932) kurtosis index.

Solution: Parker D. Trask (1932) defined a crude geometric kurtosis based solely on quartile spreads: $K = \frac{Q_1 Q_3}{2 M}$. This formula evaluates only the middle $50\%$ of the distribution and completely ignores the critical outer $90\%$ tails. Folk and Ward's formula incorporated $\phi_5$ and $\phi_{95}$, capturing the sorting behavior of $90\%$ of the sediment volume and making it far more sensitive to hydrodynamic mixing and bi-modality.

Practice Problem 7

If a cumulative curve has $\phi_5 = 0.8$, $\phi_{25} = 1.5$, $\phi_{75} = 2.0$, and $\phi_{95} = 3.6$, calculate $K_G$ and determine whether the central body or the tails are better sorted.

Solution: $$K_G = \frac{3.6 - 0.8}{2.44(2.0 - 1.5)} = \frac{2.8}{2.44 \times 0.5} = \frac{2.8}{1.22} \approx \mathbf{2.30}$$ Because $K_G = 2.30 \gg 1.00$ (Very Leptokurtic), the central body $(\phi_{75} - \phi_{25} = 0.5\phi)$ is far better sorted than the distant tails $(\phi_{95} - \phi_5 = 2.8\phi)$.

Practice Problem 8

In a Friedman (1961, 1967) bivariate scatter plot for environmental discrimination, what two Folk & Ward parameters are plotted against each other to distinguish river sands from beach sands?

Solution: Gerald M. Friedman plotted Inclusive Graphic Skewness ($Sk_I$) against Inclusive Graphic Standard Deviation (Sorting, $\sigma_I$), or Mean ($M_z$) vs. Skewness. Beach sands plot in the negative skewness ($Sk_I < 0$) and very well-sorted ($\sigma_I < 0.5\phi$) field, whereas river sands plot predominantly in the positive skewness ($Sk_I > 0$) and moderately sorted ($\sigma_I > 0.7\phi$) field.
