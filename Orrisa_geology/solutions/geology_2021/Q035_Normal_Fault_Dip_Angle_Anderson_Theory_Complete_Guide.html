1. Andersonian Dynamics & The Theoretical Dip of Normal Faults

In 1905 and 1951, the Scottish structural geologist Ernest Masson Anderson published his definitive dynamic theory of faulting based on the Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion and the physical boundary condition of Earth's free surface (where shear stress is identically zero, requiring one principal stress to be strictly vertical):

  • Principal Stress State in Normal Faulting: In an extensional tectonic regime, the maximum principal stress is vertical ($\sigma_1 = \rho g z$, lithostatic overburden load), while the intermediate stress ($\sigma_2$) and minimum principal stress ($\sigma_3$) are horizontal ($\sigma_3$ aligns with the regional extension direction).
  • Mohr-Coulomb Angle Formulation: According to Coulomb shear fracture mechanics, conjugate shear failure planes initiate at an angle $\theta$ relative to the maximum compressive stress ($\sigma_1$): $$\theta = 45^\circ - \frac{\phi}{2}$$ Where $\phi$ is the angle of internal friction of typical brittle crustal rocks ($\phi \approx 30^\circ$, friction coefficient $\mu = \tan\phi \approx 0.60$). $$\theta = 45^\circ - \frac{30^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ \quad \text{(Angle relative to vertical } \sigma_1\text{)}$$
  • Dip Angle Relative to the Horizontal Plane: Because dip is measured relative to the horizontal plane, the dip angle ($\delta$) of a newly formed normal fault is: $$\delta = 90^\circ - \theta = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = \mathbf{60^\circ} \quad \text{or} \quad \delta = 45^\circ + \frac{\phi}{2} = 45^\circ + 15^\circ = \mathbf{60^\circ}$$ Therefore, in Andersonian mechanics and global structural field measurements, normal faults initiate with a characteristic steep dip of approximately 60° to the horizontal (which corresponds to an angle of $30^\circ$ to the vertical, NOT $45^\circ$ to vertical!).
Figure 35.1 (Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, Fig. 7.14): Anderson's mechanical derivation for normal faulting. Showing vertical $\sigma_1$ generating conjugate normal faults dipping at $\approx 60^\circ$ to the horizontal.
2. Forensic Dissection: Why the Statement is FALSE

The exam question states: "Most common angle of normal faults is 45° to vertical." This statement is completely false for two distinct reasons:

  • 1. Angle with the Vertical is ~30°, NOT 45°: In pristine Andersonian rock fracture, the shear failure plane forms at an angle of $\theta = 45^\circ - \phi/2 = 30^\circ$ to the vertical ($\sigma_1$). An angle of $45^\circ$ to the vertical would require rock friction to be zero ($\phi = 0^\circ$), which never occurs in brittle crustal rocks!
  • 2. Dip with Horizontal is ~60°, NOT 45°: If the statement intended to say "45° to horizontal", that is also incorrect because normal faults dip steeply at $\approx 60^\circ$ (typically $55^\circ - 70^\circ$). Contractional thrust faults dip at $< 45^\circ$.
"Normal Faults: In normal faults, the fault plane is usually very steep, dipping at angles between 50° and 70° (most commonly around 60°) to the horizontal... The hanging wall has moved downward relative to the footwall." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Faults, p. 201)
Figure 35.2: Anderson's Conjugate Fault Geometries. Normal faults ($\sigma_1$ vertical) dip at $\sim 60^\circ$ to horizontal ($30^\circ$ to vertical); Thrust faults ($\sigma_1$ horizontal) dip at $\sim 30^\circ$ to horizontal.
3. Low-Angle Normal Faults: The Detachment Paradox

In highly extended metamorphic core complexes (e.g., Basin and Range), geologists observe low-angle normal faults (detachment faults) dipping at $< 30^\circ$:

  • The Rolling Hinge Model: Detachment faults did not initiate at low angles; they initiated as steep $60^\circ$ Andersonian normal faults, which were rotated and flattened to low angles ($< 30^\circ$) by isostatic unloading and crustal flexure as the footwall was exhumed!

Q35. Most common angle of normal faults is 45° to vertical.
(A) False  ✔
(B) True
(C) None of these
✔
Correct Answer: (A) False
The statement is False. In classical Andersonian mechanics and global structural field measurements, normal faults initiate with a characteristic steep dip of approximately 60° to the horizontal, which corresponds to an angle of 30° to the vertical ($\theta = 45^\circ - \phi/2 \approx 30^\circ$).
(A) False Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Under Andersonian fault mechanics, the maximum principal stress ($\sigma_1$) in normal fault regimes is vertical. Coulomb shear failure occurs at an angle $\theta = 45^\circ - \phi/2$ to $\sigma_1$. Because crustal rocks have an angle of internal friction $\phi \approx 30^\circ$, normal faults form at $\approx 30^\circ$ to the vertical, and dip at $\approx 60^\circ$ to the horizontal. Stating that normal faults form at "45° to vertical" would require frictionless rock ($\phi = 0^\circ$), which is physically incorrect.
(B) True Incorrect (Violates Coulomb Mechanics and Anderson's Law)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Accepting "True" ignores rock friction. Maximum shear stress ($\tau_{\text{max}}$) occurs on planes oriented at 45° to $\sigma_1$, but actual fracture occurs along planes where the ratio of shear stress to frictional resistance is maximized ($\approx 30^\circ$ to vertical).
(C) None of these Incorrect (Binary Truth Value Exists)
Why this distractor is incorrect: The statement is definitively False.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical Derivation): Using the Coulomb failure criterion with an angle of internal friction $\phi = 32^\circ$, calculate the theoretical dip angle to the horizontal ($\delta$) of a newly formed Andersonian normal fault.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $\delta = 61.0^\circ$.
    Calculation:
    $$\delta = 45^\circ + \frac{\phi}{2} = 45^\circ + \frac{32^\circ}{2} = 45^\circ + 16^\circ = 61.0^\circ$$
  • Practice Q2: Why does maximum shear failure NOT occur on the plane of maximum shear stress ($\tau_{\text{max}}$ at $45^\circ$ to $\sigma_1$)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because normal stress ($\sigma_n$) on the plane creates frictional resistance; failure occurs where the Coulomb shear stress excess ($\tau - \sigma_n \tan\phi$) is maximized.
    Explanation: Steeper planes have lower normal stress, reducing frictional clamping and shifting the failure orientation from $45^\circ$ to $\approx 30^\circ$ relative to $\sigma_1$.
  • Practice Q3: What is a conjugate normal fault system, and what is the dihedral angle between the two fault planes?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Two sets of normal faults dipping in opposite directions toward each other; dihedral angle is $2\theta = 2(45^\circ - \phi/2) \approx 60^\circ$.
    Explanation: The two conjugate planes intersect along the intermediate principal stress axis ($\sigma_2$).
  • Practice Q4: In oil exploration of rift basins, what is a rollover anticline, and along which fault type does it form?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An anticlinal closure formed in the hanging wall of a listric normal fault as the block flexes to fit the curved fault surface.
    Explanation: Rollover anticlines are premier structural hydrocarbon traps in the North Sea, Niger Delta, and Gulf of Mexico.
  • Practice Q5: What is the "Mechanical Paradox of Low-Angle Normal Faults"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: According to Byerlee's friction law ($\mu = 0.6 - 0.85$), normal faults dipping $< 30^\circ$ should lock up and be unfavorably oriented for slip under vertical overburden load.
    Explanation: To slip at $< 30^\circ$, detachment faults require extremely high pore fluid pressures, low-friction fault gouge (talc, clays), or rolling-hinge rotation.
  • Practice Q6: What structural feature forms when the hanging wall of a listric normal fault collapses due to lack of support?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A crestal collapse graben (keystone graben).
    Explanation: Bending of the hanging wall creates localized tension over the crest of the rollover, spawning antithetic and synthetic secondary normal faults.
  • Practice Q7: What is an antithetic normal fault versus a synthetic normal fault?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Synthetic faults dip in the same direction as the master border fault; antithetic faults dip in the opposite direction toward the master fault.
    Explanation: Antithetic faults accommodate rotational strain within the hanging wall block of half-grabens.
  • Practice Q8: In extensional basin analysis, what parameter is represented by the crustal stretching factor ($\beta$) in McKenzie's (1978) model?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The ratio of initial crustal thickness to final stretched crustal thickness ($\beta = t_0 / t_{\text{stretched}}$).
    Explanation: The $\beta$ factor dictates the magnitude of initial fault-controlled syn-rift tectonic subsidence and subsequent thermal post-rift sag subsidence.
