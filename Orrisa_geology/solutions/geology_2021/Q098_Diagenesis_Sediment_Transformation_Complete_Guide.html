1. Definition, Temperature-Pressure Boundaries & Framework of Diagenesis

In sedimentary geology and petroleum geoscience (Boggs, 2014, pp. 175–190; Tucker, 2001; Pettijohn et al., 1987), diagenesis is formally defined as the sum total of all physical, chemical, and biological changes and transformations that a sediment or soil undergoes after its initial deposition, during and after its burial, up to the lowest-grade boundary of metamorphism.

The term was introduced into geological science in 1888 by the Austrian geologist Carl Wilhelm von Gümbel. The pressure-temperature window of diagenesis encompasses surface conditions up to temperatures of approximately $200^\circ\text{C}$ to $250^\circ\text{C}$ and lithostatic burial pressures of approximately $1\text{ to } 2\text{ kbar}$ (depths of $\sim 5\text{ to } 7\text{ km}$), beyond which processes enter the realm of very low-grade metamorphism (anchizone / sub-greenschist facies).

Shows the tripartite diagenetic framework: Eodiagenesis (surface/shallow burial), Mesodiagenesis (deep burial compaction and thermal maturation), and Telodiagenesis (uplift and meteoric weathering).

The Tripartite Stages of Diagenesis

According to the widely accepted classification of Choquette and Pray (1970) and Schmidt and McDonald (1979), diagenesis is subdivided into three distinct chronological and depth regimes:

  1. Eodiagenesis (Early Diagenesis): Operates at or immediately beneath the depositional surface (depths from $0\text{ to a few hundred meters}$). Governed by pore fluids chemically linked to the surface environment (marine, lacustrine, meteoric), active bioturbation by benthic organisms, microbial decomposition of organic matter (aerobic respiration, sulfate reduction, methanogenesis), and early cementation (e.g., micritic calcite crusts, siderite concretions).
  2. Mesodiagenesis (Burial Diagenesis): Occurs during progressive deep basin burial (depths of $1\text{ to } 5+\text{ km}$, temperatures $50\text{--}200^\circ\text{C}$). Driven by increasing overburden stress and geothermal heat:
    • Mechanical Compaction: Reorientation and ductile degranulation of clasts, repacking grains from loose cubic to tight rhombohedral packing, causing drastic porosity reduction.
    • Chemical Compaction (Pressure Solution): Intergranular dissolution at grain contact points under high differential stress, developing concavo-convex contacts, sutured contacts, and macroscopic stylolites.
    • Authigenesis & Cementation: Precipitation of syntaxial quartz overgrowths, poikilotopic carbonate cements, and authigenic clay minerals (kaolinite, illite).
    • Smectite-to-Illite Transition: Thermally driven clay dehydration releasing bound interlayer water into pore networks: $\text{Smectite} + \text{K}^+ \xrightarrow{60\text{--}120^\circ\text{C}} \text{Illite} + \text{Quartz} + \text{Water}$.
    • Dissolution & Secondary Porosity: Leaching of unstable feldspars and carbonates by acidic pore waters generated from kerogen decarboxylation.
  3. Telodiagenesis (Uplift Diagenesis / Retrograde Diagenesis): Occurs when deeply buried sedimentary rocks are tectonically uplifted and brought back into contact with oxidizing, cool meteoric groundwaters, driving dissolution of carbonate cements, oxidation of pyrite to iron oxides/hydroxides, and kaolinitization of remaining feldspars.
2. Critical Distinction: Diagenesis vs. Lithification & Petrogenesis

Why Lithification is Only a Subset of Diagenesis

A frequent point of confusion among geology students is equating diagenesis with lithification. They are fundamentally distinct in scope:

  • Lithification (Consolidation): The specific process of turning loose, unconsolidated sediment into solid rock through mechanical compaction and intergranular mineral cementation.
  • Diagenesis (The Overarching Domain): Encompasses lithification, but also includes processes that operate in the opposite direction—such as leaching, dissolution, and secondary porosity creation, where solid rock is partially dissolved by acidic fluids, increasing void space and weakening rock competence. Diagenesis also includes recrystallization, mineral polymorphism, and isotopic re-equilibration.

Verified Correct Answer: (A) Diagenesis
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Confirmed — Diagenesis

Correct: By universal scientific definition in geology (Boggs, 2014, p. 175; Bangar, p. 68; Pettijohn et al., 1987), diagenesis is the exact term designating the entire spectrum of physical, chemical, and biological transformations that alter sediments or soils after deposition, throughout burial and compaction, up to the threshold of regional metamorphism. The question's formulation ("physical or chemical transformation of sediments or soil by geological processes") matches this textbook definition word for word.

Option (B) Refuted — Petrogenesis

Incorrect: Petrogenesis (from Greek petra = rock + genesis = origin) is the broad branch of petrology that deals with the origin, historical evolution, and chemical genesis of rocks in general. It is most commonly applied to igneous rocks (e.g., magma generation, partial melting, fractional crystallization) and metamorphic rocks (anatexis, metamorphic reactions). It does not specifically refer to the post-depositional transformation of sediments or soils.

Option (C) Refuted — Lithification

Incorrect: Lithification refers strictly and narrowly to the process of converting loose, soft sediment into solid, cohesive rock (primarily via mechanical compaction and cement precipitation). It is merely one individual sub-stage within the much broader domain of diagenesis. Diagenesis includes many major chemical transformations that do not consolidate rock—such as the creation of secondary dissolution porosity, dolomitization, neoformation of clay minerals, and telodiagenetic weathering.

Option (D) Refuted — Crustification

Incorrect: In economic geology and mineral deposit petrography, crustification refers to a distinct texture in epigenetic hydrothermal mineral veins, where successive crusts or crystalline layers of different minerals (e.g., quartz, sphalerite, galena, calcite) are deposited on the walls of an open fracture or cavity. It has no relevance to the post-depositional alteration of sedimentary beds or soils.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A quartz sandstone at $3000\text{ m}$ burial depth in a sedimentary basin exhibits intergranular porosity of $18\%$. Petrographic examination shows that $8\%$ of this porosity consists of oversized pores containing corroded, skeletal remnants of K-feldspar. Classify this porosity and explain the diagenetic reaction responsible.

Solution: The $8\%$ porosity represents secondary dissolution porosity generated during mesodiagenesis. The reaction is driven by acidic pore fluids generated during the thermal maturation of organic matter (kerogen), which releases carbon dioxide and organic carboxylic acids at temperatures of $80\text{--}120^\circ\text{C}$. The acidic fluids aggressively leach unstable detrital K-feldspar: $$2\text{KAlSi}_3\text{O}_8 + 2\text{H}^+ + 9\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5(\text{OH})_4\text{ (kaolinite)} + 4\text{H}_4\text{SiO}_4 + 2\text{K}^+$$ dissolving the feldspar interior and leaving behind empty oversized dissolution moldic pores.

Practice Problem 2

Explain how pressure solution (chemical compaction) in sandstones acts as an internal source of silica for syntaxial quartz overgrowth cements.

Solution: Under high vertical lithostatic stress, the stress concentrated at grain-to-grain contact points exceeds the hydrostatic pore-fluid pressure. According to the Riecke Principle, mineral solubility increases with directed compressive stress. Consequently, quartz selectively dissolves at grain contact points. The dissolved aqueous silica ($\text{H}_4\text{SiO}_4$) diffuses outward into adjacent low-stress, open pore spaces where fluid is supersaturated relative to hydrostatic stress, precipitating onto detrital quartz grains as syntaxial quartz overgrowths.

Practice Problem 3

What is a "stylolite" in sedimentary petrology, and in which rocks are stylolites most prominently developed?

Solution: A stylolite is an irregular, serrated, suture-like planar or undulating seam formed by localized, intense chemical compaction (pressure solution). Along the stylolite, substantial thicknesses of the host rock have been completely dissolved away, leaving behind a thin insoluble residue of clay minerals, iron oxides, organic carbon, and heavy minerals. Stylolites are most prominently developed in carbonates (limestones and dolomites) and well-sorted quartzose sandstones.

Practice Problem 4

Explain the smectite-to-illite transformation during burial diagenesis and state its significance as a geothermometer in petroleum exploration.

Solution: With increasing burial depth and temperature, expandable smectite reacts with available potassium (from dissolving K-feldspar) to form non-expandable illite through a series of mixed-layer illite/smectite (I/S) intermediates: $$\text{Smectite} + \text{K}^+ + \text{Al}^{3+} \xrightarrow{60\text{--}140^\circ\text{C}} \text{Illite} + \text{Quartz} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$ The percentage of illite layers in mixed-layer I/S clays increases progressively with temperature (e.g., $80\%$ illite layers corresponds to $\sim 100\text{--}120^\circ\text{C}$). This transformation serves as an invaluable inorganic geothermometer to map peak paleotemperatures and assess whether organic source rocks have entered the "oil generation window" ($60\text{--}120^\circ\text{C}$).

Practice Problem 5

Why does the Athy (1930) porosity-depth relationship show an exponential decline in mudrocks with depth: $\phi(z) = \phi_0 e^{-cz}$?

Solution: L.F. Athy established that mechanical compaction of fine-grained mudrocks is governed by the progressive expulsion of interstitial pore water under increasing effective overburden stress ($\sigma'_v = \sigma_v - P_p$). At shallow depths, high-porosity card-house clay fabrics collapse easily under small stress increments. As depth increases, grains become closely packed and interlocked, drastically increasing skeletal bulk stiffness; hence, progressively larger overburden increments are required to achieve further volumetric strain, producing an exponential decay curve.

Practice Problem 6

Contrast "eodiagenesis", "mesodiagenesis", and "telodiagenesis" in terms of their fluid chemistry and temperature conditions.

Solution: - Eodiagenesis: Low temperature ($< 50^\circ\text{C}$); fluids are depositional surface waters (marine or meteoric); active microbial and biological control. - Mesodiagenesis: Elevated temperatures ($50\text{--}200^\circ\text{C}$); fluids are evolved, highly saline basinal formation brines; thermochemical reactions, hydrocarbon maturation, and burial pressure dominate. - Telodiagenesis: Low temperature ($< 50^\circ\text{C}$); fluids are fresh, cold, oxidizing, carbonic-acid-charged meteoric waters introduced via tectonic uplift and erosional unconformities.

Practice Problem 7

What is "pseudomatrix" in a compacted sandstone and how does it originate diagenetically?

Solution: Pseudomatrix is an artificial, diagenetically generated interstitial matrix formed when ductile, mechanically weak framework grains (such as shale clasts, mudstone lithics, or deeply weathered volcanic fragments) are squashed, deformed, and squeezed into adjacent intergranular pore spaces between rigid quartz grains during mechanical compaction. Under the microscope, it mimics primary detrital clay matrix, but petrographic inspection reveals ghost outlines of compacted lithic grains.

Practice Problem 8

State the upper physical boundary that demarcates diagenesis from metamorphism in sedimentary basins.

Solution: The boundary between diagenesis and metamorphism is transitional but formally defined by: 1. Temperature: Approximately $200^\circ\text{C}$ to $250^\circ\text{C}$. 2. Vitrinite Reflectance ($R_o$): $R_o \approx 2.5\text{ to } 3.0\%$. 3. Clay Mineralogy: The disappearance of smectite and mixed-layer clays, marked by the onset of well-crystallized illite (Kübler Index illite crystallinity $\approx 0.42^\circ\Delta 2\theta$, defining the anchizone) and the appearance of metamorphic pyrophyllite, chloritoid, or paragonite.
