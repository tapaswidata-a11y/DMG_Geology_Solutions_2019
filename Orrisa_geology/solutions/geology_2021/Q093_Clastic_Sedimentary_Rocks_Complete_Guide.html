1. Fundamental Classification of Sedimentary Rocks: Clastic vs. Non-Clastic

In classical geology and sedimentary petrology (Bangar, p. 67; Mahapatra, p. 142; Boggs, 2014, p. 126), sedimentary rocks are categorized fundamentally into two grand genetic subdivisions based on their mode of origin and source material:

  1. Clastic (Detrital / Mechanically Formed) Rocks: Sedimentary rocks formed by the accumulation, compaction, and cementation of particulate solid mineral and rock fragments (clasts or detritus) produced by the mechanical breakdown and physical disintegration of pre-existing primary (igneous, metamorphic, or older sedimentary) rocks. The word clastic derives from the Greek klastos ($\kappa\lambda\alpha\sigma\tau\acute{o}\varsigma$), meaning "broken in pieces".
  2. Non-Clastic (Chemical and Biochemical/Organic) Rocks: Sedimentary rocks formed either by direct chemical precipitation of dissolved mineral salts from aqueous solution (e.g., evaporites like rock salt and gypsum, travertine) or by organic accumulation of biochemical skeletal debris and plant matter (e.g., fossiliferous limestones, chalk, diatomite, coal).

2. Mechanical Weathering Processes & Detrital Disintegration

Physical Weathering Mechanisms Generating Clasts

The mechanical breakdown of primary igneous and metamorphic bedrocks into detrital clasts occurs through several distinct physical weathering mechanisms operating without significant initial chemical transformation:

  • Frost Wedging (Gelifraction): Liquid water infiltrates joints and microcracks. Upon freezing at $0^\circ\text{C}$, water expands by approximately $9\%$ in volume, exerting immense crystallization pressures up to $200\text{ MPa}$, shattering massive parent rocks into angular rubble and scree.
  • Unloading / Pressure Release (Exfoliation): Deep-seated plutonic rocks (e.g., granite) crystallized under high lithostatic pressure expand elastically when overlying rock is stripped by erosion. Release of confining stress produces sheeting joints parallel to the topographic surface, causing outer shells of rock to spall off.
  • Thermal Expansion and Contraction (Insolation): In diurnal desert environments, rapid surface heating and cooling induce differential thermal stresses between mineral grains with differing coefficients of thermal expansion (e.g., quartz vs. feldspar), initiating micro-fractures.
  • Salt Crystallization (Haloclasty): Saline groundwater in arid regions evaporates in porous rocks; growing halite or gypsum crystals wedge pore walls apart.
  • Hydraulic and Abrasion Drag: Torrential rivers, glacial ice, and breaking waves physically pound, grind, and dislodge fragments from bedrock channels.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 93: Sedimentary rocks formed due to the sediments formed from mechanical breakdown of primary rocks is called :
(A) siliclastic rocks
(B) clastic rocks
(C) semi-clastic rocks
(D) chemical sediments
Verified Correct Answer: (B) clastic rocks
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Refuted — Siliclastic Rocks

Incorrect: While siliciclastic rocks (spelled siliciclastic) represent the most common subdivision of clastic rocks, the term is restricted specifically to rocks where the constituent clasts are composed exclusively of silicate minerals (quartz, feldspar, clay minerals, and silicate lithic fragments). The question asks for the fundamental, overarching class of sedimentary rocks formed from the mechanical breakdown of primary rocks. That universal, primary classification category in classical petrology (Bangar, p. 67; Mahapatra, p. 142) is clastic rocks. Furthermore, "clastic rocks" encompasses all fragmental rocks (including non-silicate clastic carbonates, evaporite-clastics, and pyroclastics), making (B) the broader and canonically correct answer.

Option (B) Confirmed — Clastic Rocks

Correct: In all authoritative geological textbooks (Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology, p. 67; Mahapatra, A Text-Book of Geology, p. 142; Boggs, Principles of Sedimentology and Stratigraphy, p. 126), sedimentary rocks formed by the accumulation of fragments and mineral particles derived from the mechanical breakdown (physical weathering and disintegration) of pre-existing primary rocks are explicitly designated as clastic rocks (or detrital rocks). Examples include conglomerates, breccias, sandstones, and shales.

Option (C) Refuted — Semi-Clastic Rocks

Incorrect: "Semi-clastic" is not a formal or standard taxonomic class in sedimentary petrology. While informal descriptions might occasionally refer to rocks with mixed origins (such as volcaniclastic-tuffaceous sediments or marls), no major geological classification system defines the product of primary rock mechanical breakdown as "semi-clastic."

Option (D) Refuted — Chemical Sediments

Incorrect: Chemical sediments represent the exact antithesis of mechanically formed clastic rocks. Chemical sedimentary rocks (such as halite, gypsum, anhydrite, chert, and travertine) are formed by precipitation of dissolved ions directly from aqueous solution through inorganic chemical processes (evaporation, pH/Eh changes, supersaturation), without involving the physical accumulation of mechanically disintegrated rock fragments.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A sedimentary rock contains $65\%$ angular gravel clasts larger than $2\text{ mm}$ cemented by iron oxide. Name this rock, state its broad class, and interpret the distance of sediment transport from the primary source area.

Solution: The rock is a sedimentary breccia. It belongs to the rudaceous (psephitic) division of clastic sedimentary rocks. Because the gravel clasts are sharply angular (exhibiting sharp edges and corners with no rounding), it indicates minimal to negligible physical transport from the source area (e.g., deposited at the base of a fault scarp or talus/scree slope directly adjacent to the weathering bedrock).

Practice Problem 2

Explain why mechanical breakdown alone cannot produce an orthoquartzite containing $99\%$ monocrystalline quartz from a parent granitic pluton.

Solution: A parent granite typically contains $25\text{--}35\%$ quartz, $50\text{--}65\%$ feldspars (orthoclase, plagioclase), and $5\text{--}15\%$ mafic silicates (biotite, hornblende). Pure mechanical breakdown shatters all minerals equally. To achieve $99\%$ quartz purity (high mineralogical maturity), mechanical breakdown must operate in conjunction with intense chemical weathering (hydrolysis) in a warm, humid climate, which chemically decomposes unstable feldspars to clay ($2\text{KAlSi}_3\text{O}_8 + 2\text{H}^+ + 9\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5(\text{OH})_4 + 4\text{H}_4\text{SiO}_4 + 2\text{K}^+$) and dissolves mafics, leaving behind chemically inert quartz.

Practice Problem 3

What is the difference between a "conglomerate" and a "breccia" in clastic petrology, and what hydrodynamic parameter does this difference reflect?

Solution: Both conglomerate and breccia are rudaceous clastic rocks containing $> 30\%$ gravel-sized clasts ($> 2\text{ mm}$). In a conglomerate, the clasts are rounded to sub-rounded due to sustained attrition, abrasion, and tumbling during prolonged aquatic transport (e.g., fluvial rivers or wave-swept beaches). In a breccia, the clasts are angular to sub-angular, reflecting rapid deposition without significant abrasion (e.g., scree slopes, alluvial fans, or landslide debris flows).

Practice Problem 4

Calculate the Wentworth phi ($\phi$) value corresponding to the boundary between arenaceous (sand) and argillaceous (silt/clay) clastic sediments.

Solution: The Wentworth phi scale is defined mathematically as: $$\phi = -\log_2(d)$$ where $d$ is the grain diameter in millimeters. The boundary between sand and silt is $1/16\text{ mm} = 0.0625\text{ mm}$. $$\phi = -\log_2\left(\frac{1}{16}\right) = -\log_2(2^{-4}) = -(-4) = +4.0\phi$$ Thus, the sand-silt boundary corresponds exactly to $+4.0\phi$. Particles coarser than $+4\phi$ are sand (arenaceous); finer than $+4\phi$ are silt/clay (argillaceous).

Practice Problem 5

Classify a rock consisting of $70\%$ volcanic ash and pumice fragments ejected violently from a volcanic vent and subsequently deposited and lithified in a lacustrine basin. Is it an igneous rock, a clastic sedimentary rock, or a hybrid?

Solution: The rock is a pyroclastic rock (specifically a lithified volcanic tuff). Petrologically, it occupies a hybrid boundary: its components are generated by igneous volcanic processes (explosive magmatic fragmentation), but its transport, sorting, settling, and depositional bedding are governed entirely by sedimentary fluid processes. In sedimentological nomenclature, it is classified as a volcaniclastic rock.

Practice Problem 6

In the Goldich Weathering Series, which common rock-forming primary silicate mineral is most susceptible to mechanical and chemical breakdown, and which is the most resilient?

Solution: According to S.S. Goldich (1938), the susceptibility of primary silicate minerals to weathering is the inverse of Bowen's Reaction Series. Olivine (specifically forsterite, crystallizing at the highest temperatures $\sim 1200^\circ\text{C}$ with isolated $\text{SiO}_4^{4-}$ tetrahedra) is the most susceptible and least stable at surface conditions. Quartz (crystallizing at lowest temperatures with a fully polymerized 3D framework of Si-O bonds) is the most resilient and chemically stable.

Practice Problem 7

Explain the role of freeze-thaw cycles (frost wedging) in the mechanical breakdown of alpine primary bedrocks, including the thermodynamic volumetric expansion of water.

Solution: When liquid water transitions to hexagonal ice at $0^\circ\text{C}$, the open hydrogen-bonded crystal lattice forces a $9\%$ increase in specific volume (from $1.000\text{ cm}^3\text{/g}$ to $1.0905\text{ cm}^3\text{/g}$). When confined within tight rock fractures, this expansion generates hydrostatic crystallization pressures theoretically reaching up to $207\text{ MPa}$ at $-22^\circ\text{C}$, vastly exceeding the tensile strength of crystalline rocks ($5\text{--}25\text{ MPa}$) and driving rapid physical shattering into block fields (felsenmeer).

Practice Problem 8

Why are graywackes (lithic/feldspathic wackes) referred to as "texturally and mineralogically immature" clastic rocks?

Solution: Graywackes contain $> 15\%$ fine-grained argillaceous/chloritic matrix, poorly sorted, angular to sub-angular framework grains (indicating textural immaturity), and high proportions of chemically unstable rock fragments and labile feldspars alongside quartz (indicating mineralogical immaturity). This combination reflects rapid erosion, short transport distances, and rapid deposition in high-energy, tectonically active environments (e.g., deep-marine turbidity currents in forearc basins).
