1. Definition, Thermal Framework & The Hornfels Facies Series

The term hornfels (German for "horn-stone", derived from its exceptional mechanical toughness and smooth, flint-like fracture resembling animal horn) designates a dense, fine-grained, non-foliated contact metamorphic rock produced by thermal recrystallization in the contact aureole surrounding an igneous intrusion.

In metamorphic petrology (Winter, 2014, pp. 484–487; Turner, 1981; Bangar, p. 81), contact metamorphism is characterized by low confining lithostatic pressures ($P < 2\text{--}3\text{ kbar}$, depths $< 10\text{ km}$) and steep thermal gradients. In the inner, highest-temperature zones of contact aureoles—specifically the Pyroxene Hornfels Facies—equilibrium crystallization temperatures commonly range from $700^\circ\text{C}$ to $800^\circ\text{C}$:

2. Heat Conduction Physics & Anhydrous Dehydration in the $700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$ Window

Magma Intrusion Temperatures and Contact Equilibrium

The maximum temperature attained at the contact interface between an intruding magma and cold country rock is governed by Jaeger's (1957) conductive heat transfer model:

$$T_{contact} = \frac{T_{magma} + T_{country}}{2}$$
  • For a granitic magma intruding at $T_{magma} \approx 800\text{--}900^\circ\text{C}$ into country rock at $200^\circ\text{C}$, the immediate contact temperature is $\sim 500\text{--}600^\circ\text{C}$ (Hornblende hornfels).
  • For intermediate and mafic magmas (diorites, gabbros, basalts) intruding at $T_{magma} \approx 1100\text{--}1200^\circ\text{C}$, the proximal contact interface and inner aureole easily reach sustained temperatures of $700^\circ\text{C}$ to $800^\circ\text{C}$, generating classic pyroxene hornfelses.

Anhydrous Breakdown Reactions at $700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$

In the $700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$ window, all hydrous amphiboles and micas become completely unstable and dehydrate:

$$\text{Hornblende} + \text{Quartz} \xrightarrow{700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}} \text{Diopside (Cpx)} + \text{Hypersthene (Opx)} + \text{Plagioclase} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\uparrow$$ $$\text{Biotite} + \text{Muscovite} + \text{Quartz} \xrightarrow{700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}} \text{Cordierite} + \text{K-feldspar} + \text{Sillimanite} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\uparrow$$

This total loss of bound hydroxyl groups results in the completely anhydrous, dense, crystalline hornfelsic texture.

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 119: Common temperatures for the formation of hornfels range from about :
(A) 700° to 800°C
(B) > 1000°C
(C) 1000-1200°C
(D) None of these
Verified Correct Answer: (A) 700° to 800°C
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Confirmed — The High-Temperature Hornfels Range

Correct: In metamorphic petrology curricula and standard examination benchmarks (Winter, 2014, p. 485; Turner, 1981; Bangar, p. 81), the classic high-temperature contact metamorphic hornfels facies—specifically the Pyroxene Hornfels Facies developed in the inner contact aureoles of igneous intrusions—is universally defined across the temperature window of $700^\circ\text{C}$ to $800^\circ\text{C}$. At these temperatures, hydrous phyllosilicates and amphiboles completely dehydrate into pyroxenes, cordierite, and sillimanite.

Option (B) Refuted — Temperatures $> 1000^\circ\text{C}$

Incorrect: Temperatures greater than $1000^\circ\text{C}$ exceed the dry melting solidus of crustal rocks, where rocks undergo extensive partial melting and anatexis, entering the magmatic or ultra-rare sanidinite facies (pyrometamorphism). Sanidinite facies conditions are restricted to microscopic xenoliths immersed directly in flowing lava flows or volcanic necks. Normal hornfelses in contact aureoles do not form at $> 1000^\circ\text{C}$.

Option (C) Refuted — Temperatures $1000\text{--}1200^\circ\text{C}$

Incorrect: The range $1000\text{--}1200^\circ\text{C}$ represents the liquidus eruption temperature of primary basaltic magmas in the mantle and oceanic spreading ridges, not the metamorphic temperature of hornfels country rock. Subjecting country rock to $1200^\circ\text{C}$ results in total complete fusion (buchite glass / magma), not solid-state metamorphic hornfels recrystallization.

Option (D) Refuted — None of These

Incorrect: Because Option (A) is the canonically verified and petrologically accurate temperature range, Option (D) is false.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

A quartz-mica shale is thermally metamorphosed in the inner contact aureole of a gabbro lopolith at $T = 750^\circ\text{C}$ and $P = 1.5\text{ kbar}$. What aluminum silicate polymorph ($\text{Al}_2\text{SiO}_5$) will crystallize, and why is kyanite absent?

Solution: At $T = 750^\circ\text{C}$ and $P = 1.5\text{ kbar}$, conditions fall squarely within the stability field of Sillimanite (or high-temperature andalusite near the boundary). Kyanite is completely absent because kyanite is the dense, high-pressure polymorph requiring confining pressures exceeding $P > 4\text{--}6\text{ kbar}$ ($z > 15\text{--}20\text{ km}$). Contact metamorphic aureoles develop at shallow crustal depths ($< 5\text{--}10\text{ km}$), where lithostatic pressures are far too low to stabilize kyanite.

Practice Problem 2

Explain the microscopic texture called "hornfelsic texture" (or decussate texture) and why it develops under contact metamorphism.

Solution: Hornfelsic (granoblastic / decussate) texture is an equigranular mosaic of interlocking, unoriented, polygonal mineral crystals meeting at triple junctions of approximately $120^\circ$. It develops because contact metamorphism operates under hydrostatic (non-directed) stress. In the absence of directed tectonic differential stress ($\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma_3$), crystals minimize their interfacial surface energy by growing with completely random, interlocking orientations without developing planar foliation.

Practice Problem 3

Calculate the width of the contact aureole where temperature exceeded $700^\circ\text{C}$ around a basaltic dyke of thickness $2a = 10\text{ m}$ intruded at $T_m = 1150^\circ\text{C}$ into country rock at $T_0 = 50^\circ\text{C}$, assuming instantaneous intrusion without latent heat.

Solution: According to Carslaw and Jaeger's 1D conductive cooling model for a dyke: The maximum temperature at the dyke wall ($x = a = 5\text{ m}$) immediately after intrusion is: $$T_{contact} = \frac{T_m + T_0}{2} = \frac{1150 + 50}{2} = \mathbf{600^\circ\text{C}}$$ Notice that for a thin dyke, conduction into cold wall rock keeps the contact temperature at $600^\circ\text{C}$, which is below $700^\circ\text{C}$. This demonstrates mathematically that narrow dykes cannot produce the $700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$ pyroxene hornfels facies; high-temperature hornfelses ($700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$) require massive plutons or batholiths where sustained magma convection and latent heat of crystallization maintain aureole wall temperatures at $> 700^\circ\text{C}$ for thousands of years.

Practice Problem 4

What is a "buchite" and under what exceptional thermal conditions does it form?

Solution: A buchite is a pyrometamorphic rock produced when pelitic or psammitic rocks are subjected to extreme temperatures ($> 850\text{--}1050^\circ\text{C}$) in direct contact with dry, superheated basaltic lava flows or volcanic chimneys. Under these extreme conditions, the rock undergoes incipient melting (pyrometamorphism), producing a distinctive rock composed of relic quartz and cordierite crystals embedded in a brown, vesicular glass matrix (pseudotachylyte-like glass).

Practice Problem 5

How do the mineral assemblages of the Hornblende Hornfels facies and the Pyroxene Hornfels facies differ in metamorphosed basaltic host rocks?

Solution: - Hornblende Hornfels Facies ($500\text{--}700^\circ\text{C}$): Dominated by hydrous minerals: Hornblende + Plagioclase (andesine/labradorite) $\pm$ Biotite $\pm$ Quartz. - Pyroxene Hornfels Facies ($700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$): Hornblende has completely dehydrated, replaced by an anhydrous assemblage: Diopside (Clinopyroxene) + Hypersthene (Orthopyroxene) + Calcic Plagioclase $\pm$ Olivine.

Practice Problem 6

What is "chiastolite" and in which metamorphic zone of a contact aureole is it characteristically found?

Solution: Chiastolite is a distinctive variety of andalusite containing cross-shaped, geometric carbonaceous and graphite inclusions oriented along its crystallographic prism diagonals. It forms in the outer to middle contact aureole (spotted slate to hornblende hornfels zone, $450\text{--}550^\circ\text{C}$) during the thermal metamorphism of carbonaceous/organic-rich shales. Rapid dendritic growth of andalusite prism corners pushes carbonaceous dust into diagonal re-entrant planes, creating the Maltese cross pattern.

Practice Problem 7

Why is high unconfined compressive strength ($\sigma_c > 200\text{--}250\text{ MPa}$) a defining mechanical property of hornfels in engineering geology?

Solution: In parent shales or slates, pervasive fissility and clay cleavage planes represent planes of extreme mechanical weakness. During high-temperature contact metamorphism ($700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$), thermal sintering, loss of hydroxyl fluids, and complete granoblastic recrystallization weld the minerals into a tightly interlocked, non-cleavable, isotropic crystalline mosaic. This obliterates all planes of weakness, transforming soft shale into one of the toughest, most abrasive, and shatter-resistant rocks known in rock mechanics.

Practice Problem 8

State the fundamental difference between the "Pyroxene Hornfels Facies" and the "Granulite Facies", given that both equilibrate at similar temperatures ($700\text{--}800^\circ\text{C}$) and contain orthopyroxene.

Solution: The defining distinction is confining pressure and tectonic stress: - Pyroxene Hornfels Facies: Forms at low lithostatic pressure ($P < 2\text{--}3\text{ kbar}$, $z < 8\text{ km}$) under hydrostatic stress; rocks are massive, non-foliated, fine-grained hornfelses. High-pressure minerals like almandine-pyrope garnet and kyanite are prohibited. - Granulite Facies: Forms at high lithostatic pressure ($P = 6\text{--}11\text{ kbar}$, $z = 20\text{--}35\text{ km}$) under regional orogenic stress in the deep continental crust; rocks are coarse-grained, pervasively banded or foliated (charnockites, granulites), and frequently contain high-pressure almandine garnet.
