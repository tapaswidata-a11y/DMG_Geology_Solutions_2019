1. Fleuty's (1964) Classification of Folds by Axial Plane Attitude

In structural geology, the spatial orientation of a fold is rigorously classified based on two independent geometric criteria: the dip of its axial surface (axial plane) and the plunge of its hinge line. In M.J. Fleuty's (1964) internationally accepted standard classification, folds are categorized by axial plane dip:

Fold Type (Fleuty, 1964) Dip of Axial Surface ($\theta_{\text{AP}}$) Limb Attitude & Stratigraphic Inversion Characteristic Kinematic Environment
Upright Fold $80^\circ - 90^\circ$ (Vertical) Both limbs dip at equal angles in opposite directions; normal younging Symmetrical horizontal compression
Steeply Inclined Fold $60^\circ - 80^\circ$ Both limbs dip in opposite directions; one limb steeper Moderate tectonic asymmetry
Moderately Inclined Fold $30^\circ - 60^\circ$ Both limbs dip in opposite directions; asymmetrical Orogenic contractional belts
Gently Inclined / Overturned $10^\circ - 30^\circ$ One limb overturned (inverted stratigraphy); limbs dip in same direction Intense asymmetric tectonic shear
Recumbent Fold $0^\circ - 10^\circ$ (Horizontal or Sub-horizontal: $\approx 0^\circ$) Axial surface is horizontal; inverted (overturned) lower limb Large-scale crustal nappe translation; Alpine/Himalayan thrust sheets
Figure 33.1 (Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology, Fig. 12.8): Fleuty's fold classification by axial plane dip, showing the progression from Upright ($90^\circ$) to Overturned and Recumbent ($0^\circ$).
2. Anatomical Architecture of a Recumbent Fold

A Recumbent Fold represents the ultimate extreme of overturned folding, where intense horizontal shear completely overturns the fold until its axial plane lies essentially horizontal ($\approx 0^\circ$):

  • Normal Limb (Upper Limb): The upper limb of the recumbent fold, which retains normal upright stratigraphic younging (older beds below, younger beds above).
  • Inverted Limb (Middle / Lower Limb): The lower limb, which has been rotated through more than $90^\circ$, causing the stratigraphic succession to be completely upside-down (inverted stratigraphy: younger beds below, older beds above).
  • Recumbent Nappes: When recumbent folds are sheared along their attenuated inverted limbs, they transition into thrust nappes (fold-nappes, e.g., the Morcles nappe in the Swiss Alps, or the Garhwal nappe in the Himalayas).
"Recumbent Fold: In this type of fold, the compressive forces are so intense that the fold is completely overturned, and the axial plane becomes practically horizontal (dips at 0° or nearly so). The limbs are horizontal or sub-horizontal, and one limb is completely overturned with inverted stratigraphy." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Ch. 7: Folds, p. 193, Fig. 7.6)
Figure 33.2 (K.M. Bangar, Fig. 7.6): Authentic cross-section of a Recumbent Fold with a horizontal axial surface ($0^\circ$ dip), showing the normal upper limb and inverted lower limb.
3. Refutation of 90°, 60°, and 45° Dip Angles

Candidate elimination based on Fleuty's boundaries:

  • 90° (Option C): Defines an Upright fold with a perfectly vertical axial plane.
  • 60° (Option B): Defines a Moderately to Steeply Inclined fold.
  • 45° (Option A): Defines an Inclined or Overturned fold, not a recumbent fold.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 33)
Q33. In a recumbent fold, the axial plane dips about _____ :
(A) 45°
(B) 60°
(C) 90°
(D) 0°  ✔
✔
Correct Answer: (D) 0°
By standard structural definition (Fleuty 1964; Bangar p. 193; Fossen p. 244), the defining geometric criterion of a recumbent fold is that its axial surface is horizontal or essentially horizontal, dipping at approximately 0° ($0^\circ - 10^\circ$).
(A) 45° Incorrect (Moderately Inclined / Overturned Fold)
Why this distractor is incorrect: An axial plane dipping at 45° characterizes an inclined fold or an overturned fold. It is far too steep to be classified as recumbent.
(B) 60° Incorrect (Steeply Inclined Fold)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A dip of 60° places the fold into the steeply inclined fold category under Fleuty's classification.
(C) 90° Incorrect (Upright Symmetrical Fold)
Why this distractor is incorrect: An axial plane dipping at 90° defines an upright fold, which is the exact polar opposite of a recumbent fold.
(D) 0° Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Derived from the Latin recumbere ("to recline or lie down"), a recumbent fold is geometrically defined as a fold whose axial surface is horizontal ($\approx 0^\circ$). The fold has been overturned so severely that its limbs and axial plane lie flat.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: In an overturned fold, what field criterion proves that the middle limb is stratigraphically inverted?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Inverted primary sedimentary structures (e.g., cross-bedding truncated at bottom, inverted graded bedding, or downward-pointing pillow lavas).
    Explanation: Because bedding dips normally, only geopetal way-up indicators prove that the strata are completely upside-down.
  • Practice Q2: What is a fold-nappe?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A colossal recumbent fold whose middle inverted limb is stretched, thinned, and translated horizontally tens of kilometers.
    Explanation: Classic examples include the Penninic nappes of the Alps, where recumbent folds travel over $50\text{ km}$ across basement rocks.
  • Practice Q3: What is the difference between a reclined fold and a recumbent fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A recumbent fold has a horizontal axial plane ($0^\circ$); a reclined fold has an inclined axial plane where the hinge line plunges down the true dip of the axial plane (rake $= 90^\circ$).
    Explanation: In a reclined fold, plunge equals dip, and the hinge points straight down the axial plane.
  • Practice Q4: In an upright symmetrical fold, how does the axial plane orient relative to the interlimb angle?
    ✔ Correct Answer: It exactly bisects the interlimb angle.
    Explanation: Symmetrical folds have identical limb lengths and equal dips, causing the axial surface to be the exact bisecting plane.
  • Practice Q5: How do parasitic folds ($S$-folds, $Z$-folds, $M$-folds) relate to the axial plane of a major recumbent fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Their axial surfaces are parallel to the master axial plane, with $M$-folds occurring in the hinge zone and $S$/$Z$-folds on the limbs.
    Explanation: Pumpelly's Rule states that minor parasitic fold axes and axial surfaces are parallel to the major tectonic fold structure.
  • Practice Q6: What cleavage type forms parallel to the horizontal axial plane of a recumbent fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Horizontal to sub-horizontal axial planar cleavage ($S_1$).
    Explanation: Recumbent folding reorients metamorphic mica grains into horizontal foliation planes parallel to the flat axial surface.
  • Practice Q7: Can a recumbent fold have a steeply plunging hinge line?
    ✔ Correct Answer: No; because the axial plane is horizontal ($0^\circ$), any line lying within it must also be horizontal ($0^\circ$ plunge).
    Explanation: By geometry, a line lying on a horizontal plane can only have a plunge of $0^\circ$.
  • Practice Q8: What is a box fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fold having a broad, flat crest and two distinct hinge lines with two conjugate axial planes dipping toward each other.
    Explanation: Box folds (conjugate folds) form where two conjugate shear bands or slip surfaces interact, creating a trapezoidal profile.
