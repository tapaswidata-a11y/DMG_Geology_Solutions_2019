1. The Fundamental Dichotomy: Body Waves vs. Surface Waves

In continuum elastodynamics, seismic waves generated by fault rupture are partitioned into two fundamental physical classes depending on their propagation geometry:

  • Body Waves (Volumetric Waves): Elastic waves that propagate through the internal three-dimensional volume or body of the Earth. Body waves travel along subterranean curved ray paths dictated by Snell's law in a spherically stratified planet.
    • Primary ($P$) Waves (Compressional / Longitudinal): Volumetric change (dilatation/compression) without shear rotation ($\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$). Particle motion is strictly parallel to the direction of wave propagation. Can propagate through solids, liquids, and gases.
    • Secondary ($S$) Waves (Shear / Transverse): Pure shear deformation without volume change ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$). Particle motion is perpendicular to the propagation direction. Because liquids and gases possess zero shear modulus ($\mu = 0$), $S$-waves cannot propagate through fluids (liquid outer core shadow zone).
  • Surface Waves (Guided Boundary Waves): Waves trapped near the free boundary of the Earth (crust-atmosphere interface) that propagate horizontally in two dimensions. Their amplitude decays exponentially with depth: $A(z) \propto e^{-k z}$.
    • Rayleigh Waves: Retrograde elliptical particle motion in the vertical plane containing the propagation vector. Produced by constructive interference of $P$ and $SV$ waves at the free surface.
    • Love Waves: Purely horizontal transverse shear motion ($SH$) guided within a low-velocity surface layer over a higher-velocity half-space.

2. Elastodynamic Velocity Equations for P and S Waves

The propagation velocities of seismic body waves depend strictly on the elastic moduli and bulk density of the rock medium:

Navier-Cauchy Elastodynamic Equations of Motion
$$\rho \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = (\lambda + \mu)\nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}) + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}$$

Where $\mathbf{u}$ is displacement, $\rho$ is density, $\lambda$ is Lamé's first parameter, and $\mu$ is shear modulus (rigidity). Taking the divergence ($\nabla \cdot$) yields the $P$-wave equation; taking the curl ($\nabla \times$) yields the $S$-wave equation:

$$v_p = \sqrt{\frac{K + \frac{4}{3}\mu}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho}}, \qquad v_s = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}}$$

Where $K = \lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu$ is bulk modulus (incompressibility). Because $K > 0$ and $\mu > 0$, $v_p$ is mathematically always strictly greater than $v_s$ in any elastic medium!

  • Theoretical Velocity Ratio: For an ideal Poisson solid ($\nu = 0.25$, where $\lambda = \mu$): $$\frac{v_p}{v_s} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$$
  • In typical continental upper crust: $v_p \approx 6.0\text{ km/s}$, $v_s \approx 3.5\text{ km/s}$.
  • In fluid media (water, air, liquid outer core): $\mu = 0 \implies v_s = 0$, while $v_p = \sqrt{K/\rho} > 0$.

Official Examination Question
Question 39
P and S waves are collectively called :
(A) Love waves
(B) Surface waves
(C) Body waves
(D) Rayleigh waves
Verified Correct Answer: (C) Body waves [Waves that propagate through the internal volume/body of the Earth, distinct from boundary-guided surface waves.]
Correct Option: (C) Body waves

Detailed Seismological Forensic Analysis:

  • In global geophysics and seismology, seismic waves are fundamentally classified into two groups according to their propagation medium: Body Waves and Surface Waves.
  • Body waves are defined as elastic vibrations that travel through the interior volume ("body") of the planet along 3D volumetric ray paths.
  • The two distinct wave types comprising body waves are:
    1. P-waves (Primary waves): Compressional/longitudinal body waves that oscillate parallel to ray propagation.
    2. S-waves (Secondary waves): Transverse/shear body waves that oscillate perpendicular to ray propagation.
  • Therefore, $P$ and $S$ waves are collectively designated as Body waves.
Incorrect Distractor: (B) Surface waves

Detailed Refutation:

  • Surface waves are guided boundary waves that propagate exclusively along the outer terrestrial surface or boundary layer of the Earth, with displacement amplitudes decaying exponentially with depth.
  • The surface wave family comprises Rayleigh waves and Love waves.
  • Neither $P$ nor $S$ waves are surface waves; they are interior body waves. Calling $P$ and $S$ waves "surface waves" violates fundamental elastodynamic wave theory.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) Love waves

Detailed Refutation:

  • Love waves (formulated by A.E.H. Love in 1911) are a specific subtype of surface waves characterized by purely horizontal transverse particle motion ($SH$) guided within a low-velocity crustal waveguide.
  • Love waves are not a collective term for $P$ and $S$ waves; they are an entirely separate, dispersion-dependent surface wave mode.
Incorrect Distractor: (D) Rayleigh waves

Detailed Refutation:

  • Rayleigh waves (discovered by Lord Rayleigh in 1885) are another specific subtype of surface waves exhibiting retrograde elliptical motion in the vertical plane.
  • They arise from the coupling of $P$ and $SV$ waves at the free surface boundary and do not constitute a collective classification for $P$ and $S$ waves.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Elastodynamics & Seismic Wave Speeds
Problem 1: Poisson's Ratio and $v_p/v_s$ Ratio

Derive the mathematical relationship between the seismic velocity ratio $(v_p/v_s)$ and Poisson's ratio ($\nu$). Calculate $v_p/v_s$ for a rock with $\nu = 0.28$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

From linear elasticity, Lamé's parameters relate to Young's modulus ($E$) and Poisson's ratio ($\nu$) by:

$$\mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}$$ $$\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\mu} = \frac{\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)} + \frac{2E}{2(1+\nu)}}{\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}} = \frac{2\nu}{1-2\nu} + 2 = \frac{2\nu + 2 - 4\nu}{1-2\nu} = \frac{2 - 2\nu}{1 - 2\nu}$$

Therefore, the velocity ratio is:

$$\frac{v_p}{v_s} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1 - \nu)}{1 - 2\nu}}$$

Substituting $\nu = 0.28$:

$$\frac{v_p}{v_s} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1 - 0.28)}{1 - 2(0.28)}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.72)}{1 - 0.56}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.44}{0.44}} = \sqrt{3.2727} \approx 1.809$$

Final Answer: The velocity ratio $v_p/v_s$ is $1.81$.

Problem 2: Elastic Moduli Calculation from Seismic Speeds

A granitic pluton has measured $P$-wave speed $v_p = 5.5\text{ km/s}$, $S$-wave speed $v_s = 3.2\text{ km/s}$, and rock density $\rho = 2700\text{ kg/m}^3$. Calculate the Shear Modulus ($\mu$) and Bulk Modulus ($K$) in Gigapascals ($\text{GPa}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$\mu = \rho v_s^2 = 2700\text{ kg/m}^3 \times (3200\text{ m/s})^2 = 2700 \times 1.024 \times 10^7 = 2.765 \times 10^{10}\text{ Pa} = 27.65\text{ GPa}$$

Bulk modulus is given by $K = \rho \left(v_p^2 - \frac{4}{3}v_s^2\right)$:

$$v_p^2 = (5500)^2 = 3.025 \times 10^7\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$$ $$\frac{4}{3}v_s^2 = \frac{4}{3}(1.024 \times 10^7) = 1.365 \times 10^7\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$$ $$v_p^2 - \frac{4}{3}v_s^2 = (3.025 - 1.365) \times 10^7 = 1.660 \times 10^7\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$$ $$K = 2700 \times 1.660 \times 10^7 = 4.482 \times 10^{10}\text{ Pa} = 44.82\text{ GPa}$$

Final Answer: Shear Modulus $\mu = 27.65\text{ GPa}$, Bulk Modulus $K = 44.82\text{ GPa}$.

Problem 3: Rayleigh Wave Phase Velocity vs. S-Wave Speed

What is the exact ratio of Rayleigh wave velocity ($v_R$) to $S$-wave velocity ($v_s$) in a Poisson solid ($\nu = 0.25$)?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Rayleigh's cubic equation for a homogeneous isotropic half-space with $\nu = 0.25$ is:

$$\left(\frac{v_R^2}{v_s^2}\right)^3 - 8\left(\frac{v_R^2}{v_s^2}\right)^2 + \left(24 - 16\frac{v_s^2}{v_p^2}\right)\left(\frac{v_R^2}{v_s^2}\right) - 16\left(1 - \frac{v_s^2}{v_p^2}\right) = 0$$

Substituting $v_s^2/v_p^2 = 1/3$ yields the real root:

$$\frac{v_R}{v_s} = \sqrt{2 - \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}} \approx 0.9194$$

Final Answer: Rayleigh wave velocity is approximately $91.94\%$ of the shear wave velocity ($v_R \approx 0.92 v_s$).

Problem 4: S-Wave Fluid Shadow Zone Physics

Why cannot $S$-waves propagate through water or the liquid outer core, whereas sound ($P$-waves) can?

Step-by-Step Solution:

A fluid by definition has zero shear resistance; it cannot sustain static shear stress without continuous flow ($\mu = 0$). Since $v_s = \sqrt{\mu/\rho}$, setting $\mu = 0$ implies $v_s = 0$. In contrast, liquids resist volumetric compression under pressure, possessing a non-zero bulk modulus ($K > 0$, for water $K \approx 2.2\text{ GPa}$). Thus $v_p = \sqrt{K/\rho} \approx 1500\text{ m/s}$ in water.

Problem 5: Apparent Velocity along the Earth's Surface

A plane $P$-wave propagating upward at an incidence angle $i = 30^\circ$ to the vertical strikes the Earth's surface with true speed $v = 6.0\text{ km/s}$. What is the apparent horizontal velocity ($c$) recorded by a surface geophone spread?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Apparent horizontal phase velocity is governed by Snell's law / horizontal slowness:

$$p = \frac{\sin i}{v} = \frac{1}{c} \implies c = \frac{v}{\sin i}$$ $$c = \frac{6.0\text{ km/s}}{\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{6.0}{0.5} = 12.0\text{ km/s}$$

Final Answer: The apparent horizontal velocity is $12.0\text{ km/s}$ (twice the true rock velocity!).

Problem 6: Surface Wave Dispersion Explanation

Why do surface waves on a seismogram appear as an extended, dispersed wave train, while body waves arrive as sharp, impulsive pulses?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Earth's crust exhibits vertical velocity stratification (velocity increases with depth). In surface waves, longer-period (lower frequency) waves have deeper penetration depths ($z \propto \lambda = v/f$) and sample higher-velocity lower crust/mantle, propagating at higher phase velocities. Shorter-period waves are confined to slow surficial soils. This frequency-dependent velocity produces geometric dispersion, stretching the signal into an elongated wave train.

Problem 7: Partition of Energy at Free Surface ($P-SV$ Conversion)

When an upward-traveling $P$-wave strikes the horizontal free surface of the Earth obliquely, what wave modes are generated by reflection?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Because particle displacement must satisfy zero-traction stress boundary conditions ($\sigma_{zz} = 0, \sigma_{xz} = 0$), mode conversion occurs. A single incident $P$-wave reflects into two distinct wave modes: a reflected $P$-wave at angle $i_p$ and a reflected $SV$-wave at a shallower angle $i_s = \arcsin\left(\frac{v_s}{v_p}\sin i_p\right)$. Pure $SH$ waves do not interact with $P$ or $SV$ waves.

Problem 8: Seismic Quality Factor ($Q$) and Anelastic Attenuation

If seismic wave amplitude decays with distance as $A(x) = A_0 e^{-\alpha x}$ where $\alpha = \frac{\pi f}{Q v}$, calculate the percentage amplitude remaining for a $5\text{ Hz}$ $P$-wave ($v_p = 6.0\text{ km/s}$) traveling $60\text{ km}$ through fractured rock with $Q = 100$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$\alpha = \frac{\pi \times 5}{100 \times 6.0} = \frac{15.708}{600} = 0.02618\text{ km}^{-1}$$ $$\alpha x = 0.02618 \times 60 = 1.5708$$ $$\frac{A}{A_0} = e^{-1.5708} \approx 0.2079 = 20.79\%$$

Final Answer: Approximately $20.8\%$ of the original wave amplitude remains after traveling $60\text{ km}$.
