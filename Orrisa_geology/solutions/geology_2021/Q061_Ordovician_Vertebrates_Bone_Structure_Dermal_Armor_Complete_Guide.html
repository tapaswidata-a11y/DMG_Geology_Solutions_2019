1. The Histological Recognition of Ordovician Vertebrates

The dawn of the subphylum Vertebrata in the fossil record is heralded not by articulated skeletons, but by microscopic fragments of mineralized dermal armor. In early Paleozoic strata (specifically the Middle to Late Ordovician, $\sim 470-450\text{ Ma}$, such as the Harding Sandstone of Colorado and the Stairway Sandstone of Australia), jawless stem-vertebrates (ostracoderms like Astraspis, Arandaspis, and Eriptychius) are diagnosed with absolute certainty based on three convergent anatomical lines of evidence:

  • Brain Impressions & Cranial Cartilage: Internal natural casts and impressions of a distinct tripartite brain (forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain) preserved on the visceral surfaces of head shields.
  • Paired Sensory Capsules: Clear skeletal accommodation and impressions for paired visual orbits (eyes) and otic capsules (inner ears containing two semicircular canals).
  • Microscopic Bone Structure (Aspidin & Dentine): The definitive diagnostic hallmark is true bone structure (specifically acellular dermal bone known as aspidin, dentine tubercles, and basal lamellar bone), proving unequivocally that these organisms had evolved a biologically regulated calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) skeletal system.
Figure 61.1 (Michael J. Benton, Fig. 2.4): Histological cross-section of an Ordovician ostracoderm (Astraspis) dermal plate, showing the three classic layers: outer enameloid/dentine caps, middle vascular spongy aspidin bone, and basal compact lamellar bone.
2. Tripartite Architecture of Early Dermal Bone

The dermal armor plates of Ordovician ostracoderms display an invariant three-layered microstructural organization:

The Classical Three-Layered Ostracoderm Exoskeleton
$$\mathbf{\text{Superficial Layer}} \text{ (Dentine/Enameloid Tubercles)} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbf{\text{Middle Layer}} \text{ (Spongy Vascular Aspidin)} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbf{\text{Basal Layer}} \text{ (Lamellar Isopedine)}$$
  • 1. Superficial Layer: Capped by pointed, stellate tubercles composed of mesodentine or orthodentine, capped by a hypermineralized enameloid glaze.
  • 2. Middle Vascular Layer: Highly porous cancellous bone riddled with interconnected vascular canals that housed cutaneous blood vessels and lateral line neuromast sensory canals. Composed of aspidin—a dense, unmineralized or acellular calcified collagenous matrix without osteocyte lacunae.
  • 3. Basal Layer: Dense, horizontally stratified compact bone (isopedine) formed of stacked lamellae of collagen fibers impregnated with microcrystalline hydroxyapatite $\text{Ca}_5(\text{PO}_4)_3(\text{OH})$.

Official Examination Question
Question 61
It is possible to recognize vertebrate fossils as early as Ordovician on the basis of evidences of brain, paired sensory organs and_____.
(A) anatomy
(B) bodyparts
(C) carbon dating
(D) bone structure
Verified Correct Answer: (D) bone structure [Ordovician vertebrates are formally recognized by the microscopic histology of their dermal armor plates, which display true bone structure (aspidin and dentine) surrounding the brain and sensory capsules.]
Correct Option: (D) bone structure

Detailed Paleontological Forensic Analysis:

  • In vertebrate paleontology (Benton 2014, Ch. 2; Romer & Parsons 1986; Janvier 1996), the earliest definitive diagnostic physical remains of vertebrates from the Ordovician period consist of bony dermal armor fragments.
  • These fossils are identified as vertebrates through three correlated anatomical features:
    1. Endocranial impressions of a differentiated brain.
    2. Skeletal capsules for paired sensory organs (eyes and inner ear otic capsules).
    3. Diagnostic microscopic bone structure (specifically cellular/acellular bone called aspidin with dentine tubercles and vascular canals).
  • Bone is a unique, exclusive biological tissue synthesized only by vertebrates; its presence in Ordovician rocks definitively identifies the specimen as a vertebrate.
  • Therefore, Option (D) bone structure is the scientifically exact correct answer.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) anatomy

Detailed Refutation:

  • "Anatomy" is an overarching general term describing the bodily structure of all living things, from bacteria and corals to humans. It does not name a specific physical fossil tissue or skeletal material.
Incorrect Distractor: (B) bodyparts

Detailed Refutation:

  • "Bodyparts" is an informal, non-scientific vernacular term. Vertebrate identification requires precise cellular histological markers, not vague references to body parts.
Incorrect Distractor: (C) carbon dating

Detailed Refutation:

  • Radiocarbon dating ($^{14}\text{C}$) has a half-life of only $5,730\text{ years}$ and is completely useless for rocks older than $\sim 50,000\text{ years}$.
  • Applying carbon dating to Ordovician rocks ($\sim 450\text{ million years old}$) is physically impossible by four orders of magnitude.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Early Vertebrate Evolution & Bone Histology
Problem 1: Hydroxyapatite vs. Calcium Carbonate in Shells

Why did early vertebrates evolve a calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) skeleton rather than the calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) used by invertebrates (molluscs, brachiopods)?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Vertebrates are active, high-metabolic animals that generate large amounts of lactic acid during anaerobic muscular exertion. Calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) readily dissolves under acidic conditions ($\text{pH} < 7$), which would cause skeletal dissolution. In contrast, calcium phosphate / hydroxyapatite [$\text{Ca}_5(\text{PO}_4)_3(\text{OH})$] is chemically stable at lower pH, resists lactic acidosis, and serves as an indispensable physiological metabolic buffer and phosphate reservoir.

Problem 2: Sharpey's Fibers in Ostracoderm Bone

What are Sharpey's fibers and how do they appear in thin sections of fossil ostracoderm plates?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Sharpey's fibers are bundles of collagenous connective tissue that anchor the external dermal armor to the underlying subdermal muscles and connective tissue. In petrographic thin sections, they appear as microscopic unmineralized or mineralized radial tubules crossing the basal compact lamellae at right angles.

Problem 3: Conodont Elements Composition

What are conodont elements, and why were they classified as vertebrates?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Conodonts are microscopic tooth-like feeding apparatuses ($0.1-2\text{ mm}$) composed of calcium phosphate (dahllite / fluorapatite). Histological studies revealed that conodont elements possess true cellular bone-like lamellar tissue and enameloid caps, while soft-bodied fossils from Granton (Scotland) revealed an eel-like animal with a notochord, chevron-shaped myomeres, and large paired eyes, proving conodonts are primitive jawless vertebrates.

Problem 4: Semicircular Canals in Ostracoderms

How many semicircular canals did Ordovician ostracoderms possess in their inner ear, compared to modern jawed vertebrates?

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Hagfishes: 1 semicircular canal.
  • Lampreys and Ostracoderms (Agnatha): 2 semicircular canals (anterior and posterior), providing pitch and roll balance.
  • Gnathostomes (Jawed Vertebrates): 3 semicircular canals (anterior, posterior, and horizontal), enabling full 3D spatial equilibrium.
Problem 5: Aspidin vs. Osteocyte Lacunae

How does aspidin differ from normal haversian bone under high-magnification polarized light?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Aspidin is acellular bone. It contains criss-crossing collagen fiber bundles and vascular spaces, but is completely devoid of spider-like osteocyte cell bodies (lacunae) and their canaliculi. Normal Haversian bone contains concentric osteons centered around Haversian canals containing living osteocytes trapped within lacunae.

Problem 6: Origin of Jaws from Branchial Arches

Which visceral branchial gill arch was modified during the transition from jawless ostracoderms to jawed gnathostomes to form the upper and lower jaws?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The first mandibular arch was modified: its dorsal element (epibranchial) evolved into the upper jaw (palatoquadrate cartilage), and its ventral element (ceratobranchial) evolved into the lower jaw (Meckel's cartilage). The second arch became the hyoid arch supporting the jaws.

Problem 7: Sensory Lateral Line Canal System

What hydrodynamic function did the porous sensory grooves on Astraspis dermal plates perform?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The grooves housed the lateral line sensory system. Neuromast hair cells inside the canals detected micro-vibrations, water currents, and low-frequency pressure waves in the surrounding water, allowing the jawless fish to navigate in murky water and evade predatory giant eurypterids.

Problem 8: Harding Sandstone Depositional Realm

In what depositional environment was the fossiliferous Harding Sandstone deposited?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Sedimentological structures (herringbone cross-bedding, tidal bundles, wave ripples) indicate an estuarine, intertidal to shallow nearshore marine environment, supporting the hypothesis that early vertebrate biomineralization originated in shallow coastal marine waters rather than fresh water.
