1. Definition, Historical Roots & The Hydrothermal-Metasomatic Equivalence

In metamorphic petrology and ore geology (Winter, 2014, pp. 635–645; Korzhinskii, 1970; Bangar, p. 81), hydrothermal metamorphism refers to the profound mineralogical and chemical alteration of rocks driven by the pervasive circulation of hot, chemically reactive aqueous solutions and supercritical fluids ($T \approx 100\text{--}600^\circ\text{C}$).

In geological literature, the universally established scientific synonym for hydrothermal metamorphism is metasomatism (derived from the Greek $\mu\epsilon\tau\acute{\alpha}$, meta, denoting change, and $\sigma\tilde{\omega}\mu\alpha$, soma, meaning body).

The term was introduced in 1826 by the German mineralogist Carl Friedrich Naumann and rigorously formulated thermodynamically by V.M. Goldschmidt (1922) and D.S. Korzhinskii (1970) as an open-system process of rock replacement where chemical components are introduced and removed simultaneously by hydrothermal diffusion and advective fluid percolation while the rock remains in the solid state.

Fundamental Contrast: Isochemical Metamorphism vs. Metasomatism

Characteristic Standard Regional Metamorphism (Autochemical) Hydrothermal Metamorphism / Metasomatism
System Behavior Closed System (except for release of volatile $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$) Open System (free exchange of major non-volatile chemical components)
Bulk Rock Composition Conserved; bulk chemistry identical to parental protolith Radically transformed; elements ($\text{Si, Fe, Mg, Ca, Na, K, metals}$) added/subtracted
Driving Mechanism Regional geothermal heat ($\Delta T$) and tectonic differential stress Chemical potential gradients ($\Delta\mu_i$) and advective hydrothermal fluid flow
Volume Relationships Substantial volume change ($\Delta V \ne 0$) under lithostatic compaction Lindgren's Law of Constant Volume: equal volume replacement via ionic exchange
2. Canonical Hydrothermal Metasomatic Processes in the Geological Record

Major Classes of Metasomatism

  • Serpentinization & Steatitization: Hydrothermal hydration of ultramafic peridotites by hot seawater ($T \approx 200\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$): $$2\text{Mg}_2\text{SiO}_4\text{ (forsterite)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Mg}_3\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5(\text{OH})_4\text{ (serpentine)} + \text{Mg(OH)}_2$$ Further $\text{CO}_2$ infiltration transforms serpentine into talc-carbonate rock (soapstone).
  • Greisenization: High-temperature ($400\text{--}500^\circ\text{C}$) auto-metasomatic alteration of granitic cupolas by post-magmatic, fluorine- and boron-rich hydrothermal fluids, converting feldspars into an aggregate of quartz + muscovite (lithium-rich) + topaz + fluorite + cassiterite ($\text{SnO}_2$).
  • Spilitization (Ocean-Floor Metamorphism): Interaction of basaltic lavas with circulating ocean floor seawater at mid-ocean ridges, replacing calcic plagioclase with albite and pyroxene with chlorite/actinolite: $$\text{CaAl}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_8\text{ (anorthite)} + \text{Na}^+ + \text{Si}^{4+} \rightarrow \text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_8\text{ (albite)} + \text{Ca}^{2+} + \text{Al}^{3+}$$
  • Fenitization: Alkaline metasomatism around carbonatite and nepheline syenite complexes, where ultra-alkaline fluids enrich host granites in aegirine, sodic amphibole, and nepheline.
  • Skarnification: Contact metasomatism where silica- and iron-rich hydrothermal fluids from a granitic magma react with host carbonate rocks (limestone/dolomite).

Official Examination Question — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2021
Question 118: The other name of hydrothermal metamorphism in geological literature is :
(A) Autochemical metamorphism
(B) Allochemical metamorphism
(C) Metasomatism
(D) All of these
Verified Correct Answer: (C) Metasomatism
Forensic Analysis & Refutation of Options
Option (A) Refuted — Autochemical Metamorphism

Incorrect: Autochemical (isochemical) metamorphism refers to standard regional metamorphism wherein the bulk chemical composition of the rock remains constant (closed system, except for the escape of volatile $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$). Hydrothermal metamorphism, by contrast, is characterized by extensive chemical addition and removal of non-volatile elements via fluid circulation. Therefore, autochemical is the exact antithesis of hydrothermal metamorphism.

Option (B) Evaluated — Allochemical Metamorphism

Incorrect as a Specific Synonym: While hydrothermal metamorphism is broad-brush allochemical in nature (meaning "different chemistry"), "allochemical metamorphism" is merely a general descriptive modifier. In geological literature and textbook nomenclature (Winter, 2014, p. 635; Bangar, p. 81; Korzhinskii, 1970), the formal, universally recognized substantive name used interchangeably with hydrothermal metamorphism is Metasomatism.

Option (C) Confirmed — Metasomatism

Correct: In all authoritative geological curricula and petrological treatises (Winter, 2014, p. 635; Bangar, p. 81; Mahapatra, p. 126; Bucher & Grapes, 2011), metasomatism is the universally accepted and widely cited alternative name for hydrothermal metamorphism. It rigorously defines the process of substantial chemical composition change and mineral replacement brought about by circulating hydrothermal aqueous solutions.

Option (D) Refuted — All of These

Incorrect: Because Option (A) is completely false and diametrically opposed to hydrothermal processes, Option (D) is logically invalid. The official and scientific correct answer is exclusively (C) Metasomatism.

Exam-Standard Practice Problems & Analytical Derivations

Practice Problem 1

Define Lindgren's "Law of Constant Volume" in metasomatism and explain how a granite can be converted into a greisen without changing its physical bulk volume.

Solution: Waldemar Lindgren (1918) established the Law of Constant Volume: metasomatic replacement in solid rocks proceeds on an equal-volume basis ($\Delta V_{bulk} \approx 0$). Because the host rock is confined under crustal pressure, replacement occurs by simultaneous, molecule-by-molecule (or ion-by-ion) dissolution of reactant minerals and precipitation of product minerals. As acidic hydrothermal fluids dissolve K-feldspar in granite, fluorine-rich mica (muscovite/lepidolite), quartz, and topaz precipitate directly within the created sub-microscopic void spaces, preserving original rock dimensions, fault markers, and structural textures without expansion or collapse.

Practice Problem 2

Distinguish between "diffusional metasomatism" and "infiltrational (advective) metasomatism" according to D.S. Korzhinskii's theory.

Solution: - Diffusional Metasomatism: Mass transfer occurs via chemical diffusion of dissolved ions through stagnant, stationary pore fluids across a chemical potential gradient ($\Delta\mu_i$). Metasomatic alteration zones are typically narrow (millimeters to centimeters), and zone boundaries exhibit continuous chemical gradients. - Infiltrational (Advective) Metasomatism: Mass transfer is driven by the physical, hydrodynamic flow (percolation) of hydrothermal solutions through interconnected fractures and pores under a hydraulic pressure gradient ($\nabla P$). Metasomatic zones can extend for hundreds of meters to kilometers, and zone boundaries are characterized by sharp, step-like compositional discontinuities.

Practice Problem 3

What is "spilitization" and what chemical exchange occurs between ocean-floor basalts and heated seawater?

Solution: Spilitization is low-temperature ($200\text{--}350^\circ\text{C}$) ocean-floor hydrothermal metasomatism of submarine basaltic lavas. Heated seawater circulating through oceanic crust exchanges ions with calcic plagioclase: $$\text{CaAl}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_8\text{ (anorthite)} + \text{Na}^+ + \text{Si}^{4+} \rightarrow \text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_8\text{ (albite)} + \text{Ca}^{2+} + \text{Al}^{3+}$$ The basalt absorbs $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ from seawater while leaching $\text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Fe}^{2+}, \text{Mn}^{2+}, \text{Cu}$, and $\text{Zn}$ into the hydrothermal fluid, converting normal basalt into an albite-chlorite rock termed spilite and feeding seafloor "black smoker" massive sulfide deposits.

Practice Problem 4

Explain the process of "greisenization" and name the primary economic metal ore associated with greisen deposits.

Solution: Greisenization is high-temperature ($400\text{--}500^\circ\text{C}$) post-magmatic hydrothermal metasomatism affecting the apical cupolas of highly fractionated, peraluminous granitic plutons. Volatile-rich acidic fluids enriched in $F, B, Li, H_2O$ attack granitic feldspars, converting them into a quartz-white mica (muscovite/zinnwaldite)-topaz-fluorite rock called greisen. The primary economic metal ore precipitated in greisens is tin (cassiterite, $\text{SnO}_2$) and tungsten (wolframite, $\text{(Fe,Mn)WO}_4$).

Practice Problem 5

How does metasomatic fenitization provide an exploration guide for carbonatite-hosted rare earth element (REE) and niobium deposits?

Solution: Fenitization produces broad, halo-like metasomatic aureoles surrounding intrusive carbonatite and alkaline complexes. Highly mobile, mantle-derived alkaline fluids rich in $\text{Na, K, Fe}^{3+}$, and volatiles invade surrounding granitic country rocks, converting quartz and biotite into aegirine, sodic amphibole (riebeckite/arfvedsonite), and alkali feldspar. Because the fenite alteration halo extends up to several kilometers beyond the central intrusion, mapping fenite zones directly vectors exploration geologists toward concealed, high-grade carbonatite plugs hosting rich REE and niobium (pyrochlore) mineralization.

Practice Problem 6

What is "propylitic alteration" in hydrothermal porphyry copper systems and what mineral assemblage characterizes it?

Solution: Propylitic alteration is a low-temperature ($200\text{--}300^\circ\text{C}$) hydrothermal metasomatic zone forming the outermost, distal halo around porphyry copper-gold deposits. It results from the interaction of cooling igneous host rocks with circulating meteoric hydrothermal fluids. The diagnostic mineral assemblage consists of epidote + chlorite + calcite + albite $\pm$ pyrite, imparting a distinctive dull green hue to the altered rocks.

Practice Problem 7

Why does metasomatism typically produce monomineralic or oligomineralic rock zones (e.g., pure dunite, pure quartzite, pure skarn zones), whereas regional metamorphism produces polymineralic assemblages?

Solution: Governed by Korzhinskii's Phase Rule for Open Systems: $$F = C_{inert} - P + 2$$ In an open metasomatic system, perfectly mobile components ($C_{mobile}$, whose chemical potentials are externally fixed by the fluid) do not count toward degrees of freedom ($C_{inert}$). Because many components ($\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{CO}_2, \text{K, Na, Ca}$) become mobile, the number of inert components ($C_{inert}$) drops drastically. To satisfy the phase rule, the number of stable phases ($P$) must also drop, causing the progressive elimination of mineral phases and generating simplified, monomineralic or oligomineralic metasomatic reaction zones.

Practice Problem 8

What is a "reaction skarn" and how does it differ from an "infiltrational ore skarn"?

Solution: - Reaction Skarn (Bimetasomatic): Forms along the contact between two chemically contrasting rocks (e.g., shale and limestone) via local chemical diffusion across the contact; fluid serves only as a diffusion medium; alteration zones are thin (centimeters) and typically barren of ore. - Infiltrational Ore Skarn: Forms by the large-scale, advective flow of magmatic-hydrothermal fluids carrying dissolved silica, iron, and base metals into reactive carbonate host rocks; alteration zones extend tens to hundreds of meters and host major economic ore bodies of copper, tungsten, iron, and zinc.
