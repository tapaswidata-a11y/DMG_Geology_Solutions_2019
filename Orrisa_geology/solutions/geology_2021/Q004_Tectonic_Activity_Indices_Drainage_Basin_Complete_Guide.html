1. The Quantitative Morphotectonic Framework (Keller & Pinter, 2002)

In regions subject to active crustal deformation, earthquakes, and fault propagation, landscape evolution is driven by the dynamic competition between tectonic rock uplift and fluvial/hillslope incision. Because tectonic processes operate over timescales of $10^3 - 10^6\text{ years}$—far exceeding instrumental seismic records—geomorphologists rely on Tectonic Activity Indices derived from digital elevation models (DEMs) and drainage basin metrics to identify, quantify, and map differential uplift and seismic hazard.

"Tectonic geomorphology evaluates the interplay between tectonic and surface processes that shape the landscape in regions of active crustal deformation. Quantitative morphotectonic indices provide a rapid and powerful reconnaissance tool for evaluating relative tectonic activity across drainage basins." — Edward A. Keller and Nicholas Pinter, Active Tectonics: Earthquakes, Uplift, and Landscape (Prentice Hall, 2002, p. 121)

The internationally standardized morphotectonic suite formalized by Keller & Pinter (2002) includes four primary morphometric indices: Drainage Basin Asymmetry ($AF$), Stream Length-Gradient Index ($SL$), Mountain-Front Sinuosity ($S_{mf}$), and the Valley Floor Width-to-Valley Height Ratio ($V_f$).

2. Mathematical Derivations & Geodynamic Sensitivity of the Four Indices

Each index diagnoses a specific structural and erosional manifestation of active deformation:

1. Asymmetry Factor ($AF$) — Hare & Gardner (1985)
$$AF = 100 \times \left(\frac{A_r}{A_t}\right)$$

Where $A_r$ is the basin area on the downstream right of the trunk stream, and $A_t$ is total basin area. Under tectonic stability, $AF \approx 50\%$. Significant deviations ($AF > 50\%$ or $AF < 50\%$) indicate lateral tectonic tilting orthogonal to stream flow.

2. Stream Length-Gradient Index ($SL$) — Hack (1973)
$$SL = \left(\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta L}\right) \cdot L$$

Where $\Delta H / \Delta L$ is the reach channel gradient, and $L$ is total channel length from the drainage divide to the midpoint of the reach. Exceptionally high $SL$ anomalies highlight active fault crossings, localized surface uplift, or tectonic knickpoints.

3. Mountain-Front Sinuosity ($S_{mf}$) — Bull & McFadden (1977)
$$S_{mf} = \frac{L_{mf}}{L_s}$$

Where $L_{mf}$ is actual length of the mountain front along the foot of the range, and $L_s$ is straight-line length. Active range-bounding normal/thrust faults produce straight fronts with $S_{mf} \approx 1.0 - 1.5$ (Class 1); inactive embayed fronts have $S_{mf} > 2.5$ (Class 3).

4. Valley Floor Width-to-Valley Height Ratio ($V_f$) — Bull (1977)
$$V_f = \frac{2 V_{fw}}{(E_{ld} - E_{sc}) + (E_{rd} - E_{sc})}$$

Where $V_{fw}$ is valley floor width, $E_{ld}$ and $E_{rd}$ are divide elevations on left/right, and $E_{sc}$ is valley floor elevation. Low $V_f (< 1.0)$ indicates deep, narrow V-shaped gorges undergoing rapid tectonic incision; high $V_f (\gg 1.0)$ indicates broad, flat valleys dominated by lateral planation.

3. Index of Relative Tectonic Activity (IRAT) & Synthesis

Because individual indices may be influenced by local lithological contrasts or structural anisotropies, modern active tectonics investigations combine these metrics into an integrated Index of Relative Tectonic Activity (IRAT):

$$IRAT = \frac{S_{mf} + V_f + AF + SL + Hypsometric\ Integral}{N}$$

This multi-parameter index classifies mountainous terrains and drainage basins into three distinct tectonic activity regimes:

  • Class 1 (High Tectonic Activity): Ongoing rapid uplift ($> 1\text{ mm/yr}$), high seismicity, straight mountain fronts ($S_{mf} < 1.4$), deeply incised V-shaped canyons ($V_f < 0.5$), and pronounced $SL$ knickpoint anomalies.
  • Class 2 (Moderate Tectonic Activity): Intermediate uplift rates ($0.1 - 0.5\text{ mm/yr}$), partially embayed fronts ($1.4 \le S_{mf} \le 2.5$), and moderately incised valleys ($0.5 \le V_f \le 1.5$).
  • Class 3 (Low / Inactive Tectonic Regime): Tectonic quiescence, extensive lateral erosion, sinuous mountain fronts ($S_{mf} > 2.5$), wide U-shaped alluvial plains ($V_f > 1.5$), and symmetrical basins ($AF \approx 50\%$).

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 4)
Q4. Which of the following indices belong to 'Tectonic Activity Indices' used for morphotectonic analysis of a drainage basin? (i) Drainage basin asymmetry, (ii) Stream Length-Gradient Index (SL Index), (iii) Mountain-front sinuosity, (iv) Ratio of valley floor width to valley height. Choose the correct set of answer from the following options :
(A) (i), (ii) and (iv)
(B) (ii) and (iv)
(C) (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv)  ✔
(D) (ii), (iii) and (iv)
✔
Correct Answer: (C) (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv)
All four parameters—(i) Drainage basin asymmetry ($AF$), (ii) Stream Length-Gradient Index ($SL$), (iii) Mountain-front sinuosity ($S_{mf}$), and (iv) Ratio of valley floor width to valley height ($V_f$)—constitute the canonical suite of morphotectonic indices established by Bull & McFadden (1977) and Keller & Pinter (2002).
(A) (i), (ii) and (iv) Incorrect (Omits Mountain-Front Sinuosity)
Why this option is incorrect: This option omits parameter (iii) Mountain-front sinuosity ($S_{mf}$). $S_{mf}$ is one of the oldest and most fundamental tectonic indices in geomorphology (Bull & McFadden, 1977), directly quantifying the activity of range-bounding piedmont fault scarps.
(B) (ii) and (iv) Incorrect (Severely Incomplete Subset)
Why this option is incorrect: While $SL$ and $V_f$ are valid indices, this option completely ignores drainage basin asymmetry ($AF$) and mountain-front sinuosity ($S_{mf}$), excluding half of the standard analytical suite.
(C) (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv) Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Every single listed metric belongs to the primary suite of quantitative indices used in modern tectonic geomorphology. In Chapter 5 of Active Tectonics, Keller & Pinter explicitly group these exact four parameters together as the indispensable toolkit for morphotectonic basin analysis.
(D) (ii), (iii) and (iv) Incorrect (Omits Drainage Basin Asymmetry)
Why this option is incorrect: This option omits parameter (i) Drainage basin asymmetry ($AF$). $AF$ is essential for detecting broad tectonic ground tilting and differential block rotation perpendicular to the master drainage channel.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical $AF$ Calculation): A drainage basin has a total catchment area $A_t = 600\text{ km}^2$. The catchment area lying to the right of the master trunk stream (looking downstream) is measured as $A_r = 432\text{ km}^2$. Calculate the Asymmetry Factor ($AF$) and infer the tectonic deformation of the basin.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $AF = 72\%$; Indicates strong lateral tectonic tilting down toward the right bank.
    Calculation:
    $$AF = 100 \times \left(\frac{A_r}{A_t}\right) = 100 \times \left(\frac{432}{600}\right) = 72\%$$ The deviation $|AF - 50| = |72 - 50| = 22\% > 15\%$, placing the basin into Class 1 (High Tectonic Activity). The stream has migrated toward the left due to ground tilting downward to the right.
  • Practice Q2 (Numerical $SL$ Calculation): Along a mountain stream reach, the channel drops in elevation by $\Delta H = 50\text{ m}$ over a reach length $\Delta L = 400\text{ m}$. The total length from the drainage headwaters divide to the reach midpoint is $L = 6000\text{ m}$. Calculate the Hack Stream Length-Gradient Index ($SL$).
    ✔ Worked Answer: $SL = 750\text{ m}$.
    Calculation:
    Reach gradient $S = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta L} = \frac{50\text{ m}}{400\text{ m}} = 0.125$.
    $$SL = \left(\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta L}\right) \cdot L = 0.125 \times 6000\text{ m} = 750\text{ m}$$ Because $SL = 750\text{ m} > 500\text{ m}$, this reach represents an anomalously steep tectonic knickpoint, likely indicating an active fault crossing or localized surface uplift.
  • Practice Q3 (Numerical $S_{mf}$ Classification): A mountain range front has a straight-line end-to-end distance $L_s = 15.0\text{ km}$. The surveyed actual mountain-piedmont junction length is $L_{mf} = 18.3\text{ km}$. Calculate $S_{mf}$ and assign the tectonic activity class under the Bull & McFadden (1977) scheme.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $S_{mf} = 1.22$; Assigned to Class 1 (Tectonically Active Mountain Front).
    Calculation:
    $$S_{mf} = \frac{L_{mf}}{L_s} = \frac{18.3\text{ km}}{15.0\text{ km}} = 1.22$$ Because $S_{mf} < 1.40$, tectonic uplift along the range-bounding fault outpaces stream incision and pediment embayment, maintaining a nearly straight fault-line scarp.
  • Practice Q4 (Numerical $V_f$ Valley Inversion): Cross-sectional profiling across a valley yields: valley floor width $V_{fw} = 30\text{ m}$, left divide elevation $E_{ld} = 850\text{ m}$, right divide elevation $E_{rd} = 810\text{ m}$, and valley floor elevation $E_{sc} = 550\text{ m}$. Calculate the $V_f$ ratio and interpret the geomorphic state.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $V_f = 0.107$; Indicates intense, rapid vertical channel incision driven by active tectonic uplift.
    Calculation:
    Left relief $= 850 - 550 = 300\text{ m}$; Right relief $= 810 - 550 = 260\text{ m}$.
    $$V_f = \frac{2 V_{fw}}{(E_{ld} - E_{sc}) + (E_{rd} - E_{sc})} = \frac{2(30)}{300 + 260} = \frac{60}{560} \approx 0.107$$ Since $V_f \ll 0.5$, the cross-section is an acute V-shaped canyon characteristic of rapid tectonic incisive response to crustal uplift.
  • Practice Q5: How is Hack's Law expressed mathematically, and what does it relate in drainage basin morphometry?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $L = C \cdot A^h$ (where $L$ is mainstream length, $A$ is basin area, $C \approx 1.4$, and $h \approx 0.55 - 0.60$).
    Explanation: Hack's Law proves that drainage basins become increasingly elongated rather than geometrically circular as they grow in size, because the exponent $h > 0.5$.
  • Practice Q6: What does a high Hypsometric Integral ($HI > 0.60$) indicate regarding Strahler's cycle of landscape erosion?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Indicates a "youthful" landscape experiencing active tectonic uplift where most rock mass has not yet been eroded.
    Explanation: The hypsometric integral represents the volume of unconsumed bedrock remaining beneath the hypsometric curve. $HI > 0.60$ is diagnostic of young, actively uplifting mountain ranges; mature landscapes have $0.35 \le HI \le 0.60$; and old peneplains have $HI < 0.35$.
  • Practice Q7: How can a tectonic knickpoint be distinguished from a lithologic knickpoint along a longitudinal stream profile?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Tectonic knickpoints migrate upstream across varying rock types at similar elevations across adjacent parallel valleys; lithologic knickpoints are stationary and coincide strictly with resistant bedrock contacts.
    Explanation: A transient knickpoint generated by a pulse of fault uplift propagates upstream into the landscape independently of underlying rock hardness, appearing as a coherent elevation step across neighboring catchments.
  • Practice Q8: Why do paired fluvial terraces signify active tectonic uplift or base-level fall, whereas unpaired terraces typically indicate lateral channel meandering?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Paired terraces occur at identical elevations on opposite sides of a valley, proving rapid, symmetrical downcutting too fast for lateral erosion; unpaired terraces form when a meandering river cuts down slowly while simultaneously shifting laterally.
    Explanation: Tectonic pulses accelerate stream incision rates, stranding former floodplains synchronously on both banks at matching elevations (paired terraces).
