1. Definition, Microstructure & Crystallographic Architecture of Antiperthite

The term antiperthite describes an intimate subsolidus intergrowth texture in which a host crystal of plagioclase feldspar (typically oligoclase, andesine, or albite) contains oriented, bleb-like, rod-like, rectangular, or irregular inclusions of potassium feldspar (orthoclase or microcline).

It is the exact crystallographic and structural mirror-image of perthite:

The Fundamental Perthite–Antiperthite Distinction:
$$\mathbf{\text{Perthite}} = \mathbf{\text{Potassium Feldspar Host}} \quad \text{containing exsolved blebs/lamellae of } \mathbf{\text{Plagioclase (Albite)}}$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Antiperthite}} = \mathbf{\text{Plagioclase Host}} \quad \text{containing exsolved blebs/inclusions of } \mathbf{\text{Potassium Feldspar (Kfs)}}$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Mesoperthite}} = \text{Roughly equal proportions } (\sim 50:50) \text{ of mutually intergrown K-feldspar and Plagioclase}$$
Figure 81.1 — The Ternary Feldspar Solvus and Subsolidus Exsolution: Authentic textbook diagram from J.D. Winter (Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology, Ch. 6, Fig. 6-16). Illustrating the subsolidus miscibility gap (solvus) across the $Ab\text{--}Or\text{--}An$ system where homogeneous high-temperature feldspars unmix into perthite (K-rich host) or antiperthite (Na-Ca-rich host).

Optical Identification Under the Polarizing Microscope

  • Host Grain: Displays continuous polysynthetic albite twinning ($\{010\}$ plane) or combined Carlsbad-albite twinning across the entire grain.
  • Exsolved Inclusions: Appear as tiny, discrete, rectangular, bleb-like, or flame-shaped islands of K-feldspar scattered throughout the plagioclase host. Under cross-polarized light (XPL), the K-feldspar inclusions extinguish at a distinctly different angle from the host plagioclase and often display subtle gridiron/tartan twinning (microcline) or remain untwinned with lower refractive index ($n < 1.525$).
2. Thermodynamics of the Feldspar Solvus: Spinodal Decomposition & Nucleation

At elevated magmatic temperatures ($T > 800\text{--}900^\circ\text{C}$), alkali feldspars and ternary feldspars form a complete, continuous solid solution because thermal kinetic energy overcomes the strain caused by the size mismatch between the large potassium ion ($r_{\text{K}^+} = 1.38\text{ \AA}$) and smaller sodium ($r_{\text{Na}^+} = 1.02\text{ \AA}$) and calcium ($r_{\text{Ca}^{2+}} = 1.00\text{ \AA}$) ions.

Subsolidus Phase Separation Across the Miscibility Gap

As the crystalline rock cools below the solvus temperature ($T < 650\text{--}700^\circ\text{C}$), the excess Gibbs free energy of mixing ($\Delta G_{\text{mix}} = \Delta H_{\text{mix}} - T\Delta S_{\text{mix}}$) becomes positive due to the high enthalpy of lattice strain ($\Delta H_{\text{mix}} > 0$). The single homogeneous solid solution becomes thermodynamically unstable and unmixes:

  • Binodal (Solvus Boundary): The equilibrium boundary where two distinct coexisting feldspar phases are in thermodynamic equilibrium ($\mu_i^{\text{plag}} = \mu_i^{\text{Kfs}}$).
  • Spinodal Boundary ($\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial X^2} = 0$): Inside the chemical spinodal, there is no activation energy barrier to phase separation. The homogeneous lattice spontaneously breaks down via uphill diffusion (spinodal decomposition), producing ultrafine, periodic, coherent exsolution lamellae (cryptoperthites).
  • Nucleation and Growth: In the metastable region between the binodal and spinodal ($\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial X^2} > 0$), unmixing requires overcoming an activation energy barrier, producing discrete, isolated blebs or patches of K-feldspar within plagioclase—the classic patchy antiperthite.
3. Petrologic Environments & Geothermobarometry of Antiperthite

Antiperthitic plagioclase is not ubiquitous; its presence serves as a diagnostic fingerprint of specific, extreme high-temperature and water-undersaturated geological environments:

  • Charnockite and Granulite Terrains: In granulite facies high-grade metamorphic complexes (e.g., Eastern Ghats, Southern Granulite Terrain of India), anhydrous conditions and high temperatures ($750\text{--}900^\circ\text{C}$) permit substantial substitution of potassium into plagioclase at peak conditions. Subsequent slow cooling yields spectacular antiperthitic andesine in enderbites and acid charnockites.
  • Proterozoic Anorthosite Massifs: Massive, plagioclase-dominated cumulates of massif-type anorthosites (e.g., Chilka Lake anorthosite, Odisha) typically contain coarse antiperthitic labradorite/andesine formed by slow subsolidus cooling of dry, mantle-derived parent magmas.
  • Two-Feldspar Geothermometry: The partitioning of albite component between coexisting perthitic K-feldspar and antiperthitic plagioclase forms the basis of the famous Two-Feldspar Geothermometer (Stormer, 1975; Fuhrman & Lindsley, 1988), allowing quantitative derivation of peak metamorphic and magmatic temperatures.

Official Examination Question
Question 81
An inter-growth texture in which plagioclase contains inclusions of potash feldspar is known as :
(A) Antiperthite
(B) Perthite
(C) Graphic texture
(D) Ophitic
CORRECT ANSWER: (A)
Antiperthite: A subsolidus exsolution texture where a host crystal of plagioclase feldspar contains lamellae, blebs, or irregular inclusions of exsolved potassium feldspar.
Forensic Analysis of Answer Choices
(A) Antiperthite — CORRECT

Antiperthite is the exact, universally accepted petrographic definition for an intergrowth where plagioclase acts as the host mineral containing exsolved inclusions, blebs, or lamellae of potassium feldspar. It is commonly observed in high-grade granulite-facies rocks, charnockites (especially enderbite), and massif-type anorthosites (Winter, 2014, p. 95; Mahapatra, p. 119; Deer, Howie & Zussman, p. 390).

(B) Perthite — INCORRECT

Perthite is the inverse texture: the host crystal is potassium feldspar (microcline or orthoclase), which contains exsolved lamellae, strings, or blebs of sodic plagioclase (albite). Mixing up perthite and antiperthite is one of the most common errors in optical mineralogy.

(C) Graphic texture — INCORRECT

Graphic texture is a primary cotectic magmatic intergrowth consisting of angular, cuneiform, runic prisms of quartz embedded within a potassium feldspar host in granitic pegmatites. It does not involve a plagioclase-potash feldspar exsolution relationship.

(D) Ophitic — INCORRECT

Ophitic texture is a magmatic crystallization texture characteristic of hypabyssal dolerite/diabase, where laths of plagioclase are completely enclosed poikilitically within large plates of clinopyroxene (augite).

Problem 1: Ternary Feldspar Modal Reconstitution to Homogeneous Protolith

Microprobe analysis of an antiperthitic feldspar grain in an enderbite from the Eastern Ghats yields a plagioclase host of composition $\text{Ab}_{68}\text{An}_{30}\text{Or}_2$ occupying $85\text{ vol}\%$, and exsolved K-feldspar blebs of composition $\text{Or}_{92}\text{Ab}_7\text{An}_1$ occupying $15\text{ vol}\%$. Assuming identical molar densities, calculate the bulk ternary composition of the original homogeneous high-temperature feldspar prior to subsolidus unmixing.

Step-by-Step Mass Balance Calculation:
Bulk mole fraction of each end-member component is given by: $$X_i^{\text{bulk}} = (0.85 \times X_i^{\text{host}}) + (0.15 \times X_i^{\text{exsolved}})$$
1. Orthoclase ($\text{Or}$) Component: $$X_{\text{Or}}^{\text{bulk}} = (0.85 \times 0.02) + (0.15 \times 0.92) = 0.017 + 0.138 = \mathbf{0.155} \implies \mathbf{15.5\text{ mol}\%}$$
2. Albite ($\text{Ab}$) Component: $$X_{\text{Ab}}^{\text{bulk}} = (0.85 \times 0.68) + (0.15 \times 0.07) = 0.578 + 0.0105 = \mathbf{0.5885} \implies \mathbf{58.85\text{ mol}\%}$$
3. Anorthite ($\text{An}$) Component: $$X_{\text{An}}^{\text{bulk}} = (0.85 \times 0.30) + (0.15 \times 0.01) = 0.255 + 0.0015 = \mathbf{0.2565} \implies \mathbf{25.65\text{ mol}\%}$$
Conclusion: The original peak metamorphic feldspar had a homogeneous ternary composition of $\mathbf{Ab_{58.8}An_{25.7}Or_{15.5}}$, confirming crystallization above the ternary solvus at $T > 850^\circ\text{C}$.
Problem 2: Size Scale Classification of Perthitic Intergrowths

Define the boundaries between: (a) Macroperthite; (b) Microperthite; and (c) Cryptoperthite, and state the analytical technique required to resolve each variety.

Dimensional Classification:
- (a) Macroperthite: Exsolution lamellae are thick ($> 0.1\text{ mm}$), coarse, and distinct enough to be seen with the naked eye or hand lens without optical magnification.
- (b) Microperthite: Exsolution lamellae range from $1\text{ to } 100\ \mu\text{m}$, invisible to the naked eye but beautifully resolved under the standard petrographic polarizing light microscope (PLM).
- (c) Cryptoperthite: Submicroscopic exsolution lamellae on the nanometer scale ($< 1\ \mu\text{m}$, typically $10\text{--}100\text{ nm}$). Completely invisible under optical microscopy; can only be detected via Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) or single-crystal X-ray diffraction (XRD). Cryptoperthite causes light diffraction producing the bluish iridescence known as moonstone (schiller effect).
Problem 3: Crystallographic Preferred Orientation of Exsolution Lamellae

Why do perthite and antiperthite exsolution lamellae align preferentially parallel to the $(6\bar{0}1)$ or $(\bar{6}01)$ crystallographic lattice planes rather than along random directions?

Lattice Strain Minimization (Willaime & Brown, 1974):
- During coherent subsolidus exsolution, the lattices of the two unmixing feldspars remain continuous across the phase interface.
- Because $K^+$ and $Na^+$ have different ionic radii, lattice dimensions differ ($a_{\text{Kfs}} = 8.6\text{ \AA}$ vs $a_{\text{Albite}} = 8.1\text{ \AA}$), introducing significant elastic strain energy ($\Delta G_{\text{elastic}}$).
- The crystallographic orientation that minimizes total coherent elastic strain energy between potassium feldspar and sodic plagioclase is the irrational plane oriented close to $(6\bar{0}1)$, inclined at $\sim 73^\circ$ to the $(001)$ cleavage. Aligning along this plane minimizes interfacial lattice mismatch, making phase separation thermodynamically spontaneous.
Problem 4: Hypersolvus vs. Subsolvus Granites and Perthite Formation

Compare the feldspar mineralogy of: (a) a Hypersolvus Granite crystallized at shallow crustal depth under low $P_{H_2O}$; and (b) a Subsolvus Granite crystallized at deep crustal levels under high $P_{H_2O}$.

Petrogenetic Phase Boundary Analysis:
- (a) Hypersolvus Granite (One-Feldspar Granite): Under dry, low $P_{H_2O}$ conditions, the granite solidus lies above the feldspar solvus ($T_{\text{solidus}} > T_{\text{solvus}}$). The magma crystallizes a single, homogeneous ternary alkali feldspar. Upon subsolidus cooling across the solvus, this single feldspar unmixes into spectacular perthite or mesoperthite.
- (b) Subsolvus Granite (Two-Feldspar Granite): Under high $P_{H_2O}$, dissolved water depresses the silicate solidus until it intersects the solvus ($T_{\text{solidus}} < T_{\text{solvus}}$). The magma crystallizes two separate primary magmatic feldspars directly from the melt: an independent potassium feldspar and an independent plagioclase feldspar.
Problem 5: Incoherent vs. Coherent Exsolution Microstructures

Distinguish between coherent and incoherent exsolution in feldspars. Which mechanism governs the formation of coarse patchy antiperthite?

Exsolution Physics:
- Coherent Exsolution: Crystal lattice planes are continuous across the boundary between the host and exsolved phase with zero bond breakage, accommodated entirely by elastic strain. Common in cryptoperthites formed by rapid cooling.
- Incoherent Exsolution: The lattice mismatch is relieved by edge dislocations, lattice severance, and recrystallization. The phase boundary is a true incoherent grain boundary with no continuous lattice planes.
- Patchy Antiperthite: Coarse patchy antiperthite in granulites and charnockites is formed by incoherent exsolution accompanied by fluid-enhanced recrystallization, allowing potassium feldspar to coalesce into large, irregular, isolated blebs within the plagioclase host.
Problem 6: Two-Feldspar Geothermometer Principles

Explain how the thermodynamic exchange of the albite ($Ab$) component between coexisting plagioclase and alkali feldspar is calibrated to calculate metamorphic equilibration temperatures.

Thermodynamic Exchange Derivation:
The exchange equilibrium of albite between coexisting plagioclase ($pl$) and alkali feldspar ($af$) is: $$\text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_{8\ (\text{in alkali feldspar})} \rightleftharpoons \text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_{8\ (\text{in plagioclase})}$$
At thermodynamic equilibrium: $$\mu_{Ab}^{af} = \mu_{Ab}^{pl}$$
Using Margules asymmetric solution parameters ($W_{AbOr}, W_{AbAn}, W_{OrAn}$) to account for non-ideal mixing, the distribution of albite is a sensitive, monotonic function of temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$). By measuring the chemical compositions of perthite host and antiperthite host via electron microprobe, one can solve for the exact equilibrium temperature ($T_{\text{equilibration}}$).
Problem 7: Massif-Type Anorthosite Plagioclase Mineralogy

In the Chilka Lake anorthosite massif of Odisha, plagioclase megacrysts exhibit antiperthitic texture. What does this reveal about the crystallization temperature and volatile state of the parent magma?

Petrogenetic Interpretation:
1. High Crystallization Temperature: The high solubility of potassium in plagioclase (requiring $> 10\text{--}15\text{ mol}\% Or$ component in the initial plagioclase lattice) proves crystallization occurred at very high temperatures ($T \approx 1050\text{--}1200^\circ\text{C}$).
2. Water-Undersaturated Parent Magma: Massif-type anorthosites crystallized from deep crustal/sub-crustal ponded basaltic melts under essentially anhydrous ($H_2O$-deficient) conditions, precluding early separation of two discrete feldspars and forcing extensive subsolidus antiperthitic exsolution upon prolonged cooling.
Problem 8: Optical Discrimination: Antiperthite vs. Plagioclase Alteration (Sericite)

Under the microscope, fine inclusions inside plagioclase can easily be mistaken for alteration products. How do you optically differentiate true exsolved K-feldspar inclusions (antiperthite) from secondary sericite or saussurite?

Microscopic Criteria:
- Antiperthite (Exsolution): Inclusions are K-feldspar. They have low refractive index ($n < n_{\text{plag}}$), low birefringence ($\delta = 0.006\text{--}0.008$, displaying first-order grey/white interference colors identical to quartz), form crisp, rectangular, optical-crystallographically oriented blebs, and exhibit uniform extinction.
- Sericite (Secondary Alteration): Consists of fine-grained white mica (muscovite/paragonite). Exhibits intense, brilliant high second-to-third order interference colors (pinks, blues, greens) and fibrous/flaky morphology.
- Saussurite: Microcrystalline aggregate of high-relief, high-birefringence epidote/zoisite, calcite, and albite, easily distinguished by its extreme relief and speckled appearance.
