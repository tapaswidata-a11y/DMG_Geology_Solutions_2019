1. The Principle of Linear Elasticity: Hooke's Law (1678)

In continuum mechanics, rock mechanics, and geodynamics, the mechanical response of solid Earth materials to applied tectonic stresses is governed by rheology. When a solid geomaterial is subjected to differential stress below its elastic yield limit ($\sigma < \sigma_y$), it deforms through recoverable elastic deformation:

  • Hooke's Law Formulation: First formulated anagrammatically in 1676 and published in 1678 by the English polymath Robert Hooke as Ut tensio, sic vis ("As the extension, so the force"), Hooke's Law states that strain ($\epsilon$) is directly proportional to applied stress ($\sigma$): $$\sigma = E \cdot \epsilon$$ Where $\sigma$ is normal stress ($\text{Pa}$ or $\text{MPa}$), $\epsilon = \Delta L / L_0$ is dimensionless axial strain, and $E$ is the material proportionality constant known as Young's Modulus (Modulus of Elasticity).
  • Instantaneous & Recoverable: Elastic strain occurs instantaneously upon stress application and is completely recovered (reverses to zero strain) when the stress is removed, storing energy as strain potential energy ($U = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \epsilon$).
The Four Fundamental Elastic Constants for Isotropic Rocks
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon} \quad \text{(Young's Modulus)} \qquad \nu = -\frac{\epsilon_{\text{lateral}}}{\epsilon_{\text{axial}}} \quad \text{(Poisson's Ratio)}$$ $$G = \frac{\tau}{\gamma} = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} \quad \text{(Shear Modulus)} \qquad K = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V / V} = \frac{E}{3(1 - 2\nu)} \quad \text{(Bulk Modulus)}$$

2. The Complete Rock Deformation Spectrum: Elastic, Plastic & Brittle

Under laboratory triaxial testing, crystalline rocks pass through three distinct deformation regimes as differential stress ($\sigma_1 - \sigma_3$) increases:

  • 1. Linear Elastic Region (Hookean): The initial straight-line portion where strain is proportional to stress. The slope of this line equals Young's Modulus ($E$). Rocks like quartzite and granite have high $E \approx 60 - 80\text{ GPa}$; soft shales have $E \approx 10 - 20\text{ GPa}$.
  • 2. Elastic Limit / Yield Strength ($\sigma_y$): The critical threshold beyond which deformation ceases to be linear and permanent irreversible strain begins.
  • 3. Ductile / Plastic Deformation: Permanent strain accumulated without loss of macroscopic cohesion (governed by power-law dislocation creep $\dot{\epsilon} = A \sigma^n \exp(-Q/RT)$). Strain is NOT proportional to stress.
  • 4. Brittle Rupture / Failure: Catastrophic loss of cohesion along shear fracture planes governed by Mohr-Coulomb and Griffith's failure criteria.

3. Refutation of Mohr, Griffith, and Plasticity Distractors

Candidates must clearly differentiate Hooke's Law from failure and non-elastic rheologies:

  • (A) Mohr’s Circle: A two-dimensional graphical representation of the coordinate transformation equations for normal ($\sigma$) and shear ($\tau$) stress acting on planes of varying orientation. It is a mathematical stress transformation tool, not a physical law of proportionality.
  • (B) Plastic Deformation: Irreversible permanent strain occurring beyond the yield strength without cohesion loss. In plasticity, strain continues to increase under constant yield stress (perfect plasticity), violating proportionality.
  • (D) Griffith’s Theory: A brittle fracture mechanics theory (A.A. Griffith, 1921) explaining rock failure via stress concentration at the tips of microscopic pre-existing elliptical micro-cracks ($K_{IC} = \sigma \sqrt{\pi a}$). It governs tensile and shear rupture, not linear elastic proportionality.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 23)
Q23. In elastic deformation, when strain is proportional to stress is known as :
(A) Mohr’s circle
(B) Plastic deformation
(C) Hooke’s law  ✔
(D) Griffith’s theory
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Hooke’s law
In solid mechanics and rock physics, the fundamental relationship stating that strain is directly proportional to applied stress ($\sigma = E\epsilon$) during recoverable elastic deformation is known universally as Hooke’s law.
(A) Mohr’s circle Incorrect (Stress Transformation Graphical Circle)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Mohr's circle is a geometric graphical construct (developed by Christian Otto Mohr in 1882) used to calculate normal and shear stresses acting on arbitrarily oriented planes from known principal stresses ($\sigma_1, \sigma_3$). It does not define stress-strain proportionality.
(B) Plastic deformation Incorrect (Non-Linear, Irreversible Permanent Flow)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Plastic deformation occurs only after the elastic limit has been exceeded. It represents permanent, non-recoverable change in shape where strain is highly non-linear and not proportional to stress.
(C) Hooke’s law Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Formulated by Robert Hooke in 1678, Hooke's Law represents the foundational constitutive equation of linear elasticity: $\sigma = E\epsilon$. It states that within the elastic limit, strain is directly proportional to the applied stress, and the material returns to its original dimensions when the stress is released.
(D) Griffith’s theory Incorrect (Microcrack Brittle Fracture Mechanics)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A.A. Griffith's (1921) theory is a brittle failure criterion based on the thermodynamic balance between surface energy and elastic strain energy around micro-cracks. It explains why rocks fracture at stresses far below their theoretical atomic bond strengths, but does not describe linear elasticity.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A core of Charnockite with an original length $L_0 = 100.0\text{ mm}$ and cross-sectional area $A = 2000\text{ mm}^2$ is compressed in a uniaxial press. The applied axial load is $F = 160\text{ kN}$. If the rock's Young's Modulus is $E = 80\text{ GPa}$, calculate the resulting axial stress ($\sigma$), axial strain ($\epsilon$), and total length reduction ($\Delta L$).
    ✔ Worked Answer: $\sigma = 80\text{ MPa}$; $\epsilon = 0.0010$ ($0.10\%$); $\Delta L = 0.10\text{ mm}$.
    Calculation:
    $$\sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{160,000\text{ N}}{2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2} = 80 \times 10^6\text{ Pa} = 80\text{ MPa}$$
    By Hooke's Law ($\epsilon = \sigma / E$): $$\epsilon = \frac{80 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}}{80 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} = 0.0010$$
    $$\Delta L = \epsilon \cdot L_0 = 0.0010 \times 100.0\text{ mm} = 0.10\text{ mm}$$
  • Practice Q2: In elasticity, what is Poisson’s Ratio ($\nu$), and what is its typical value for crystalline rocks?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The ratio of lateral transverse strain to axial longitudinal strain ($\nu = -\epsilon_{\text{lat}} / \epsilon_{\text{ax}}$); typically $\nu \approx 0.20 - 0.30$ (Poisson solid: $\nu = 0.25$).
    Explanation: Under uniaxial compression, rocks expand laterally as they shorten longitudinally. An incompressible material has $\nu = 0.50$.
  • Practice Q3: What geophysical theory, proposed by H.F. Reid following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, directly relies on Hooke's Law of elasticity?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Elastic Rebound Theory.
    Explanation: Tectonic plate motions slowly bend crustal rocks elastically (storing Hookean strain energy) until shear stress overcomes fault friction, causing rapid elastic rebound that releases seismic waves.
  • Practice Q4: What are the two Lamé parameters ($\lambda$ and $\mu$) used to describe linear isotropic elasticity in seismology?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $\mu$ is the Shear Modulus (modulus of rigidity $G$), and $\lambda$ is Lamé's first parameter ($\lambda = K - \frac{2}{3}\mu$).
    Explanation: In a Poisson solid ($\lambda = \mu$), the seismic compressional wave velocity relates to shear velocity as $V_p = \sqrt{3} V_s \approx 1.732 V_s$.
  • Practice Q5: How is seismic P-wave velocity ($V_p$) expressed in terms of Bulk Modulus ($K$), Shear Modulus ($G$), and rock density ($\rho$)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $V_p = \sqrt{\frac{K + \frac{4}{3}G}{\rho}}$.
    Explanation: P-wave speed is controlled by the elastic resistance of the rock lattice to volumetric compression ($K$) and shear deformation ($G$).
  • Practice Q6: What mechanical model represents a Maxwell viscoelastic body, and how does it respond to an instantaneous stress step?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A Hookean spring connected in series with a Newtonian viscous dashpot; responds with instantaneous elastic strain followed by steady viscous creep.
    Explanation: The Maxwell model explains post-glacial isostatic rebound and post-seismic relaxation in the upper mantle.
  • Practice Q7: What is elastic hysteresis in rock mechanics?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The loop-like divergence between the loading and unloading stress-strain curves caused by micro-scale frictional sliding along internal microcracks.
    Explanation: The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop represents energy permanently dissipated as heat (internal friction damping) during cyclic loading.
  • Practice Q8: Why does Bulk Modulus ($K$) describe the elastic behavior of fluids (water, magma), whereas Shear Modulus ($G$) is zero?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Fluids resist volumetric compression ($K > 0$) but possess zero resistance to shear deformation ($G = 0$).
    Explanation: Because $G = 0$ in liquids, shear waves cannot propagate ($V_s = \sqrt{G/\rho} = 0$), explaining why S-waves are blocked by Earth's liquid outer core.
