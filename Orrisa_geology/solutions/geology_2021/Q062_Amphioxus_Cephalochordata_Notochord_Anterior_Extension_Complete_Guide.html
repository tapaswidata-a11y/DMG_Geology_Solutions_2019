1. The Notochord of Amphioxus (Branchiostoma)

The lancelet, popularly known as Amphioxus (genus Branchiostoma, Subphylum Cephalochordata), represents the classical archetype of the primitive chordate body plan. While sharing all five fundamental diagnostic chordate characteristics (notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal gill slits, endostyle, and post-anal tail), its notochord exhibits a unique and profound anatomical distinction from all true vertebrates:

  • Etymology and Definition: The subphylum name Cephalochordata is derived directly from Greek kephale (meaning "head") + chorde (meaning "string" or "cord"), which literally translates as “head-chordates”.
  • Anterior Extension Past the Brain: In Amphioxus, the notochord does not terminate beneath the brain as in vertebrates. Instead, it extends along the entire length of the animal to the extreme anterior end (tip of the snout / rostrum), extending well beyond the anterior-most cerebral vesicle of the neural tube.
  • Hydrodynamic Burrowing Function: This extreme anterior extension serves a vital mechanical adaptation: when Amphioxus burrows forward into marine coarse sand, the stiff, hydrostatic notochord acts as a rigid pointed hydraulic battering ram, protecting the delicate front end of the nerve cord from compression injury.

2. Anatomical Comparison: Cephalochordates vs. Vertebrates (Craniates)

The anterior termination of the notochord marks the fundamental evolutionary divergence between invertebrates and craniate vertebrates:

The Rostral Notochord Boundary Condition
$$\text{Cephalochordates (Amphioxus): } \quad x_{\text{notochord}} \ge x_{\text{rostrum}} \quad (\text{Notochord extends past brain to extreme anterior})$$ $$\text{Vertebrates (Craniata): } \quad x_{\text{notochord}} \le x_{\text{midbrain}} \quad (\text{Notochord stops at pituitary infundibulum / basicranium})$$
  • In True Vertebrates (Craniata): The notochord terminates anteriorly beneath the posterior part of the diencephalon / midbrain, directly behind the hypophysis (pituitary gland). The anterior cranium (ethmoid and orbital cartilage) develops independently from neural crest cells.
  • Muscularized Notochord in Amphioxus: Unlike the gelatinous, fluid-vacuolated notochord of vertebrate embryos, the notochord of Amphioxus is unique in consisting of disk-shaped, transversely stacked paramyosin muscle plates that can contract to adjust the stiffness of the rod during swimming!

Official Examination Question
Question 62
The notochord of amphioxus differs from that of vertebrates by extending to the extreme _______ end of the body.
(A) posterior
(B) anterior
(C) open
(D) None of these
Verified Correct Answer: (B) anterior [In Amphioxus (Cephalochordata), the notochord extends to the extreme anterior end of the body (tip of the snout), whereas in vertebrates it terminates behind the brain.]
Correct Option: (B) anterior

Detailed Anatomical Forensic Analysis:

  • In comparative chordate anatomy (Benton 2014; Romer & Parsons 1986; Kardong 2015), the defining morphological characteristic of Amphioxus (Subphylum Cephalochordata) is that its notochord extends to the extreme anterior end of the body, protruding beyond the anterior tip of the nerve cord into the pointed rostrum.
  • In contrast, in all true vertebrates (Craniata), the notochord terminates anteriorly beneath the midbrain/hindbrain, never reaching the front of the head.
  • This anterior extension gives the cephalochordates their name (cephalo- = head) and provides structural rigidity for forward sand-burrowing.
  • Therefore, Option (B) anterior is the definitive correct answer.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) posterior

Detailed Refutation:

  • The notochord extends into the posterior tail in *both* vertebrates (embryos) and amphioxus.
  • Extending to the posterior tail does not differentiate amphioxus from vertebrates; it is the unique extension to the extreme anterior rostrum that distinguishes it.
Incorrect Distractor: (C) open

Detailed Refutation:

  • "Open" is a nonsensical anatomical descriptor for the terminus of a skeletal rod. The notochord is enclosed within a continuous collagenous sheath.
Incorrect Distractor: (D) None of these

Detailed Refutation:

  • Because Option (B) anterior is the precise textbook anatomical term, Option (D) is false.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Chordate Evolution & Comparative Anatomy
Problem 1: Cambrian Chordate Ancestry (*Pikaia*)

What Burgess Shale fossil from the Middle Cambrian of Canada is recognized as the earliest known stem-cephalochordate closely related to Amphioxus?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Pikaia gracilens. This ribbon-shaped, laterally compressed organism ($\sim 5\text{ cm}$ long) possesses a distinct dorsal notochord, a dorsal nerve cord, and over 100 zigzag chevron-shaped myomeres, demonstrating that cephalochordate-like ancestors had already radiated in the early Cambrian seas ($508\text{ Ma}$).

Problem 2: The Endostyle and Thyroid Homology

What homologous organ in adult vertebrates evolves from the ciliated mucus-secreting endostyle found in Amphioxus and larval lampreys?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The thyroid gland. In Amphioxus, the endostyle is a ventral pharyngeal groove that binds environmental iodine into iodinated tyrosine compounds to trap food. During vertebrate development, this iodine-binding epithelium invaginates to form the endocrine thyroid gland producing thyroxine ($\text{T}_4$) and triiodothyronine ($\text{T}_3$).

Problem 3: Hydrostatic Stiffness of the Notochord

Explain the physical mechanism that provides flexural stiffness to the chordate notochord without bone or cartilage.

Step-by-Step Solution:

The notochord consists of a core of tightly packed, fluid-engorged vacuolated cells surrounded by an outer acellular sheath of densely woven, helically wound collagen fibers. The osmotic absorption of water creates high internal hydrostatic turgor pressure. Because water is incompressible, the rod resists longitudinal shortening under muscle contraction while allowing lateral sinusoidal bending for undulatory swimming.

Problem 4: Neural Crest Cells and the "New Head" Hypothesis

What embryological tissue, completely absent in Amphioxus, is responsible for forming the vertebrate skull, sensory capsules, and jaws (Gans & Northcutt "New Head" hypothesis)?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Neural Crest Cells. In vertebrate embryos, neural crest cells migrate from the margins of the folding neural tube throughout the body to generate the cartilaginous cranium, jaw skeleton, sensory ganglia, melanocytes, and tooth dentine. The evolutionary emergence of neural crest cells transformed passive filter-feeders like amphioxus into active, predatory craniate vertebrates.

Problem 5: Nucleus Pulposus in Human Anatomy

What anatomical structure in the adult human body represents the direct embryonic remnant of the notochord?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The nucleus pulposus—the gelatinous, shock-absorbing central core of each intervertebral disc in the human spine. Herniation of an intervertebral disc ("slipped disc") involves the rupture of the outer annulus fibrosus and extrusion of this ancient notochordal remnant.

Problem 6: Solenocytes vs. Glomerular Kidneys

Why is the excretory system of Amphioxus considered non-vertebrate?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Amphioxus excretes waste via ectodermally derived protonephridia with solenocytes (flagellated cells identical to the flame cells of flatworms and polychaetes) that discharge directly into the atrium. Vertebrates possess mesodermally derived nephric tubules with blood-filtering glomeruli, a completely independent evolutionary innovation.

Problem 7: *Haikouichthys* and the Origin of Craniates

How does the Early Cambrian fossil Haikouichthys from the Chengjiang Biota of China advance beyond the Amphioxus grade?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Unlike Amphioxus, Haikouichthys ($\sim 518\text{ Ma}$) exhibits a distinct cartilaginous head skeleton (cranium), paired eyes, otic capsules, branchial arches supporting true gills, a dorsal fin with fin rays, and vertebral arcualia, establishing it as the world's oldest known craniate vertebrate.

Problem 8: Myomere Folding Geometry

Contrast the shape of the myomeres (muscle blocks) in Amphioxus with those in teleost fishes.

Step-by-Step Solution:

In Amphioxus, the myomeres are simple V-shaped chevrons pointing anteriorly. In jawed fishes (teleosts and sharks), myomeres evolve into complex W-shaped 3D folded cones intersected by a horizontal connective tissue septum (dividing epaxial and hypaxial muscles), enabling powerful three-dimensional undulatory thrust.
