Q41: Genetic Classification of Mountain Systems (Tectonic vs. Residual)

Exam: Geology Paper-II (2021)  |  Authoritative Reference: Arthur Holmes (Principles of Physical Geology); W.D. Thornbury (Principles of Geomorphology); Bangar (Ch. 2)

Part I: Theoretical Foundation & Concept Masterclass

41.1 Genetic Classification of Mountain Systems

In physical geology and geomorphology (Arthur Holmes, Principles of Physical Geology; Thornbury, Principles of Geomorphology; Bangar, Ch. 2), mountains are categorized based on their fundamental genetic origin into Tectonic (Endogenic) mountains and Residual (Exogenic) mountains:

Genetic Category Mountain Type Primary Formation Mechanism Representative Global & Indian Examples
TECTONIC MOUNTAINS
(Endogenic Internal Forces)		 Fold Mountains Crustal convergence, buckling, and orogenic shortening of thick geosynclinal/basin strata. Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies
Fault-Block Mountains (Horsts) Crustal extension or differential vertical displacement along steep fault systems. Satpura Range, Vindhyan Scarp, Vosges, Sierra Nevada
Volcanic Mountains (Accumulation) Extrusion and constructional accumulation of lava, tephra, and pyroclastic debris around a vent. Mt. Fuji, Kilimanjaro, Mauna Loa, Barren Island
NON-TECTONIC / EROSIONAL
(Exogenic External Forces)		 Residual Mountains (Relict / Circumdenudational) NOT formed by active tectonic deformation. Formed by prolonged differential weathering and stream denudation of ancient plateaus, leaving hard, resistant rocks standing as relief hills. Aravalli Range (relict hills), Nilgiris, Parasnath Hills, Scottish Highlands

41.2 The Concept of Residual (Relict) Relief

Residual mountains (monadnocks, bornhardts, mesas, buttes) are products of the degradation of old landmasses. They represent the unconsumed erosional remnants of ancient peneplains or plateaus that have resisted denudation due to superior lithological hardness or structural compactness (e.g. quartzite ridges standing above eroded schists). Therefore, they belong to the erosional/denudational category, not tectonic mountains.

Part II: Official Question & Critical Option Dissection

Q41. The mountain type which does not belong to the tectonic mountain categories are :
(A) Complex mountains
(B) Volcanic mountains
(C) Fault block mountains
(D) Residual mountains
VERIFIED ANSWER: OPTION (D)

Correct Answer: (D) Residual mountains — Residual mountains (relict hills) are formed by exogenic differential weathering and stream erosion, and do not belong to the tectonic mountain categories.

Option-by-Option Critical Dissection

  • (A) Complex mountains — INCORRECT: Complex mountains (or polyphase orogenic belts) are classic tectonic mountains formed by multi-stage folding, thrusting, and metamorphism.
  • (B) Volcanic mountains — INCORRECT: Volcanic mountains are endogenic/tectonic constructive edifices formed by magmatic activity associated with subduction or mantle plumes.
  • (C) Fault block mountains — INCORRECT: Fault block mountains (horsts) are direct tectonic mountains formed by crustal faulting and differential vertical displacement.
  • (D) Residual mountains — CORRECT: Residual mountains are exclusively exogenic/erosional in genesis, formed by the circumdenudation of ancient plateaus.

High-Yield Geomorphic Evolution Problem

Practice Problem 41: Morphoclimatic Inversion of Relief

The Aravalli Range is one of the oldest mountain belts in the world (Proterozoic Delhi-Aravalli Orogen). Explain why the present-day topography of the Aravallis is classified geomorphically as a "Residual Mountain" system rather than an active "Fold Mountain" chain.

Solution:

1. While the rocks of the Aravalli Supergroup were intensely folded, faulted, and metamorphosed during the Proterozoic orogeny ($~1.5\text{–}1.0\text{ Ga}$), active tectonic folding ceased over $500\text{ million years ago}$.

2. Over the subsequent hundreds of millions of years, prolonged subaerial weathering and denudation planed the ancient orogen down to a peneplain.

3. Differential erosion then carved away the soft mica schists and phyllites, leaving the highly resistant Alwar quartzites standing as narrow, elevated relict ridges (e.g. Guru Shikhar, $1,722\text{ m}$). Because their current elevation is maintained purely by differential erosional resistance rather than active tectonic uplift, they are quintessential Residual Mountains.
