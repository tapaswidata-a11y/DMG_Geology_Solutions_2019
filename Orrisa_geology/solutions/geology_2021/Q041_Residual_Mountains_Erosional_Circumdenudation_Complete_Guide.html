1. Genetic Taxonomy: Endogenic Tectonic vs. Exogenic Residual Mountains

In physical geology and structural geomorphology, all terrestrial mountain systems are classified genetically into two fundamental categories based on the primary geodynamic agency responsible for their relief:

  • Tectonic Mountains (Endogenic Constructional Relief): Mountain ranges formed directly by internal crustal forces (diastrophism and volcanism) that deform, uplift, or accumulate rock masses above the regional base level:
    1. Fold Mountains (Orogenic Belts): Formed by horizontal crustal shortening, buckling, thrusting, and nappe stacking along convergent plate boundaries (e.g., Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Appalachians).
    2. Fault-Block Mountains (Horsts): Formed by tensional crustal extension or differential vertical block displacement along high-angle normal faults (e.g., Sierra Nevada, Harz Mountains, Vosges and Black Forest, Satpura Range).
    3. Volcanic Mountains (Accumulation Relief): Built up by the surface eruption, effusion, and cooling of lava, pyroclastics, and cinders around central vents (e.g., Mt. Fuji, Mt. Kilimanjaro, Mauna Loa).
    4. Complex (Relief / Composite) Tectonic Mountains: Polycyclic mountain systems exhibiting intense interference of multiple deformation phases, deep metamorphism, and igneous plutonism.
  • Residual Mountains (Mountains of Circumdenudation / Relict Relief): Residual mountains are NOT tectonic mountains. They owe their elevated relief not to contemporary tectonic uplift, but to the protracted exogenic differential erosion and circumdenudation of ancient, eroded plateaus or peneplains. Resistant rock masses (monadnocks, inselbergs, quartzite ridges) withstand weathering while the softer surrounding strata are stripped away (e.g., the Aravalli Range, the Eastern Ghats, the Catskill Mountains of New York, and the Scottish Highlands).

2. The Circumdenudation Process: Evolution of Residual Highlands

The transition of an ancient orogenic belt into a residual mountain system involves long-term denudation cycles governed by Davisian or Penckian landscape evolution:

Differential Denudation Dynamic Equilibrium
$$\Delta h(t) = \int_0^t \left( U(t) - E_{\text{hard}}(t) \right) dt - \int_0^t \left( U(t) - E_{\text{soft}}(t) \right) dt = \int_0^t \left( E_{\text{soft}} - E_{\text{hard}} \right) dt$$

Where $U(t) \to 0$ in stable cratonic post-orogenic regimes, and residual topographic relief $\Delta h$ develops solely due to the differential erosion rate between resistant quartzites/charns ($E_{\text{hard}}$) and erodible phyllites/shales ($E_{\text{soft}}$).

  • The Aravalli Example: The Aravalli Range represents one of the oldest Proterozoic fold belts in the world ($\sim 1.8-1.0\text{ Ga}$). While originally formed as a high-altitude collision fold mountain belt, contemporary Aravalli topography is purely a residual mountain system: millions of years of subaerial weathering have planed down the softer surrounding Archean basement, leaving hard Alwar quartzites standing as isolated strike ridges.
  • Catskill Mountains: The Catskills in New York consist of undeformed, flat-lying Devonian clastic sedimentary strata. Deep fluvial canyon incision by the Hudson and Delaware drainage networks carved isolated plateau blocks that resemble mountains, yet possess zero internal folding or fault-block tectonism.

Official Examination Question
Question 41
The mountain type which does not belong to the tectonic mountain categories are :
(A) Complex mountains
(B) Volcanic mountains
(C) Fault block mountains
(D) Residual mountains
Verified Correct Answer: (D) Residual mountains [Residual mountains are exogenic relict landforms sculpted by long-term differential weathering and circumdenudation, not by endogenic tectonic uplift or deformation.]
Correct Option: (D) Residual mountains

Detailed Geomorphological Forensic Analysis:

  • In genetic geomorphology, mountains are broadly classified into Tectonic (or Constructional) Mountains and Residual (or Relict / Circumdenudation) Mountains.
  • Tectonic mountains owe their elevated topography to direct endogenic geodynamic activity—such as compressional crustal folding, extensional block faulting, or volcanic accumulation.
  • Residual mountains (e.g., the Aravalli Hills, Eastern Ghats, or Catskill Mountains) are not formed by tectonic forces. Instead, they represent the remnants of ancient elevated plateaus or eroded mountain roots where prolonged exogenic weathering and fluvial erosion have removed softer surrounding rock, leaving harder, resistant rock standing as residual highlands (monadnocks).
  • Because their formation is driven by exogenic denudation rather than endogenic tectonic processes, residual mountains do not belong to the tectonic mountain categories.
Incorrect Distractor: (A) Complex mountains

Detailed Refutation:

  • Complex mountains (composite tectonic mountains) are formed by polyphase tectonic orogeny involving deep crustal folding, nappe transport, high-grade regional metamorphism, and extensive granitic batholithic intrusions.
  • Because their internal architecture and topographic relief are entirely the consequence of intense endogenic plate tectonic processes, they are canonical tectonic mountains.
Incorrect Distractor: (B) Volcanic mountains

Detailed Refutation:

  • Volcanic mountains (such as stratovolcanoes and shield volcanoes) are formed by endogenic magmatic and volcanic eruptions at subduction zones, mid-ocean ridges, or mantle plumes.
  • Because volcanism is an endogenic tectonic/igneous process that directly constructs mountain relief, volcanic mountains represent a primary subcategory of tectonic mountains.
Incorrect Distractor: (C) Fault block mountains

Detailed Refutation:

  • Fault-block mountains (horsts and tilted fault blocks) are formed by crustal extensional tectonics, where displacement along normal faults lifts crustal blocks relative to adjacent grabens (e.g., the Sierra Nevada, Satpura Range).
  • Since their relief is directly generated by tectonic faulting, they are classic tectonic mountains.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Geomorphology & Orogenesis
Problem 1: Denudation Chronology and Relief Inversion

Explain the geological mechanism of relief inversion: how can an ancient synclinal fold become a mountain ridge while adjacent anticlines become valleys?

Step-by-Step Solution:

During folding, anticlinal crests experience tensional fracturing and joint opening, making them highly vulnerable to mechanical weathering and fluvial incision. Conversely, synclinal troughs experience compressional compaction, closing fractures and increasing rock density. Over tens of millions of years of exogenic circumdenudation, streams excavate the weakened anticlines into valleys, leaving the tightly packed synclinal hinges standing as high-relief ridges (synclinal mountains).

Problem 2: Fluvial Incision vs. Rock Mass Erodibility

In a residual mountain terrain, a river cuts through alternating beds of quartzite (erodibility $K_q = 1.2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^{1-2m}/\text{yr}$) and shale ($K_s = 9.6 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^{1-2m}/\text{yr}$). Calculate the ratio of incision rate between shale and quartzite under identical hydraulic shear stress.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Under the stream power incision law, incision rate is proportional to erodibility: $E = K \tau^a$.

$$\frac{E_s}{E_q} = \frac{K_s}{K_q} = \frac{9.6 \times 10^{-5}}{1.2 \times 10^{-6}} = 80$$

Final Answer: The shale erodes $80$ times faster than quartzite, creating dramatic residual quartzite ridges flanked by deeply excavated shale valleys.

Problem 3: Horst and Graben Block Throw Calculation

A fault-block mountain (horst) is bounded by two normal faults dipping at $60^\circ$ that have experienced a heave $H = 500\text{ m}$. Calculate the vertical tectonic uplift (throw $T$) of the mountain block.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$T = H \tan\theta = 500\text{ m} \times \tan 60^\circ = 500 \times 1.732 = 866\text{ m}$$

Final Answer: The horst block is tectonically uplifted by $866\text{ m}$ relative to the adjacent graben floors.

Problem 4: Monadnock Definition in Davisian Peneplanation

In W.M. Davis's geographical cycle of erosion, what is a monadnock and how does it relate to residual mountains?

Step-by-Step Solution:

A monadnock (named after Mount Monadnock in New Hampshire) is an isolated hill or ridge of resistant bedrock that rises conspicuously above the level of a surrounding nearly flat erosional plain (peneplain) during the old-age stage of the geomorphic cycle. Monadnocks are the ultimate micro-scale manifestation of residual mountains.

Problem 5: Appalachian vs. Himalayan Tectonic Maturity

Contrast the tectonic geomorphic state of the Himalayas with that of the Appalachian Mountains.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Himalayas: Active tectonic collision belt ($U \approx 5-10\text{ mm/yr}$), high seismicity, active thrust faults (MFT, MBT, MCT), youthful V-shaped gorges, immature drainage.
  • Appalachians: Paleozoic orogen (Alleghanian orogeny $\sim 300\text{ Ma}$), zero active plate convergence. Present topography is a rejuvenated residual mountain belt shaped by differential erosion of folded Paleozoic quartzites and shales (Ridge and Valley Province).
Problem 6: Volcanic Cone Geomorphic Degradation

What landform remains when a volcanic mountain is subjected to prolonged circumdenudation that strips away its pyroclastic cone?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The softer pyroclastics (ash, cinders, tuff) of the cone are rapidly eroded away, leaving the solidified, crystalline basaltic or phonolitic conduit plug standing as a vertical cylindrical column of rock known as a volcanic neck (or volcanic plug / diatreme), such as Shiprock in New Mexico.

Problem 7: Pediplanation vs. Peneplanation

Contrast L.C. King's concept of pediplanation with W.M. Davis's concept of peneplanation in creating residual highlands.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Davis's peneplanation involves down-wearing (slope decline) where slope angles progressively diminish over time. King's pediplanation involves scarp retreat (parallel slope retreat) driven by scarp-foot pedimentation, leaving steep-sided residual hills (inselbergs / bornhardts) that maintain steep slope angles throughout the denudation cycle.

Problem 8: Tectonic Geomorphic Indices for Distinguishing Mountains

Which quantitative morphotectonic index can unambiguously distinguish an active tectonic mountain front from a relict residual mountain front?

Step-by-Step Solution:

The Mountain Front Sinuosity ($S_{mf} = L_{mf}/L_s$): Active tectonic mountain fronts bounded by active faults are straight ($S_{mf} \approx 1.0-1.4$). In contrast, ancient residual mountain fronts shaped by long-term embayment and differential weathering are highly sinuous and irregular ($S_{mf} > 2.0-3.0$).
