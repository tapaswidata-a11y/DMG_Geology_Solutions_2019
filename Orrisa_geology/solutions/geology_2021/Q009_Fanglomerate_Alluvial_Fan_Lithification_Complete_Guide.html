1. Historical Petrogenesis: Lawson's (1913) Coining of "Fanglomerate"

In 1913, the renowned Scottish-American geologist Andrew Cowper Lawson published a landmark paper in the University of California Publications Bulletin of the Department of Geology entitled "The Petrographic Designation of Alluvial Fan Deposits". Lawson recognized that while unconsolidated alluvial fan gravels are common in arid and semi-arid intramontane basins (such as the Great Basin of Nevada and California), sedimentary petrology lacked a dedicated lithologic rock name for their indurated, lithified ancient equivalents:

"For the consolidated deposits of alluvial fans... the term 'fanglomerate' is here proposed. The rock is a distinct petrographic species, possessing characteristics which separate it from ordinary water-worn conglomerates on the one hand and from volcanic or fault breccias on the other." — Andrew C. Lawson, The Petrographic Designation of Alluvial Fan Deposits (Univ. Calif. Publ. Bull. Dept. Geol., 1913, Vol. 7, No. 15, p. 329)

Lawson defined Fanglomerate as the sedimentary rock formed by the lithification and induration of coarse, poorly sorted, angular to subrounded gravels, boulders, and sand-matrix debris flows deposited upon an alluvial fan.

2. Petrology: Paraconglomerates, Textural Immaturity & Diagenesis

Petrologically, fanglomerates are characterized by extreme textural and mineralogical immaturity reflecting rapid deposition adjacent to mountainous fault scarps:

  • Matrix-Supported Diamictites (Paraconglomerates): In proximal and medial fan facies, episodic cohesive debris flows deposit unstratified, chaotic beds where coarse boulders ($> 1\text{ meter}$) float in a muddy, sandy matrix ($> 15 - 50\%$ matrix), forming authentic paraconglomerates.
  • Clast-Supported Orthoconglomerates: Medial and distal braided channel and sheetflood facies deposit clast-supported gravel sheets ($< 15\%$ matrix) with distinct $a(\text{p})b(\text{i})$ pebble imbrication dipping up-fan toward the mountain source.
  • Polymictic / Petromict Lithologies: Fanglomerates contain heterogeneous assortments of unstable sedimentary, metamorphic, and igneous rock fragments reflecting the unroofing history of the adjacent mountain block.
  • Diagenetic Cementation: In arid and semi-arid basins, early diagenesis is driven by evaporative pore fluids depositing calcium carbonate (caliche / calcrete) or amorphous silica (silcrete) cements, rapidly converting loose gravels into indurated rock.

3. Forensic Dissection: Why Bajada, Agglomerate & Orthoconglomerate Fail

To master this question, candidates must clearly separate geomorphic landform terms from igneous and textural classifications:

  • Bajada (Geomorphic Landform): A continuous, undulating piedmont alluvial plain produced by the lateral coalescence and overlapping of multiple adjacent alluvial fans along a mountain front. A bajada is a topographic landform, not a lithified petrographic rock name.
  • Agglomerate (Pyroclastic Igneous Rock): A coarse-grained, chaotic volcanic rock composed predominantly of consolidated, rounded to subangular volcanic bombs ($> 64\text{ mm}$) ejected during explosive volcanic eruptions. It has zero relation to alluvial fans.
  • Orthoconglomerate (Broad Textural Class): A purely textural classification term for any conglomerate containing less than $15\%$ matrix (grain-supported). While some fluvial stream gravels are orthoconglomerates, many alluvial fan beds are matrix-rich paraconglomerates; hence, orthoconglomerate is not specific to alluvial fans.

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 9)
Q9. The lithified portions of alluvial fans are termed as :
(A) Bajada
(B) Agglomerate
(C) Fanglomerate  ✔
(D) Orthoconglomerate
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Fanglomerate
The consolidated, indurated petrographic equivalent of an alluvial fan deposit is specifically termed a Fanglomerate, coined by Andrew C. Lawson in 1913. A bajada is an unconsolidated geomorphic landform; an agglomerate is a pyroclastic volcanic rock; and an orthoconglomerate is a generic textural category.
(A) Bajada Incorrect (Geomorphological Landform, Not a Lithified Rock)
Why this distractor is incorrect: A bajada (or piedmont apron) is a geomorphological landscape feature formed when neighboring alluvial fans coalesce along a mountain piedmont. It describes modern unconsolidated topography, not a lithified rock unit.
(B) Agglomerate Incorrect (Coarse Pyroclastic Volcanic Rock)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Agglomerate is an igneous pyroclastic rock consisting of consolidated volcanic bombs and coarse ejected pyroclasts ($> 64\text{ mm}$) shaped in the air during volcanic eruptions. It is entirely unrelated to sedimentary alluvial fans.
(C) Fanglomerate Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Coined specifically by A.C. Lawson (1913) for the consolidated, lithified rock equivalents of alluvial fan deposits, fanglomerate is the exact, standard petrological term recognized in all international dictionaries of geology and standard sedimentology treatises (Boggs, Pettijohn, Bangar).
(D) Orthoconglomerate Incorrect (Generic Textural Clast-Supported Category)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Orthoconglomerate refers strictly to any conglomerate with a clast-supported framework ($< 15\%$ matrix), such as beach gravels or braided river channel gravels. Alluvial fans contain substantial matrix-supported debris-flow deposits (paraconglomerates); hence, orthoconglomerate is neither diagnostic of nor synonymous with alluvial fan rocks.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: In which classic American geological province did Andrew C. Lawson first define and name fanglomerates in 1913?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The Basin and Range Province (specifically the arid intermontane basins of California and Nevada).
    Explanation: Lawson investigated fault-bounded grabens bordered by steep fault-line scarps where alluvial fans aggraded rapidly under arid conditions, preserving thick fanglomerate sequences.
  • Practice Q2: What is a sieve deposit on an alluvial fan, and what rock type does it form upon lithification?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Highly permeable gravel sheets devoid of mud formed when water infiltrates instantly through coarse debris, forming clean orthoconglomerates.
    Explanation: Where source rocks produce coarse, mud-free rock debris (e.g., quartzites), flood waters sink rapidly through the permeable gravels, stranding clean, open-framework gravel sheets known as sieve lobes.
  • Practice Q3: What clast fabric (imbrication) is characteristic of water-laid sheetflood conglomerates in contrast to debris-flow deposits?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $a(\text{p})b(\text{i})$ imbrication, where flatter $ab$ planes dip upstream (up-fan) against the flow.
    Explanation: Water currents orient discoid pebbles with their longest axes transverse to flow ($b$-axis) and flat planes dipping into the current ($a$-plane). Cohesive debris flows lack organized imbrication, exhibiting random or vertical clast orientations.
  • Practice Q4: In Indian stratigraphy, the famous Neogene-Quaternary Upper Siwalik Boulder Conglomerate Formation is a lithified archetype of which depositional system?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Giant coalescing alluvial fan and fanglomerate aprons shed from the rising Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) of the Himalayas.
    Explanation: The Upper Siwalik Boulder Conglomerates represent coarse orogenic fanglomerates prograding southwards into the foreland basin during Pleistocene Himalayan orogenic pulses.
  • Practice Q5: What is the diagnostic petrographic difference between an oligomictic conglomerate and a petromict (polymictic) fanglomerate?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Oligomictic conglomerates contain clasts of a single resistant rock type (e.g., $> 90\%$ vein quartz or chert); petromict fanglomerates contain unstable, multi-lithology rock fragments (granites, basalts, schists, shales).
    Explanation: Because alluvial fans form adjacent to unroofed mountain scarps without long-distance transport, unstable rock clasts are preserved without chemical weathering.
  • Practice Q6: What pedogenic cement is most commonly responsible for the early induration and lithification of Quaternary arid alluvial fans into fanglomerates?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Calcrete (caliche / pedogenic calcium carbonate $\text{CaCO}_3$).
    Explanation: In arid and semi-arid climates, intense seasonal evaporation of groundwater draws bicarbonate-rich water toward the surface, precipitating micritic and sparry calcite that welds loose gravels into hard rock.
  • Practice Q7: How do tectonic normal faults along a rift basin margin control the vertical stratigraphic stacking of fanglomerates?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Repeated fault slip creates recurring accommodation space, generating stacked, multistory coarsening-upward fanglomerate wedges.
    Explanation: Each episodic faulting event rejuvenates relief, dumping coarse proximal debris directly over finer distal fan or lacustrine facies.
  • Practice Q8: Why is an intraformational conglomerate distinct from a fanglomerate?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Intraformational conglomerates contain clasts derived from within the same depositional basin (e.g., ripped-up shale rip-up clasts); fanglomerates contain extraformational clasts eroded from an external mountainous source.
    Explanation: Intraformational clasts are penecontemporaneous mud-flakes eroded by energetic currents within the basin, whereas fanglomerates are classic extraformational rudites.
