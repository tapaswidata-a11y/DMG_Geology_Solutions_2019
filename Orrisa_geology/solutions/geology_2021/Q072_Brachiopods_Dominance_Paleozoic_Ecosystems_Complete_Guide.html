Q72: Evolutionary Paleoecology - Brachiopods vs. Bivalves in Paleozoic Ecosystems

Exam: Geology Paper-II (2021)  |  Authoritative Reference: G.B. Mahapatra (A Text-Book of Geology, p. 348); Sepkoski (1981, Evolutionary Faunas)

Part I: Theoretical Foundation & Concept Masterclass

1. Jack Sepkoski's Three Great Evolutionary Faunas

In macroevolutionary paleontology, the quantitative analysis of Phanerozoic marine biodiversity conducted by J. John Sepkoski Jr. (1981, 1984) demonstrated that global marine life evolved through three successive, overlapping assemblages termed the Three Evolutionary Faunas:

  1. The Cambrian Fauna: Dominated by trilobites, inarticulate brachiopods, hyoliths, and monoplacophorans.
  2. The Paleozoic Fauna: Dominated by articulate brachiopods, crinoids, rugose and tabulate corals, bryozoans, and cephalopods.
  3. The Modern Fauna: Dominated by bivalve molluscs, gastropods, echinoids, crustaceans, and teleost fishes.

2. Ecological Dominance: Brachiopoda in the Paleozoic

Throughout the Paleozoic Era (Ordovician through Permian, ~485 to 252 Ma), brachiopods were vastly more diverse, abundant, and ecologically dominant than bivalve molluscs (G.B. Mahapatra, A Text-Book of Geology, Chapter 91, p. 348 / PDF p. 182; Gould & Calloway, 1980, Paleobiology). On Paleozoic level-bottom shallow marine carbonate platforms and clastic shelves, brachiopod shells formed dense pavements, shell beds, and biostromes, occupying nearly all epifaunal suspension-feeding niches.

During the Paleozoic, articulate brachiopods (strophomenids, orthids, spiriferids, productids, pentamerids) achieved staggering global taxonomic diversity exceeding 3,000 genera. In contrast, bivalves were ecologically marginalized to shallow infaunal burrowing and cryptic habitats with comparatively low diversity.

3. The Great Paleozoic Turnover & Extinction

The turning point occurred during the catastrophic End-Permian Mass Extinction (~252 Ma), which annihilated over 95% of marine species. Brachiopods suffered a devastating, irreversible collapse from which they never recovered. Following the extinction, bivalves diversified explosively in the Mesozoic and Cenozoic oceans, outcompeting brachiopods due to their active muscular foot for burrowing, mobile siphon systems for infaunal feeding, and higher metabolic rates, relegating modern brachiopods to marginal, deep-water, or cryptic cold-water marine refugia.

Part II: Official Question & Critical Option Dissection

Q72. Brachiopods are considered more important than bivalves in , —________. due to their greater global diversity and greater abundance in many fossil assemblages.
(A) Mesozoic ecosystems
(B) Cenozoic ecosystems
(C) Paleozoic ecosystems
(D) Paleocene ecosystems

Exam Question Analysis

Prompt: "Brachiopods are considered more important than bivalves in , —________. due to their greater global diversity and greater abundance in many fossil assemblages."

Option-by-Option Scientific Dissection

  • (A) Mesozoic ecosystems (INCORRECT): In the Mesozoic Era (Triassic to Cretaceous), bivalves (such as rudists, oysters, inoceramids, and pectinids) diversified exponentially and displaced brachiopods as the dominant benthic suspension feeders.
  • (B) Cenozoic ecosystems (INCORRECT): In Cenozoic and modern oceans, bivalves completely dominate coastal, shelf, and deep-sea benthic niches. Brachiopods are rare, cryptic relict organisms.
  • (C) Paleozoic ecosystems (CORRECT): In Paleozoic ecosystems (Ordovician to Permian), brachiopods reached the absolute pinnacle of their evolutionary diversity, abundance, and ecological dominance, completely outclassing bivalve molluscs in fossil assemblages worldwide. Verbatim text in G.B. Mahapatra, p. 348 confirms: "The brachiopods reached the acme of their development during Paleozoic times... in tertiary age, brachiopods occur only occasionally."
  • (D) Paleocene ecosystems (INCORRECT): The Paleocene is the basal epoch of the Cenozoic Era (~66–56 Ma), long after brachiopod dominance had ended.
