PART I
Theoretical Foundation & Scientific Principles
Authoritative textbook grounding, analytical derivations, and geodynamic mechanics
1. The Himalayan Collisional Orogenic System

The Himalayan Mountain Belt is Earth's premier active archetype of an in-sequence continent-continent collisional orogen. Initiated approximately $50 - 55\text{ million years ago}$ (Early Eocene) following the closure of the Neo-Tethys Ocean and the collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate, the orogen accommodates roughly $15 - 20\text{ mm/yr}$ of ongoing horizontal tectonic convergence:

  • Contractional Tectonic Regime: Because continental lithosphere is buoyant and cannot be subducted deep into the mantle, convergence is accommodated by colossal crustal shortening and vertical thickening (doubling crustal thickness up to $70 - 75\text{ km}$ beneath the Tibetan Plateau).
  • Primary Deformational Structures: By fundamental structural mechanics, horizontal compression ($\sigma_1$ horizontal and oriented NNE-SSW) produces two dominant, ubiquitous structural styles throughout the Himalayas: Folds (anticlinoria, synclinoria, recumbent folds, nappes) and Reverse / Thrust Faults (south-vergent imbricate thrust sheets).
Figure 32.1 (van der Pluijm & Marshak, Fig. 21.21): Geodynamic cross-section of the India-Asia continental collision. Shows horizontal contraction generating south-vergent crustal thrust wedges and colossal folds.
2. The Foreland-Propagating Thrust Belt: MFT, MBT, and MCT

The entire $2400\text{ km}$ arc of the Himalayas is partitioned into distinct lithotectonic zones bounded by four master south-verging thrust faults (reverse faults) and folded nappes:

  • 1. Main Frontal Thrust (MFT / HFT): The southernmost active thrust fault, placing Neogene-Quaternary folded Siwalik molasses over the Indo-Gangetic Alluvial Plain.
  • 2. Main Boundary Thrust (MBT): Separates the folded Siwalik group from the highly folded and faulted Proterozoic Lesser Himalayan sedimentary sequence.
  • 3. Main Central Thrust (MCT): A colossal ductile shear zone and low-angle thrust dipping north, placing the high-grade metamorphic Higher Himalayan Crystalline Sequence (HHC) over the Lesser Himalayas.
  • 4. Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT): The basal mega-decollement detachment underlying the entire orogen at $15 - 30\text{ km}$ depth, upon which all major Himalayan earthquakes nucleate.

3. Refutation of Normal Faulting Distractors

Candidates must clearly refute options containing normal faults:

  • Normal Faults (Options A & C): Normal faults require horizontal crustal extension ($\sigma_1$ vertical, $\sigma_3$ horizontal). While minor localized normal faulting occurs high in the orogenic superstructure due to gravitational collapse (e.g., South Tibetan Detachment System, STDS), the regional tectonic deformation of the Himalayan mountain belt is overwhelmingly contractional. Claiming normal faults are the common deformational structure is geodynamically false.
"The Himalayan orogen is characterized by intense crustal shortening accommodated by south-verging thrust faults and associated asymmetric to recumbent folds... The major tectonic boundaries (MCT, MBT, MFT) are all contractional reverse/thrust fault systems." — K.S. Valdiya, The Making of India: Geodynamic Evolution (2016, pp. 385–395)

Official Question Statement — OPSC 2021 Geology Paper-II (Question 32)
Q32. Rocks are deformed in the Himalayan mountain belt commonly forms which deformational structures ?
(A) Folds and normal faults
(B) Folds and reverse faults  ✔
(C) Normal faults and reverse faults
(D) None of these
✔
Correct Answer: (B) Folds and reverse faults
As a direct result of ongoing NNE-directed continental collision and crustal shortening between the Indian and Eurasian plates, rocks in the Himalayan mountain belt are overwhelmingly deformed into Folds and reverse faults (including major thrust nappes like the MCT, MBT, and MFT).
(A) Folds and normal faults Incorrect (Normal Faults Indicate Extensional Regimes)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Normal faults are characteristic of extensional rift settings (such as the East African Rift or Basin and Range). The Himalayas represent an active convergent orogen dominated by compressive stress, not regional extension.
(B) Folds and reverse faults Correct Verified Answer
Why this option is correct: Under horizontal compressive stress ($\sigma_1$ horizontal), rocks deform via horizontal shortening and vertical thickening, generating extensive fold systems (anticlinoria, synclinoria, recumbent folds) and south-vergent reverse/thrust fault systems (MCT, MBT, MFT) that define the Himalayan architecture.
(C) Normal faults and reverse faults Incorrect (Omits Fundamental Folding & Includes Extensional Faults)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Folding is the most prominent structural feature visible in Himalayan outcrops (e.g., Krol and Garhwal nappes). Omitting folds while including extensional normal faults misrepresents orogenic geodynamics.
(D) None of these Incorrect (Valid Answer Exists in Option B)
Why this distractor is incorrect: Option (B) is the universally established, textbook consensus across all Indian and international geology literature.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Questions)
  • Practice Q1: What is a Dun valley (e.g., Dehradun) in Himalayan structural geomorphology?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A longitudinal synclinal piggyback basin (intermontane valley) enclosed between the Siwalik hills and the Lesser Himalaya.
    Explanation: Duns develop behind the active Main Frontal Thrust (MFT) as fault-bend fold gravels aggrade in piggyback synclines.
  • Practice Q2: What singular structural exception in the Himalayas exhibits normal faulting, and what causes it?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The South Tibetan Detachment System (STDS); caused by gravitational orogenic collapse and ductile extrusion (channel flow) of the Higher Himalayan Crystallines.
    Explanation: While the base of the HHC was thrust south along the MCT, its top collapsed north along the low-angle normal STDS during the Miocene.
  • Practice Q3: What is the direction of tectonic transport (vergence) of the major folds and thrusts throughout the Himalayas?
    ✔ Correct Answer: South to South-Southwest vergence (toward the Indian foreland).
    Explanation: Underthrusting of the Indian craton beneath Asia drives top-to-the-south reverse displacement across all major thrusts.
  • Practice Q4: What is the Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The master basal decollement dipping gently ($\sim 5^\circ - 10^\circ$) northward, which roots all frontal thrusts (MFT, MBT, MCT).
    Explanation: The MHT accommodates the entire convergence budget and is locked over a $100\text{ km}$ width, rupturing periodically in giant earthquakes (e.g., 2015 Gorkha earthquake).
  • Practice Q5: In Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, the Almora and Shimla crystalline masses are preserved in the core of what structural feature?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Synformal klippen (klippes).
    Explanation: Erosion removed the Higher Himalayan thrust sheet over anticlinal arches, leaving crystalline rocks preserved only in down-warped synformal depressions (klippen).
  • Practice Q6: What is the average rate of active horizontal convergence between the Indian Plate and Eurasian Plate across the Himalayas?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Approximately $35 - 40\text{ mm/yr}$ total convergence, with $15 - 20\text{ mm/yr}$ absorbed across the Himalayan thrust belt.
    Explanation: About half the convergence is accommodated by Himalayan thrust shortening, and the remainder by internal deformation and eastward extrusion of Tibet.
  • Practice Q7: The Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) places which older rock unit over which younger rock unit?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Proterozoic Lesser Himalayan metasediments over Neogene Siwalik molasse sediments.
    Explanation: The MBT represents a major in-sequence contractional boundary active during the Pliocene-Pleistocene.
  • Practice Q8: Why do transverse river gorges (such as the Kali Gandaki and Indus) cut across the highest Himalayan peaks?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Antecedent drainage; rivers established their flow courses prior to the tectonic uplift of the Higher Himalayan peaks and cut down as the mountains rose.
    Explanation: Downcutting rates equaled or exceeded orogenic uplift rates, creating the deepest river gorges on Earth.
