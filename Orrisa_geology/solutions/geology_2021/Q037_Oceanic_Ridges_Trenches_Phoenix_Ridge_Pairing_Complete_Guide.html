1. Architecture of Global Mid-Ocean Ridges and Oceanic Trenches

The global system of lithospheric plate boundaries is defined by two complementary bathymetric first-order features covering over 70% of Earth's surface:

  • Mid-Ocean Ridges (Divergent Boundaries): An interconnected global submarine mountain chain exceeding $65,000\text{ km}$ in total length. Ridges are locus of seafloor spreading where upwelling asthenospheric mantle undergoes adiabatic decompression melting, generating new oceanic basaltic crust ($6-7\text{ km}$ thick).
    • Atlantic Ocean: Bisected axially by the slow-spreading ($2-4\text{ cm/yr}$) Mid-Atlantic Ridge (MAR). Its northern continuation south of Iceland is termed the Reykjanes Ridge.
    • Indian Ocean: Dominated by an inverted 'Y' shaped junction meeting at the Rodriguez Triple Junction: the Carlsberg Ridge (northwest branch traversing the Arabian Sea), the Central Indian Ridge, and the Southeast/Southwest Indian Ridges.
    • Arctic Ocean: Traversed by the ultra-slow spreading ($<1.5\text{ cm/yr}$) Gakkel Ridge (Nansen-Gakkel Ridge), which extends from the northern tip of Greenland across the polar basin toward the Laptev Sea.
  • Deep-Sea Trenches (Convergent Subduction Zones): Narrow, elongate, arcuate topographic depressions where cold, dense oceanic lithosphere descends back into the asthenospheric mantle.
    • Pacific Ocean: Bordered by the "Ring of Fire", including the Mariana Trench ($10,994\text{ m}$), Kermadec-Tonga Trench ($10,882\text{ m}$), Kuril-Kamchatka Trench, and Peru-Chile Trench.

2. Forensic Tectonic Analysis: Phoenix Ridge vs. Arctic Ocean

Question 37 tests precise knowledge of global plate geography by pairing oceanic ridges and trenches with their respective ocean basins:

The Geodynamic Identity of the Phoenix Ridge

The Phoenix Ridge (also historically termed the Drake Passage Ridge or Antarctic-Phoenix Ridge) is an extinct mid-ocean spreading ridge situated in the Southern Ocean / Southeastern Pacific Ocean between the Antarctic Peninsula and the South American plate (Drake Passage, coordinates approximately $60^\circ\text{S}, 60^\circ\text{W}$).

  • Subduction of the Phoenix Plate: During the Mesozoic and Cenozoic, the ancient oceanic Phoenix Plate (Aluk Plate) subducted southeastward beneath the Antarctic Peninsula magmatic arc. Subduction ceased progressively from southwest to northeast as segments of the active Phoenix spreading ridge collided with the Antarctic continental trench.
  • Extinction of Spreading: The final segment of the Phoenix Ridge ceased seafloor spreading approximately $3.3\text{ Ma}$ (Pliocene), leaving an extinct, fossil spreading center in the Drake Passage region of the Southern / South Pacific Ocean.
  • The Arctic Ridge is Gakkel: The ocean spreading ridge traversing the Arctic Ocean is unequivocally the Gakkel Ridge, NOT the Phoenix Ridge. Therefore, pairing Phoenix Ridge — Arctic Ocean is an erroneous geographical and tectonic mismatch.

Official Examination Question
Question 37
Find the incorrect pair :
(A) Kermadec Trench — Pacific Ocean
(B) Reykjanes Ridge — Atlantic Ocean
(C) Phoenix Ridge — Arctic Ocean
(D) Carlsberg Ridge — Indian Ocean
Verified Correct Answer: (C) Phoenix Ridge — Arctic Ocean [This is the incorrect pair; Phoenix Ridge is located in the Antarctic/Southern Pacific, whereas the Arctic Ocean spreading centre is the Gakkel Ridge.]
Correct Option (Target Incorrect Pair): (C) Phoenix Ridge — Arctic Ocean

Detailed Tectonic Forensic Analysis:

  • The Phoenix Ridge is an extinct spreading center located in the Drake Passage and Southeast Pacific Ocean bordering the northern Antarctic Peninsula (coordinates $\approx 60^\circ\text{S}, 60^\circ\text{W}$). It marks the boundary where the ancient oceanic Phoenix Plate ceased spreading upon collision with the Antarctic trench at $\sim 3.3\text{ Ma}$.
  • The mid-ocean ridge system located within the Arctic Ocean is the Gakkel Ridge (also known as the Nansen Cordillera or Arctic Mid-Ocean Ridge), which represents the northernmost continuation of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge between $80^\circ\text{N}$ and $90^\circ\text{N}$.
  • Because the Phoenix Ridge is physically located in the Southern/Pacific polar waters and completely absent from the Arctic Ocean, this pair is false and constitutes the correct answer to the question.
Incorrect Option (Valid Pair): (A) Kermadec Trench — Pacific Ocean

Detailed Refutation:

  • The Kermadec Trench is an authentic deep-sea trench located in the Southwest Pacific Ocean, extending over $1,000\text{ km}$ from the Louisville Seamount Chain to the North Island of New Zealand.
  • It reaches maximum depths exceeding $10,047\text{ m}$ and marks the active convergent subduction zone where the Pacific Plate subducts westward beneath the Kermadec microplate of the Indo-Australian Plate.
  • Since this pairing is completely accurate, it cannot be the incorrect pair.
Incorrect Option (Valid Pair): (B) Reykjanes Ridge — Atlantic Ocean

Detailed Refutation:

  • The Reykjanes Ridge is a well-known, prominent segment of the Northern Mid-Atlantic Ridge extending for $\sim 900\text{ km}$ southwest from the Reykjanes Peninsula of Iceland to the Bight Fracture Zone ($56.5^\circ\text{N}$).
  • It is characterized by slow, oblique seafloor spreading between the North American and Eurasian plates, devoid of a classical central axial rift valley due to hot-spot crustal thickening.
  • Because the Reykjanes Ridge is undeniably located in the North Atlantic Ocean, this is a true pair and therefore not the target option.
Incorrect Option (Valid Pair): (D) Carlsberg Ridge — Indian Ocean

Detailed Refutation:

  • The Carlsberg Ridge is the northern divergent branch of the Central Indian Ridge system, located entirely in the Northwest Indian Ocean (Arabian Sea).
  • It separates the African (Somali) Plate from the Indo-Australian Plate, trending northwest from the Rodriguez Triple Junction toward the Gulf of Aden and Owen Fracture Zone.
  • Because this pairing is entirely factually and tectonically correct, it is an incorrect distractor.

High-Yield Practice Problems: Marine Geodynamics & Plate Boundaries
Problem 1: Half-Spreading Rate and Crustal Age Calculation

Magnetic anomaly $5$ (age $t = 10\text{ Ma}$) is identified at a distance $x = 120\text{ km}$ from the axis of the Reykjanes Ridge. Calculate the half-spreading rate ($u$) and the full-spreading rate ($2u$) in $\text{cm/year}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Half-spreading rate is the velocity of one lithospheric plate away from the ridge axis:

$$u = \frac{x}{t} = \frac{120\text{ km}}{10\text{ Ma}} = 12\text{ km/Ma}$$

Converting $\text{km/Ma}$ to $\text{cm/year}$:

$$1\text{ km/Ma} = \frac{10^5\text{ cm}}{10^6\text{ yr}} = 0.1\text{ cm/yr}$$ $$u = 12 \times 0.1 = 1.2\text{ cm/year}$$

The full spreading rate is:

$$2u = 2 \times 1.2\text{ cm/year} = 2.4\text{ cm/year}$$

Final Answer: The half-spreading rate is $1.2\text{ cm/year}$ and the full spreading rate is $2.4\text{ cm/year}$ (typical slow-spreading Mid-Atlantic Ridge rate).

Problem 2: Ocean Bathymetry vs. Age (Thermal Subsidence)

The depth $d$ of ocean crust as a function of age $t$ (in $\text{Ma}$) follows Parsons-Sclater thermal cooling: $d(t) = d_0 + C\sqrt{t}$, where $d_0 = 2500\text{ m}$ is the ridge crest depth and $C = 350\text{ m/Ma}^{1/2}$. Calculate the expected ocean depth for $36\text{ Ma}$ old crust in the Indian Ocean.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$d(36) = 2500 + 350 \times \sqrt{36}$$ $$d(36) = 2500 + 350 \times 6 = 2500 + 2100 = 4600\text{ m}$$

Final Answer: The expected seafloor depth is $4600\text{ m}$ ($4.6\text{ km}$).

Problem 3: Hydrothermal Heat Flow at Ridge Crests

Why do observed heat flow measurements directly at mid-ocean ridge axes systematically fall below theoretical conductive cooling predictions?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Near the ridge axis, oceanic crust is extensively fractured, allowing cold seawater to penetrate deep into the hot basaltic crust. This drives vigorous hydrothermal circulation (black smokers). Up to $30-40\%$ of the total axial lithospheric heat loss is carried away by advective hydrothermal venting rather than conductive heat flow measured by heat flow probes.

Problem 4: Wadati-Benioff Zone Dip Angle

Compare the dip angle of the subducting slab in the Kermadec-Tonga Trench with that in the Peru-Chile Trench.

Step-by-Step Solution:

The Kermadec-Tonga slab consists of ancient, cold, dense Cretaceous oceanic lithosphere ($>100\text{ Ma}$) with low convergence velocity; hence, negative buoyancy causes it to sink steeply into the mantle ($\text{dip} \approx 60^\circ-70^\circ$, reaching depths of $680\text{ km}$). In contrast, the Peru-Chile trench subducts young, buoyant Nazca plate ($<30-50\text{ Ma}$) at rapid speed, producing shallow or flat-slab subduction ($\text{dip} \approx 10^\circ-30^\circ$).

Problem 5: Identification of the Gakkel Ridge Ultra-Slow Regime

What unique petrological characteristic distinguishes ultra-slow spreading ridges like the Arctic Gakkel Ridge from fast-spreading ridges like the East Pacific Rise?

Step-by-Step Solution:

At ultra-slow spreading rates ($<20\text{ mm/yr}$), conductive cooling from above suppresses mantle decompression melting, producing an extremely thin or absent basaltic Layer 2/3 crust ($<2-3\text{ km}$). Instead of continuous sheeted dikes and pillow basalts, extensive oceanic detachment faulting exhumes mantle serpentinized peridotites directly onto the seafloor over entire ridge segments.

Problem 6: Rodriguez Triple Junction Kinematics

Name the three tectonic plates that meet at the Rodriguez Triple Junction in the Indian Ocean, and state its junction stability type.

Step-by-Step Solution:

The Rodriguez Triple Junction (RTJ) is an authentic Ridge-Ridge-Ridge (RRR) triple junction located at $25^\circ\text{S}, 70^\circ\text{E}$. It represents the meeting point of the African (Somali) Plate, the Indo-Australian Plate, and the Antarctic Plate. In McKenzie-Morgan plate kinematic theory, an RRR triple junction is geometrically stable for all plate orientations.

Problem 7: Trench Suction and Slab Pull Forces

Calculate the driving force of plate tectonics: which single force accounts for over $90\%$ of total lithospheric plate drive?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Slab Pull ($F_{SP}$): As cold oceanic lithosphere subducts, basalt and gabbro undergo metamorphic phase transition into high-density eclogite ($\rho \sim 3.5\text{ g/cm}^3$ vs. mantle peridotite $\rho \sim 3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$). This negative buoyancy pulls the entire trailing plate seaward, dominating over ridge push ($F_{RP}$) and basal mantle drag ($F_D$).

Problem 8: Seismic Gap Method in Deep Trenches

How does the seismic gap hypothesis assist in forecasting mega-thrust earthquakes along oceanic trenches?

Step-by-Step Solution:

Subduction plate boundaries accumulate elastic strain at a steady rate defined by the plate convergence velocity ($v$). A segment of an oceanic trench (like the Kermadec or Kuril trench) that has historically produced great earthquakes ($M_w \ge 8.0$) but has experienced an anomalously long period of seismic quiescence represents a seismic gap. Elastic strain is locked and accumulating, making it a high-probability candidate for a future mega-thrust rupture.
