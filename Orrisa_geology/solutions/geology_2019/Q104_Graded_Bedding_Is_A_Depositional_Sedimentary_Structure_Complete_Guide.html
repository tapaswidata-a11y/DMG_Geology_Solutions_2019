1. Theoretical Foundation: Classification of Sedimentary Structures

Sedimentary structures provide vital clues to the physical processes operating during sedimentation. In genetic classifications (Pettijohn & Potter, 1964; Collinson et al., 2006; Boggs, 2014), structures are rigorously divided into Primary Depositional, Erosional (Scour), and Post-Depositional (Deformational / Diagenetic) structures:

Genetic Category Physical Genesis Classic Sedimentary Examples Option Analysis
PRIMARY DEPOSITIONAL STRUCTURES Formed directly during the physical deposition of sediment from flowing or settling fluid. GRADED BEDDING (Option A), Planar Bedding, Lamination, Ripple Cross-Lamination, Trough Cross-Bedding (Option B). Graded bedding represents the pure hydrodynamic depositional settling structure from waning suspension currents.
Primary Erosional (Scour) Structures Carved into cohesive substratum prior to deposition; preserved as sole marks on the undersides of beds. Flute casts, groove casts, bounce marks, prod marks, channels, scour-and-fill troughs. Erosional bedforms preceding deposition.
Post-Depositional Deformational Structures Formed after deposition by gravity collapse, liquefaction, pore-fluid escape, or tectonic loading. Collapse structures (Option C), Injection structures / Clastic dykes (Option D), Slump folds, Convolute bedding. Post-depositional disturbance; NOT primary depositional.
Diagenetic / Chemical Structures Formed by post-depositional chemical precipitation or dissolution in solid rock. Concretions, nodules, geodetic cavities, stylolites, cone-in-cone structures. Diagenetic authigenesis.
"A graded bed is one in which there is a systematic vertical change in grain size from coarse at the base to fine at the top. It is the premier depositional sedimentary structure resulting from the gradual waning of current velocity or settling from a dense suspension (such as a turbidity current). Each graded bed records a single discrete depositional event."
— F.J. Pettijohn & P.E. Potter, Atlas and Glossary of Primary Sedimentary Structures (Springer-Verlag, 1964)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 104
Which of the following is a depositional sedimentary structure ?
A Graded bedding
B Trough cross bedding
C Collapse structure
D Injection structure
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION A (Graded bedding) [with Option B also evaluated as a primary depositional bedform]
OPTION A • PRIMARY DEPOSITIONAL STRUCTURE (STANDARD KEY)
Graded bedding

Graded bedding is the textbook archetype of a primary depositional structure. It forms directly through the progressive gravitational settling of suspended particles from a waning turbidity current or flood surge, with coarse grains depositing first at the bottom and progressively finer particles settling on top.

OPTION B • PRIMARY DEPOSITIONAL BEDFORM
Trough cross bedding

Trough cross-bedding is also a primary depositional bedform (formed by migrating 3D dunes). However, because it involves an initial phase of erosional scouring of a curved trough followed by deposition, standard syllabi frequently designate graded bedding as the pure depositional structure.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Collapse structure

A collapse structure is a post-depositional deformational or diagenetic structure caused by underground dissolution (e.g., karst cave collapse or evaporite dissolution collapse breccias), completely non-depositional.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Injection structure

An injection structure (such as a clastic/sandstone dyke or sill) is a soft-sediment deformational structure formed when liquefied sediment is forcefully intruded into surrounding rocks under excess fluid pressure.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What is reverse (inverse) graded bedding and where does it typically form?
Answer: Inverse grading occurs where finer grains are at the base and coarser clasts are at the top, forming in high-concentration debris flows and grain flows.
Dispersive pressure (Bagnold effect) forces larger clasts toward the upper shearing surface of a high-density debris flow.
2. Who established the classic five-division turbidite succession containing graded bedding at its base?
Answer: Arnold H. Bouma (1962, The Bouma Sequence: $T_a, T_b, T_c, T_d, T_e$)
The basal $T_a$ division is massive to normally graded sand deposited directly from the decelerating turbulent head.
3. What is a flute cast?
Answer: An erosional sole mark formed when a turbulent vortex scoops out a hollow in soft mud, which is subsequently cast by the overlying sand.
Flute casts are primary erosional structures, not depositional structures.
4. What is a 'varve'?
Answer: A rhythmic, annually deposited couplet of thin sedimentary layers (light coarse silt in summer, dark organic clay in winter) in glacial lakes.
Varves are seasonal primary depositional structures used for precise geochronological dating.
5. What is the fundamental difference between lamination and bedding?
Answer: Lamination is less than 1 cm ($< 10\text{ mm}$) thick; bedding is greater than 1 cm thick (McKee & Weir, 1953).
By standard geological convention, strata thicker than 1 cm are beds; thinner planar units are laminae.
6. What structure forms when gas bubbles or water escape through a soft mud surface?
Answer: Pitted mud surfaces or pit-and-mound structures (spring pits).
Escaping gas bubbles burst at the surface, creating tiny circular crater-like pits.
7. How does graded bedding confirm that a vertical bed of turbidites has NOT been overturned?
Answer: If coarse grains are at the lower stratigraphic contact and fine grains are toward the upper contact, the bed is in normal right-way-up position.
If the fine mud is at the bottom and coarse pebbles are at the top, the sequence is inverted (overturned).
8. What sedimentary structure forms when a rapid flood carves a scoop into river sand and fills it with concave cross-strata?
Answer: Scour-and-fill structure
Scour-and-fill represents an initial erosional event followed immediately by deposition in the scoured depression.
