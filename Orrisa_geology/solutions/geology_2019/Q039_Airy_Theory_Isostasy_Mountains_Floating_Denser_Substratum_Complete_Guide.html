Planetary Geodynamics: Theories of Isostasy (Airy vs. Pratt)

Isostasy (coined by C.E. Dutton in 1889, from Greek isostasios, meaning "equal standing") describes the condition of gravitational hydrostatic equilibrium between Earth's rigid lithospheric crust and the denser, ductile underlying mantle asthenosphere. The concept originated during the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (1854) under Sir George Everest, when gravitational deflection of the plumb-line near the Himalayas yielded significantly less gravitational pull than predicted by the visible mass of the mountains.

  • Airy's Hypothesis (1855): Proposed by English Astronomer Royal Sir George Biddell Airy. Airy postulated that:
    • Earth's crust has a constant, uniform density ($\rho_c \approx 2.7 - 2.8\text{ g/cm}^3$) throughout all continents and mountains.
    • This lighter crust floats upon a denser, ductile substratum (the mantle, $\rho_m \approx 3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$), exactly like icebergs floating in water.
    • High mountains are mechanically supported not by high density, but by deep crustal roots plunging downward into the denser mantle substratum. The taller the mountain, the deeper its root: $$r = h \cdot \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_m - \rho_c}$$
  • Pratt's Hypothesis (1859): Proposed by Archdeacon John Henry Pratt. In Pratt's model, the crust has a uniform depth of compensation ($D$), but mountains have variable, lower densities (the higher the peak, the lighter the rock: $\rho \cdot H = \text{constant}$).
  • Seismic Validation: Modern deep seismic reflection and refraction profiling (e.g., receiver functions) confirms that continental mountain belts like the Himalayas and Alps indeed possess deep crustal roots extending down to $60-75\text{ km}$ into the denser mantle, proving that Airy's model is the fundamentally correct mechanism for mountain belts.
"According to Airy's theory of isostasy, the earth's crust is made of lighter material of uniform density ($\rho_c$) which is floating on a denser substratum ($\rho_m$). Just as an iceberg floats in water with a large part submerged beneath the water, the mountains have deep roots projecting down into the denser substratum. The higher the mountain, the deeper is its root." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Interior of the Earth: Isostasy, p. 32)
Airy's Isostatic Equilibrium Formulation
$$\text{Mass Conservation at Compensation Depth: } \quad \rho_c (h + T_0 + r) = \rho_c T_0 + \rho_m r$$ $$\rho_c h + \rho_c r = \rho_m r \implies \mathbf{r = h \left( \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_m - \rho_c} \right)}$$ $$\text{Total Crustal Thickness beneath Mountain: } \quad T = T_0 + h + r = T_0 + h \left( 1 + \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_m - \rho_c} \right)$$

For crustal density $\rho_c = 2.8\text{ g/cm}^3$ and mantle density $\rho_m = 3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$, the root ratio is $r / h = 2.8 / 0.5 = 5.6$. Every $1\text{ km}$ of topography requires $\approx 5.6\text{ km}$ of crustal root beneath it!

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q039
According to Airy’s theory of isostasy :
(A) Lighter mountains are floating on the lighter substratum
(B) Lighter mountains are floating on the denser substratum
(C) Denser mountains are floating on denser substratum
(D) Denser mountains are floating on the lighter substratum
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (B) Lighter mountains are floating on the denser substratum
Verified against Bangar (p. 32), Fowler (The Solid Earth, p. 195), and Turcotte & Schubert (Geodynamics). Sir George Airy established that the Earth's crust is composed of lighter rocks ($ ho_c pprox 2.7 ext{ g/cm}^3$) floating in hydrostatic equilibrium upon a denser mantle substratum ($ ho_m pprox 3.3 ext{ g/cm}^3$), supported by deep crustal roots.
(A) Lighter mountains are floating on the lighter substratum INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Under Archimedes' principle, a body cannot float in a fluid of equal or lesser density; it would sink immediately. For isostatic flotation to occur, the substratum must be mechanically denser than the floating mountain mass.
(B) Lighter mountains are floating on the denser substratum CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: Airy's fundamental thesis is that continental mountains consist of relatively light granitic/silicate crustal rocks ($\rho_c \approx 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$) that float upon a significantly denser, ductile ultramafic mantle substratum ($\rho_m \approx 3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$). Just as an iceberg floats in seawater with the majority of its bulk submerged as a root, tall mountains are held in gravitational equilibrium by deep crustal roots displacing the denser mantle substratum below.
(C) Denser mountains are floating on denser substratum INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Mountains are not denser than the substratum; they are composed of felsic and intermediate silicate rocks ($2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$), whereas the underlying asthenospheric mantle is composed of dense ultramafic peridotite ($3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$).
(D) Denser mountains are floating on the lighter substratum INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: If mountains were denser than the substratum, hydrostatic equilibrium would be physically impossible, and gravitational instability would cause the mountains to sink to the core.

8 Fully Worked Exam-Standard Practice Problems: Isostatic Balancing & Root Calculations
Problem 1: Himalayan Crustal Root Calculation
Crustal root $r = 28.0\text{ km}$; total thickness $T = 68.0\text{ km}$
The Tibetan plateau stands at an average elevation of $h = 5.0\text{ km}$. Standard crustal thickness is $T_0 = 35.0\text{ km}$. Assuming $\rho_c = 2.8\text{ g/cm}^3$ and $\rho_m = 3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$, calculate the root depth $r$ and total crustal thickness $T$.
$$r = h \left( \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_m - \rho_c} \right) = 5.0 \left( \frac{2.8}{3.3 - 2.8} \right) = 5.0 \times 5.6 = 28.0\text{ km}$$ $$T = T_0 + h + r = 35.0 + 5.0 + 28.0 = 68.0\text{ km}$$
Problem 2: Isostatic Uplift during Mountain Erosion
For $1000\text{ m}$ of erosion, isostatic rebound restores $\approx 848\text{ m}$
When erosion strips thickness $\Delta h$ of rock, the crust unburdens and rebounds by $\Delta z = \Delta h (\rho_c / \rho_m) = 1000 \times (2.8 / 3.3) = 848.5\text{ m}$. Thus, net lowering of the mountain summit is only $151.5\text{ m}$, prolonging mountain lifespans.
Problem 3: Anti-Roots beneath Ocean Basins
Mantle bulges upward beneath thin oceanic crust
Because ocean water has low density ($\rho_w = 1.03\text{ g/cm}^3$), oceanic crust is thin ($7\text{ km}$). The compensation depth is maintained by dense mantle ($3.3\text{ g/cm}^3$) rising to shallow depths of only $10\text{ km}$ below sea level (an isostatic anti-root).
Problem 4: Post-Glacial Rebound of Scandinavia
Negative isostatic anomaly recovering at $\approx 1\text{ cm/year}$
The melting of the $3\text{ km}$ thick Weichselian ice sheet removed immense mass. The depressed lithosphere is currently rebounding upward toward isostatic equilibrium as mantle asthenosphere flows back underneath.
Problem 5: Free-Air vs. Bouguer Anomalies over Mountain Chains
Free-Air anomaly is near zero; Bouguer anomaly is strongly negative
If a mountain chain is in isostatic equilibrium, its total mass is compensated at depth, making the Free-Air anomaly $\approx 0$. However, the Bouguer reduction removes the mountain mass without adding the low-density root, producing a large negative Bouguer anomaly (e.g., $-450\text{ mGal}$ over Tibet).
Problem 6: Flexural Isostasy (Vening Meinesz Model)
Lithosphere acts as an elastic plate with finite flexural rigidity ($D$)
Airy assumes localized hydrostatic pistons with zero lateral shear strength. Vening Meinesz (1931) introduced flexural isostasy, where regional elastic bending distributes loads over a broader flexural wavelength ($\lambda = 2\pi (D / ho_m g)^{1/4}$).
Problem 7: Great Trigonometrical Survey Discrepancy
5.236 seconds of arc plumb-line deflection deficit
In 1854, J.H. Pratt found the gravitational attraction of the Himalayas on a plumb-line at Kaliana was $5.236''$ less than expected from surface mass, directly proving the presence of an underlying low-density crustal root.
Problem 8: Isostatic Response to Reservoir Filling
Crustal flexural sag beneath deep reservoirs (e.g., Lake Mead, Kariba)
Filling colossal reservoirs places hundreds of billions of kilograms of water over small areas, causing immediate elastic deflection ($pprox 10-20 ext{ cm}$) followed by viscous isostatic subsidence.
