Planetary Hypsometry: Bimodal Crustal Elevation & Mean Ocean Depth

The first-order topography of the Earth is characterized by a fundamental bimodal distribution of surface elevations, vividly displayed on the global Hypsographic (Hypsometric) Curve. Unlike planets lacking plate tectonics and liquid water (such as the Moon or Venus, which exhibit unimodal Gaussian topographic distributions), Earth features two distinct dominant elevation levels reflecting its two distinct crustal regimes:

  • Continental Platform Peak: Centered at an average elevation of approximately $+840\text{ meters}$ above sea level, sustained by thick, low-density granitic-felsic continental crust ($\approx 35-40\text{ km}$ thick, density $\rho_c \approx 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$).
  • Oceanic Abyssal Floor Peak: Centered at an average depth of approximately $-3,729\text{ meters}$ (widely cited in classical oceanography and physical geology texts as $3,729\text{ m}$, or approximately $3,700-3,800\text{ m}$), governed by thin, dense basaltic oceanic crust ($\approx 7\text{ km}$ thick, density $\rho_o \approx 2.9-3.0\text{ g/cm}^3$).

The ocean basins cover approximately $361 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2$, representing $70.8\%$ of the Earth's total surface area ($510 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2$). The total volume of seawater contained within global oceans is estimated at approximately $1.332 \times 10^9\text{ km}^3$. Dividing this total oceanic volume by total oceanic surface area yields the mean depth of the global ocean:

Global Ocean Hypsometric Formulation
$$\bar{d}_{\text{ocean}} = \frac{V_{\text{ocean}}}{A_{\text{ocean}}} = \frac{1.332 \times 10^9\text{ km}^3}{3.61 \times 10^8\text{ km}^2} \approx \mathbf{3,690 - 3,729\text{ meters}}$$ $$\text{Standard Examination Benchmark: } \quad \mathbf{3,729\text{ meters}} \quad (\text{Classical oceanographic datum})$$ $$\text{Maximum Oceanic Depth: } \quad \text{Challenger Deep (Mariana Trench)} \approx 10,994 \pm 40\text{ meters}$$ $$\text{Mean Continental Elevation: } \quad +840\text{ meters} \quad \big| \quad \text{Maximum Continental Elevation: } \text{Mt. Everest} = +8,848.86\text{ meters}$$

The depth of $3,729\text{ m}$ represents the exact standard figure derived from early precision soundings by the Challenger Expedition and classical physical geography textbooks.

"The oceans cover about 71 percent of the earth's surface. The floor of the ocean lies at an average depth of about 3,729 meters (or about 3.8 kilometers) below sea level, in contrast to the continents which have an average elevation of about 840 meters above sea level. The deepest part of the ocean is the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific with a depth of about 11,000 meters." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (The Oceans: Physical Dimensions, p. 35) & Sverdrup et al., The Oceans (1942)

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q041
The average depth of the ocean is:
(A) 5,200 meters
(B) 4,630 meters
(C) 3,729 meters
(D) 3,062 meters
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (C) 3,729 meters
Verified against Bangar (p. 35), Sverdrup et al. (The Oceans), and standard physical geography curricula. The precise classical benchmark for the average depth of the global ocean is 3,729 meters (in contrast to the mean continental elevation of 840 meters).
(A) 5,200 meters INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: A depth of $5,200 ext{ m}$ corresponds to the deepest sectors of abyssal plains and deep ocean basins (e.g., the Argentine Basin or Philippine Basin), not the global oceanic average. Elevating the mean to $5,200 ext{ m}$ overlooks the elevated mid-ocean ridge systems ($2,500 ext{ m}$) and broad continental shelves ($0-200 ext{ m}$).
(B) 4,630 meters INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: $4,630 ext{ m}$ is an arbitrary distractor value well below the global average depth.
(C) 3,729 meters CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: The precise value of 3,729 meters (or $pprox 3.73 ext{ km} / 12,234 ext{ feet}$) is the classic, definitive geodetic and oceanographic benchmark for the mean depth of the world's oceans. Calculated by dividing total ocean water volume ($1.332 imes 10^9 ext{ km}^3$) by total oceanic surface area ($361 imes 10^6 ext{ km}^2$), it forms the foundation of all global hypsometric and isostatic calculations in geosciences.
(D) 3,062 meters INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: $3,062 ext{ m}$ significantly underestimates ocean depth; it is close to the average crest depth of mid-ocean ridges rather than the abyssal ocean floor.

Problem 1: Hydrostatic Pressure at Average Ocean Depth
Hydrostatic Pressure $P = 37.64\text{ MPa}$ ($371.5\text{ atmospheres}$)
Calculate the hydrostatic pressure at the average ocean depth $d = 3,729\text{ m}$, assuming mean seawater density $\rho_w = 1030\text{ kg/m}^3$ and $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$.
$$P = \rho_w g d = 1030 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 3729 = 3.768 \times 10^7\text{ Pa} = 37.68\text{ MPa} \approx 372\text{ atm}$$
Problem 2: Ratio of Ocean Volume to Continental Crust Volume Above Sea Level
Ocean water volume is $\approx 10.7$ times greater than subaerial continental volume
Continental area is $149 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2$ with mean height $+840\text{ m}$, giving a subaerial rock volume of $V_{\text{cont}} = 149 \times 0.84 = 125.16 \times 10^6\text{ km}^3$. The ocean water volume ($1,332 \times 10^6\text{ km}^3$) is $1332 / 125.16 \approx 10.64$ times larger. If the Earth were leveled flat, water would cover the entire globe to a depth of $\approx 2,600\text{ m}$.
Problem 3: Sonic Depth Finding (Echo Sounding) Calculation
Depth $d = 3,750\text{ meters}$
An echo sounder emits an acoustic pulse that reflects off the abyssal plain and returns in two-way travel time $t = 5.0\text{ seconds}$. Assuming average sound velocity in seawater $v_s = 1500\text{ m/s}$, calculate ocean depth.
$$d = \frac{v_s \cdot t}{2} = \frac{1500 \cdot 5.0}{2} = 3,750\text{ meters}$$
Problem 4: Calcite Compensation Depth (CCD) in Relation to Ocean Depth
CCD occurs at $4,000 - 4,500\text{ m}$ depth
Because the average ocean depth ($3,729\text{ m}$) is shallower than the global CCD ($4,000-4,500\text{ m}$), vast mid-ocean ridge flanks are draped in calcareous foraminifera oozes, whereas deeper abyssal plains ($>4,500\text{ m}$) dissolve calcite and deposit pelagic red clays.
Problem 5: Sea Level Drop during Last Glacial Maximum (LGM)
Eustatic sea level dropped by $\approx 120-130\text{ meters}$
During the LGM ($21,000\text{ yr BP}$), continental ice sheets locked up $50 \times 10^6\text{ km}^3$ of water, exposing the entire continental shelf ($0-120\text{ m}$) to subaerial erosion and river incision.
Problem 6: Origin of Bimodal Hypsometry
Differentiation of Earth into felsic continents and mafic ocean floors
The bimodal hypsographic curve is unique to Earth because plate tectonics continuously generates low-density, buoyant felsic crust through hydrous subduction magmatism, while generating dense, thin mafic crust at mid-ocean ridges.
Problem 7: Challenger Deep Discovery
HMS Challenger (1875) recorded 8,184 m; HMS Challenger II (1951) recorded 10,863 m
The deepest point on Earth was discovered during the 1872-1876 Challenger Expedition using weighted hemp soundings, and precisely surveyed by echo sounding in 1951.
Problem 8: Thermocline Depth in the Open Ocean
Permanent thermocline spans $200\text{ m}$ to $1,000\text{ m}$ depth
Below the warm mixed surface layer ($0-200\text{ m}$), ocean temperature plunges through the permanent thermocline from $20^\circ\text{C}$ down to $4^\circ\text{C}$. Deep abyssal ocean water ($>1,000\text{ m}$ to the bottom at $3,729\text{ m}$) remains near-freezing at $1-3^\circ\text{C}$.
