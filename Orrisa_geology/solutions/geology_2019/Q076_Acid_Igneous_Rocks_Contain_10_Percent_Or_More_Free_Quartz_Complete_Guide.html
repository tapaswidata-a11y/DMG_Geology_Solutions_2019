1. Theoretical Foundation: Chemical vs. Mineralogical Classification

In igneous petrology, rocks are classified chemically based on their total silica ($SiO_2$) weight percentage, and mineralogically based on the abundance of free quartz ($\alpha$-quartz modal volume percentage):

Rock Class Total Silica ($SiO_2$ wt%) Normative / Modal Free Quartz Characteristic Mineral Assemblage Representative Plutonic & Volcanic Examples
Acidic (Felsic / Silicic) > 66% (or > 65%) ≥ 10% Free Quartz (often 20–40%) Quartz + K-Feldspar (Orthoclase/Microcline) + Sodic Plagioclase ± Biotite/Muscovite Granite, Adamellite, Granodiorite (Plutonic); Rhyolite, Dacite (Volcanic)
Intermediate 52% – 66% < 10% Free Quartz (or silica-saturated) Plagioclase (Oligoclase–Andesine) + Hornblende + Biotite ± minor Pyroxene Syenite, Diorite, Monzonite (Plutonic); Trachyte, Andesite (Volcanic)
Basic (Mafic) 45% – 52% 0% Free Quartz (Silica-undersaturated or quartz-free) Calcic Plagioclase (Labradorite/Bytownite) + Clinopyroxene (Augite) ± Olivine Gabbro, Norite (Plutonic); Dolerite (Hypabyssal); Basalt (Volcanic)
Ultrabasic (Ultramafic) < 45% 0% Free Quartz (Desilicated) Olivine (Forsterite) + Pyroxenes (Enstatite/Augite); Feldspar < 10% Peridotite (Dunite, Lherzolite, Harzburgite), Pyroxenite (Plutonic); Komatiite (Volcanic)
"Acid igneous rocks are characterized chemically by a high silica content exceeding 65 or 66 percent. In mineralogical terms, because all bases (alumina, potash, soda, lime, iron, and magnesia) are completely saturated with silica, the surplus silica crystallizes as free quartz, which generally amounts to 10% or more of the rock's total modal volume. Rocks with more than 60-65% free quartz are rare monomineralic quartz-rocks (silexites/quartzolites) or sedimentary sandstones."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Petrology Section, pp. 110–112)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 76
Generally, acid igneous rocks contain :
A 10% or more free quartz
B 15% or more free quartz
C 60% or more free quartz
D 65% or more free quartz
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION A (10% or more free quartz)
OPTION A • CORRECT
10% or more free quartz

By standard petrological convention (Hatch, Wells & Wells; Bangar; IUGS granitoid field), an igneous rock is classified mineralogically as acidic when it contains at least 10% or more free quartz (modal volume). In the QAPF classification of plutonic rocks, the boundary between silica-saturated intermediate rocks (monzonite, syenite, diorite) and true acid granitoids requires $Q > 5\text{--}10\%$. Free modal quartz in normal granites ranges from 20% to 35%.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
15% or more free quartz

While many granites contain 15% to 30% quartz, 15% is an arbitrary cut-off and not the definition of acid igneous rocks. Rocks containing 10% to 14% free quartz (such as quartz syenites, quartz diorites, and granodioritic transition rocks) are already acid rocks supersaturated in silica.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
60% or more free quartz

This option confuses free modal quartz with total chemical silica ($SiO_2$). An igneous rock with 60% or more free quartz is an ultra-rare hydrothermal pegmatitic facies known as a silexite or quartzolite. Normal granites contain 60% to 70% feldspar, leaving 20% to 35% quartz.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
65% or more free quartz

This is a classic trap: acid rocks contain 65% or more total chemical silica ($SiO_2$), NOT 65% free quartz! In a granite containing 72% bulk $SiO_2$, over 45% of that $SiO_2$ is chemically bound in the crystal lattices of feldspars ($KAlSi_3O_8, NaAlSi_3O_8$) and micas, leaving approximately 25% to 30% as free quartz crystals.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. In the chemical classification of igneous rocks, a rock having 58% bulk $SiO_2$ and 4% free modal quartz is classified as:
Answer: Intermediate igneous rock
Intermediate rocks have bulk silica between 52% and 66% and contain less than 10% free modal quartz.
2. What distinguishes 'total silica' from 'free silica' in petrographic analysis?
Answer: Total silica is all $SiO_2$ present chemically; free silica is uncombined $SiO_2$ crystallizing as quartz/tridymite/cristobalite.
Most silica in igneous rocks is bound in framework and chain silicates (feldspars, amphiboles). Only the excess silica above cation saturation forms free quartz.
3. What is an igneous rock with greater than 90% modal quartz called in the IUGS classification?
Answer: Quartzolite (or Silexite)
Field 1 of the IUGS QAPF double triangle covers rocks where $Q / (Q+A+P) > 90\%$, representing the most quartz-rich igneous extreme.
4. A typical granite has 72 wt% $SiO_2$. What is its expected modal quartz percentage?
Answer: Approximately 25% to 35%
Feldspars (potash feldspar and albite) require ~65% $SiO_2$ in their formula stoichiometry. In a 72% bulk rock, ~25-30% modal quartz precipitates.
5. Why cannot olivine ($Mg_2SiO_4$) and free quartz ($SiO_2$) coexist in equilibrium under normal magmatic conditions?
Answer: Because they react to form orthopyroxene (enstatite): $Mg_2SiO_4 + SiO_2 \rightarrow 2 MgSiO_3$.
Olivine is an unsaturated nesosilicate that is unstable in the presence of free silica melt, immediately reacting peritectically to form enstatite.
6. Which volcanic rock is the compositional equivalent of a granite?
Answer: Rhyolite
Rhyolite is the fine-grained, extrusive/volcanic equivalent of granite, possessing >66% $SiO_2$ and ≥10% free normative quartz.
7. What is the Color Index ($M'$) of acid igneous rocks?
Answer: $M' < 20$ (Leucocratic to extreme leucocratic)
Acid rocks are dominated by felsic minerals (quartz + feldspars > 80%), leaving less than 20% dark ferromagnesian minerals.
8. What is the normative mineral calculated in CIPW norm when a rock has excess silica over all mineral bases?
Answer: Normative Quartz ($q$)
When all alumina, alkalis, lime, iron, and magnesia are saturated to form feldspars, pyroxenes, and magnetite, remaining $SiO_2$ is allocated to normative quartz ($q$).
