Biological Oceanography: Ecological Divisions of Pelagic Life (Plankton vs. Nekton)

The vast marine pelagic realm (the open water column) is divided ecologically into two fundamental categories of organisms based on their locomotory capabilities relative to ocean currents: Plankton and Nekton. Understanding their precise definitions is a core requirement in geological oceanography, paleoceanography, and micropaleontology.

  • Scientific Definitions of Marine Ecological Realms:
    1. Plankton (From Greek Planktos = Drifter / Wanderer):
      • Physical Principle: Plankton comprises all organisms that live suspended in the water column and are incapable of swimming against horizontal ocean currents. They drift, float, or wander passively wherever tides, waves, and ocean currents transport them.
      • Locomotion: While many planktonic organisms possess cilia, flagella, or swimming appendages capable of weak vertical migration (e.g., diel vertical migration of copepods), their propulsion cannot overcome horizontal fluid currents.
      • Subdivisions:
        • Phytoplankton (Autotrophic): Microscopic photosynthetic organisms inhabiting the euphotic zone ($0-200\text{ m}$), including diatoms, dinoflagellates, coccolithophores, and cyanobacteria.
        • Zooplankton (Heterotrophic): Microscopic to macroscopic animal drifters, including foraminifera, radiolarians, copepods, krill, and jellyfish.
    2. Nekton (From Greek Nektos = Swimming):
      • Physical Principle: Actively swimming pelagic animals capable of swimming against horizontal water currents and navigating independently across vast ocean distances.
      • Examples: Bony fish (teleosts), sharks, cephalopods (squids, cuttlefish, extinct ammonites and belemnites), and marine mammals (whales, dolphins).
    3. Benthos: Organisms living directly on the seabed (epifauna) or within bottom sediments (infauna).
"Plankton vs. Nekton: Marine organisms are divided into three groups according to their mode of life: (1) Plankton: Organisms that float or drift passively in the upper layers of the ocean, swept along by currents. (2) Nekton: Free-swimming animals capable of directing their own movements against ocean currents. (3) Benthos: Organisms dwelling on the sea bottom." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Paleoecology: Marine Environments, p. 258)

OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 • Paper-II • Question 66
Plankton generally refers to :
(A) Free swimming organisms
(B) Bottom dwelling organisms
(C) Burrowing organisms
(D) Unicellular organisms
✔ OFFICIAL EXAM VERDICT & FORENSIC DUAL-PERSPECTIVE ANALYSIS
(A) Free swimming organisms [Exam Key Phrasing: Free-floating / Water-Column Pelagic] PRIMARY EXAM INTENDED ANSWER
Forensic Analysis: In competitive state examinations, examiners frequently distinguish between (B) bottom-dwellers (benthos), (C) burrowers (infauna), and pelagic water-column organisms, loosely phrasing plankton as "free-floating or free-swimming organisms in water". While rigorous marine science restricts *free-swimming against currents* to nekton, examination boards commonly intend Option (A) to differentiate pelagic unattached organisms from substrate benthos.
(B) Bottom dwelling organisms INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Bottom-dwelling organisms are definitively classified as Benthos (Epifauna), completely distinct from plankton.
(C) Burrowing organisms INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Burrowing organisms living beneath the seafloor are classified as Infauna (Endobenthos).
(D) Unicellular organisms [Biological Grounding Alternative] MICROBIOLOGICAL VALIDITY
Forensic Analysis: In micropaleontology, plankton is predominantly represented by unicellular micro-organisms (diatoms, radiolarians, planktonic foraminifera, dinoflagellates). However, because plankton also encompasses multicellular organisms (jellyfish, salps, krill), Option (A) is typically the broad pelagic category targeted by examiners contrasting against bottom-dwellers (B and C).

8 Fully Worked Practice Problems: Biological Oceanography & Pelagic Ecology
Problem 1: Size Classification of Plankton
Femtoplankton (<0.2 µm), Picoplankton (0.2-2 µm), Nanoplankton (2-20 µm), Microplankton (20-200 µm), Mesoplankton (0.2-20 mm), Macroplankton (2-20 cm), Megaplankton (>20 cm)
Plankton spans extraordinary size ranges: from marine viruses (femtoplankton) and unicellular bacteria up to giant colonial siphonophores and jellyfish exceeding 2 meters (megaplankton).
Problem 2: Holoplankton vs. Meroplankton
Holoplankton spend entire life cycle in plankton; Meroplankton are temporary larval stages of benthic adults
Diatoms and radiolarians are holoplankton. In contrast, the swimming veliger larvae of clams and bipinnaria larvae of starfish are meroplankton that settle to become benthos as adults.
Problem 3: Graptolites as Paleozoic Plankton
Colonial hemichordates adapted for passive epi-planktonic or pseudo-planktonic drift
Graptolites (Class Graptolithina) developed air-filled nema attachments and vane-like floats (pneumatocysts), drifting globally across Paleozoic oceans as premier index fossils.
Problem 4: Diel Vertical Migration (DVM)
Ascending to surface waters at dusk to feed; descending to dark mesopelagic depths at dawn to avoid predators
DVM represents the largest synchronized animal biomass movement on Planet Earth, executed daily by zooplankton (copepods, krill, euphausiids).
Problem 5: Microfossil Oozes Formed by Planktonic Skeletons
Calcareous ooze (foraminifera, coccoliths) and Siliceous ooze (radiolaria, diatoms)
When plankton die, their micro-skeletons rain onto the abyssal plain. Above the CCD (~4,500 m), calcareous ooze accumulates; below the CCD, only insoluble siliceous ooze survives.
Problem 6: Morphological Adaptations for Buoyancy in Plankton
High surface-area-to-volume ratio, spines, gas vesicles, and lipid droplets
Stokes' Law governs settling velocity ($v \propto r^2$). Plankton develop radial spines, flattened shapes, and store low-density oils to minimize sinking rates without expending muscular energy.
Problem 7: Neuston vs. Pleuston
Neuston live in the top millimeter film; Pleuston have bodies projecting into the air above the surface
The Portuguese man o' war (*Physalia*) is a classic pleuston, using a gas-filled sail blown directly by surface winds.
Problem 8: Trophic Role of Phytoplankton in Global Carbon Cycling
Responsible for ~50% of global photosynthetic carbon fixation (The Biological Pump)
Marine phytoplankton fix ~50 gigatons of carbon annually, sequestering atmospheric CO2 into the deep ocean through fecal pellet and skeletal sedimentation.
