Geometric Classification of Faults: The 45-Degree Threshold

In structural geology and fault mechanics (Billings, 1972; Bangar, p. 212; Davis & Reynolds, 1996; Fossen, 2016), faults are systematically classified based on the angle of inclination (dip, $\delta$) of the fault plane relative to the horizontal datum:

  • High-Angle Faults: Faults whose fault planes dip at an angle greater than $45^\circ$ ($\text{Dip } > 45^\circ$).
  • Low-Angle Faults: Faults whose fault planes dip at an angle less than or equal to $45^\circ$ ($\text{Dip } \le 45^\circ$).
"According to the angle of dip of the fault plane, faults are divided into high-angle faults and low-angle faults. Faults which dip at an angle greater than $45^\circ$ are called high-angle faults. Faults which dip at an angle less than $45^\circ$ are called low-angle faults (such as thrust faults). Most normal faults are high-angle faults, typically dipping at $60^\circ$." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Chapter 7: Fault Classification, p. 212)

The selection of $45^\circ$ as the critical dividing threshold is rooted in the mathematical mechanics of displacement resolution:

Mathematical & Mechanical Significance of the 45-Degree Boundary
$$\text{Throw } T = S_d \cdot \sin \delta \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Heave } H = S_d \cdot \cos \delta$$ $$\text{When } \delta > 45^\circ: \sin \delta > \cos \delta \implies \mathbf{\text{Throw} > \text{Heave}} \quad (\text{Vertical displacement dominates})$$ $$\text{When } \delta < 45^\circ: \sin \delta < \cos \delta \implies \mathbf{\text{Heave} > \text{Throw}} \quad (\text{Horizontal displacement dominates})$$ $$\text{At } \delta = 45^\circ: \sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071 \implies \mathbf{\text{Throw} = \text{Heave}}$$

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 24)
24. High angle faults are those that dip greater than :
(A) 60 degree
(B) 65 degree
(C) 40 degree
(D) 45 degree  ✔
✔
Correct Answer: (D) 45 degree
In standard structural geology (K.M. Bangar, p. 212; Marland P. Billings, p. 145; Davis & Reynolds, 1996; Haakon Fossen, p. 338), high-angle faults are defined as faults that dip greater than 45 degrees ($ ext{Dip} > 45^\circ$).
(A) 60 degree Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: While $60^\circ$ is the classic theoretical dip angle for Andersonian normal faults in pristine isotropic rock ($ heta = 45^\circ + \phi/2$), it is not the formal boundary defining high-angle faults. Any fault dipping at $50^\circ$ or $55^\circ$ is legally and structurally classified as a high-angle fault.
(B) 65 degree Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: $65^\circ$ is an arbitrary steep dip value without mathematical or taxonomic standing as a boundary criterion.
(C) 40 degree Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A fault dipping at $40^\circ$ is a low-angle fault (such as a classic thrust fault dipping at $30^\circ - 40^\circ$).
(D) 45 degree Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: The universal textbook standard divides faults into low-angle ($\le 45^\circ$) and high-angle ($> 45^\circ$). At $45^\circ$, throw exactly equals heave; above $45^\circ$, vertical displacement exceeds horizontal displacement, defining a high-angle fault.

Anderson's Theoretical Conjugate Angles vs. High/Low Angle Classification

Anderson's fault classification elegantly mirrors the $45^\circ$ threshold:

  • Normal Faults (Extensional): $\sigma_1$ vertical $\implies ext{Dip } heta = 45^\circ + \phi/2 pprox 60^\circ > 45^\circ \implies$ High-Angle Faults.
  • Thrust Faults (Compressional): $\sigma_1$ horizontal $\implies ext{Dip } heta = 45^\circ - \phi/2 pprox 30^\circ < 45^\circ \implies$ Low-Angle Faults.
  • Strike-Slip Faults: $\sigma_2$ vertical $\implies ext{Dip } heta = 90^\circ > 45^\circ \implies$ Vertical / High-Angle Faults.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What is a "thrust fault", and what is its maximum allowable dip angle in standard structural classification?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A reverse fault with a dip angle of less than or equal to $45^\circ$ (typically $pprox 30^\circ$).
    Explanation: A reverse fault with dip $>45^\circ$ is termed a high-angle reverse fault; if dip $\le 45^\circ$, it is termed a thrust fault.
  • Practice Q2: What is a "low-angle normal fault" (LANF), and why was its discovery controversial in Mohr-Coulomb mechanics?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A normal fault dipping at $<30^\circ$; controversial because classic Andersonian mechanics predicts normal faults cannot initiate at dips $<45^\circ$.
    Explanation: Slip on low-angle normal faults requires extreme fluid overpressure ($u o \sigma_v$) or rolling-hinge rotation of initially steep faults.
  • Practice Q3: At what exact dip angle does the Throw of a fault equal its Heave?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Exactly $45^\circ$.
    Explanation: $ an \delta = ext{Throw} / ext{Heave}$. When $\delta = 45^\circ$, $ an 45^\circ = 1.0 \implies ext{Throw} = ext{Heave}$.
  • Practice Q4: What is a "blind fault"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fault that terminates upward within the crust and does not rupture the ground surface, often generating folding above its tip.
    Explanation: Blind thrust faults (e.g., 1994 Northridge earthquake) represent major seismic hazards because they lack surface fault scarps.
  • Practice Q5: What is a "transform fault", and what is its typical dip angle?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A plate-boundary strike-slip fault connecting offset mid-ocean ridge segments, typically with a vertical dip ($\delta pprox 90^\circ$).
    Explanation: First defined by J. Tuzo Wilson (1965); displacement is horizontal and terminates abruptly at active ridge crests.
  • Practice Q6: In geological maps, what map pattern distinguishes a high-angle fault from a low-angle thrust fault crossing rugged topography?
    ✔ Correct Answer: High-angle faults cut across topographic contours in straight lines (Rule of V's); low-angle faults trace highly sinuous paths parallel to topographic contour lines.
    Explanation: A vertical or near-vertical plane is unaffected by hills and valleys, cutting straight across the map.
  • Practice Q7: What is the "Byerlee's Law" friction coefficient for crustal rocks, and how does it determine fault dip?
    ✔ Correct Answer: $\mu pprox 0.85$ (for $\sigma_n < 200 ext{ MPa}$) and $\mu pprox 0.60$ (for higher stress), giving an internal friction angle $\phi pprox 30^\circ$.
    Explanation: Setting $\phi = 30^\circ$ into $ heta = 45^\circ + \phi/2$ yields the canonical $60^\circ$ dip for normal faults and $30^\circ$ for thrust faults.
  • Practice Q8: What is a "scissors fault"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A rotational fault where the hanging wall is upthrown at one end of the fault strike and downthrown at the opposite end.
    Explanation: Movement rotates around a central pivot point where throw is zero, reversing the apparent throw direction along strike.
