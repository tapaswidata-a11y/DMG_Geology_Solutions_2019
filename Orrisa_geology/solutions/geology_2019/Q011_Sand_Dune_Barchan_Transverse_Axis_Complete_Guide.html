Morphodynamic Classification of Aeolian Dunes & Wind Regimes

In aeolian geomorphology, sand dunes are classified based on the geometric relationship between the crest-ridge orientation (long axis) of the dune and the prevailing effective wind direction (Bagnold, 1941; McKee, 1979). When the long axis or crest of a sand dune is oriented perpendicular (transverse, $90^\circ$) to the prevailing sand-transporting wind, it belongs to the transverse dune family, of which the crescentic barchan is the classic, quintessential example.

"Dunes whose long axes are oriented across (transverse to) the direction of the prevailing wind are called transverse dunes. The most common and classic variety of transverse dune is the barchan, which is crescent-shaped in plan with its convex side facing upwind and its two horns or tips pointing downwind in the direction of the wind." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Geological Work of Wind: Sand Dunes, p. 23)

The two fundamental geometric classes of sand dunes are:

  • Transverse Dunes (Including Barchans): Formed where wind blows predominantly from a single unidirectional quadrant ($RDP/DP > 0.8$). The crest line and slip face are oriented at right angles (transverse) to the wind vector. When sand supply is abundant and continuous, transverse ridges form; when sand supply is limited on a hard desert floor, isolated crescent-shaped barchans develop with their long transverse axes and downwind-pointing horns.
  • Longitudinal Dunes (Seifs / Sand Ridges): Formed where wind blows from two converging directions or in bidirectional wind regimes. The long axis of the dune extends parallel to the resultant wind direction, forming straight or sinuous razor-backed sand ridges that can extend unbroken for tens of kilometers.

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 11)
11. A sand dune with its long axis transverse to wind direction is called :
(A) Longitudinal dune
(B) Sand ridge
(C) Parallel dune
(D) Barchan  ✔
✔
Correct Answer: (D) Barchan
In physical geology and aeolian geomorphology (K.M. Bangar, p. 23; Sam Boggs Jr., p. 270; McKee, 1979), a barchan is defined as a crescentic sand dune whose long ridge crest is oriented transverse (perpendicular) to the prevailing wind direction.
(A) Longitudinal dune Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A longitudinal dune (seif) is defined by its long crest axis extending parallel to the resultant prevailing wind direction, not transverse to it. They develop under bidirectional wind systems and can extend linearly for scores of kilometers.
(B) Sand ridge Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A sand ridge is a synonym for longitudinal or seif dunes (e.g., the vast sand ridges of the Simpson and Great Sandy Deserts in Australia), where continuous parallel ridges run parallel to the wind corridors.
(C) Parallel dune Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: "Parallel dune" is another descriptive term for longitudinal dunes whose elongate axes run parallel to the prevailing wind streamlines, the exact antithesis of a transverse dune.
(D) Barchan Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: Among the choices provided, the barchan is the only dune whose primary long crest axis is oriented transverse (perpendicular) to the wind direction. Wind blows against its convex back, saltating sand up to the crest, where it avalanches down the transverse lee slip face, while the lateral wings are dragged forward into downwind horns.

Kinematics of Barchan Migration: Bagnold's Equation

The forward migration velocity ($C$) of a barchan dune is inversely proportional to its height ($H$):

$$C = \frac{q}{\gamma_b \cdot H}$$

where $q$ is the sand transport rate ($\text{kg}/\text{m}\cdot\text{s}$) and $\gamma_b$ is the bulk unit weight of sand. Because the lateral flanks and horns of a barchan are much lower in height than the central dune crest ($H_{\text{horns}} \ll H_{\text{center}}$), the horns migrate forward substantially faster than the thick central body. This differential migration velocity is what generates and maintains the classic crescent shape with horns pointing downwind.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A small desert barchan dune has a crest height $H = 4.0\text{ m}$. In a given wind storm season, the measured volumetric sand flux across the crest is $Q_s = 24.0\text{ m}^3/\text{m}\cdot\text{year}$. Calculate the annual migration distance of the dune.
    ✔ Worked Answer: Migration velocity $C = 6.0\text{ meters/year}$.
    Derivation:
    Using Bagnold's kinematic dune migration formula:
    $$C = \frac{Q_s}{H} = \frac{24.0\text{ m}^3/\text{m}\cdot\text{year}}{4.0\text{ m}} = 6.0\text{ m/year}$$
    If the dune were $12.0\text{ m}$ high, its migration rate would slow to $C = 24.0 / 12.0 = 2.0\text{ m/year}$.
  • Practice Q2: Why do the horns of a Barchan dune point in the direction of the wind, whereas the horns of a Parabolic dune point against the wind?
    ✔ Correct Answer: In barchans, the lower horns migrate faster downwind than the high central crest; in parabolic dunes, vegetation anchors the horns while the blowout center migrates downwind.
    Explanation: Vegetation along coastal margins physically stabilizes the arms of parabolic dunes, allowing the unanchored central sand mass to be blown out downwind.
  • Practice Q3: What internal sedimentary structure uniquely diagnoses ancient aeolian sandstones in the stratigraphic rock record?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Giant, high-angle ($>25^\circ - 34^\circ$) tabular and wedge-planar cross-bedding sets with grainfall and grainflow laminae.
    Explanation: Sand avalanching down dune slip faces forms cross-bed sets that can reach tens of meters in thickness (e.g., Navajo Sandstone), showing inverse grading within grainflow avalanches.
  • Practice Q4: In what type of desert surface do isolated Barchans typically form?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Over hard, rocky or gravelly desert floors (reg/serir) with limited sand availability and a constant unidirectional wind regime.
    Explanation: When sand is scarce, sand grains cannot cover the entire surface, forming discrete migrating crescent dunes over the hard bedrock/pavement floor.
  • Practice Q5: What is a "draa"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A mega-dune bedform with wavelengths exceeding $500\text{ m}$ to several kilometers and heights up to hundreds of meters, upon which smaller dunes are superposed.
    Explanation: Wilson (1972) classified aeolian bedforms into a hierarchical spectrum: ripples (wavelength cm), dunes (wavelength meters to tens of meters), and draas (wavelength kilometers).
  • Practice Q6: What wind condition produces "Star Dunes" with pyramidal summits?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Complex, multidirectional wind regimes where winds blow from three or more distinct directions during different seasons.
    Explanation: Star dunes do not migrate laterally; instead, they grow vertically as sand is blown into the central summit from opposing directions, reaching heights over $300 - 400\text{ m}$.
  • Practice Q7: What is the difference between "grainfall" and "grainflow" (sand avalanche) deposition on a dune slip face?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Grainfall is the settling of saltating grains from suspension in the flow separation zone; grainflow is gravitational shearing and avalanching when oversteepened past the angle of repose.
    Explanation: Grainflow deposits display diagnostic inverse grading (coarse grains rise to the top of the shearing avalanche tongue due to kinetic sieving).
  • Practice Q8: What is a "barchanoid ridge"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A continuous wavy, sinuous transverse sand ridge formed by the lateral coalescence of multiple individual barchan dunes under increasing sand supply.
    Explanation: As sand supply increases, isolated barchans merge side-by-side, forming a sinuous transverse ridge with alternating crests and troughs.
