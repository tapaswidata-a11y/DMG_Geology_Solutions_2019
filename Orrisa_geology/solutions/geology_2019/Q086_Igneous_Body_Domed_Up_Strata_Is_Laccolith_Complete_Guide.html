1. Theoretical Foundation: Morphology & Mechanics of Concordant Intrusions

In structural and igneous geology, intrusive bodies are classified as concordant when their contacts are parallel to the bedding or foliation planes of the country rock, and discordant when they cross-cut pre-existing planar structures. The classic concordant forms are defined by their geometric cross-sections:

LACCOLITH (Option D) Arched / Convex Upward (Domed) Flat / Planar Horizontal Forces overlying sedimentary beds upward into a gentle to steep structural dome. High-viscosity felsic/intermediate melts (Henry Mountains, Utah; G.K. Gilbert, 1877).

"A laccolith is a concordant, mushroom-shaped or blister-like igneous intrusion that has an approximately flat floor and an arched or domed roof. When viscous magma is injected along bedding planes, instead of spreading laterally as a thin sill, it accumulates locally and pushes up the overlying strata into a magnificent dome or anticline."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Forms of Igneous Bodies, p. 118)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 86
An igneous body that has doomed up the strata into which it has been inserted is known as :
A Lopolith
B Dyke
C Bysmalith
D Laccolith
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION D (Laccolith)
OPTION D • CORRECT
Laccolith

By standard definition (G.K. Gilbert, 1877; Bangar; Mahapatra), a laccolith (from Greek lakkos = reservoir/cistern, and lithos = stone) is a concordant igneous intrusion characterized by a flat floor and a convex-upward roof that has physically arched or domed up the overlying strata.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Lopolith

A lopolith is a saucer-shaped intrusion where the floor and roof are concave upward (sagged downward into a basin under magmatic weight), the exact opposite of an upward-domed laccolith.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Dyke

A dyke is a discordant tabular body that cuts perpendicularly or obliquely across bedding planes. It does not dome up strata.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Bysmalith

While a bysmalith is an extreme cylindrical plug-shaped offshoot of a laccolith, its roof is lifted vertically along steep bounding peripheral faults like a piston, rather than gently doming continuous flexible strata.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. Who first described and named laccoliths in classical geological literature?
Answer: Grove Karl Gilbert (1877), in his classic report on the Henry Mountains of Utah.
G.K. Gilbert established the fundamental principles of laccolithic intrusion, hydraulic lift, and structural doming.
2. Why do basic (basaltic) magmas typically form sills rather than laccoliths?
Answer: Low viscosity allows basic magma to spread laterally for long distances; viscous felsic magma pools locally, pushing up the roof.
Magma viscosity governs morphology: low-viscosity basalt spreads into thin extensive sills, while high-viscosity dacite/rhyolite bulges into laccoliths.
3. What is a concordant intrusion located along the crest of an anticline or trough of a syncline called?
Answer: Phacolith
Alfred Harker (1909) coined 'phacolith' for lens-shaped intrusions that occupy fold crests or troughs where mechanical pressure is lowest.
4. What is a lopolith, and what famous igneous complex represents the world's greatest example?
Answer: A huge saucer-shaped intrusion with sagging floor; the Bushveld Igneous Complex of South Africa.
The Bushveld lopolith covers over $65,000\text{ km}^2$ and holds world-class platinum group elements (Merensky Reef) and chromite.
5. What is the surface area requirement for an igneous pluton to be classified as a batholith?
Answer: Greater than 100 square kilometers ($> 100\text{ km}^2$)
By definition, discordant plutons with exposed areas $< 100\text{ km}^2$ are termed stocks or bosses; those $> 100\text{ km}^2$ are batholiths.
6. What is a ring-dyke versus a cone-sheet?
Answer: Ring-dykes dip steeply outward/vertically; cone-sheets dip inward toward a common magmatic focus.
Cone-sheets form during upward magmatic pressure; ring-dykes form by cauldron subsidence when the magma chamber roof drops.
7. How can a sill be distinguished in the field from a buried lava flow?
Answer: A sill produces thermal metamorphism (baking) on BOTH its top and bottom contacts and has no vesicular top; a flow only bakes its floor.
Sills bake both upper and lower country rocks and may contain xenoliths from both; lava flows display ropy/vesicular tops and only bake the base.
8. What is a bysmalith?
Answer: A plugged laccolith whose roof has been vertically uplifted along circular fault boundaries.
J.P. Iddings (1898) defined a bysmalith as a cylindrical plug-shaped intrusion where uplift exceeded bending limits, shearing the bounding walls.
