Subglacial Hydrology & Morphogenesis of Eskers

An esker (derived from the Irish word eiscir, meaning a ridge or elevation) is a long, narrow, winding, steep-sided ridge composed of stratified, cross-bedded sand and gravel deposited by a subglacial, englacial, or proglacial meltwater stream flowing under hydrostatic pressure through an ice-walled tunnel within or beneath a melting or stagnant glacier. Genetically and lithologically, an esker is a classic and definitive glaciofluvial (fluvioglacial) deposit.

"Eskers are long, narrow, sinuous ridges of stratified sands and gravels deposited by subglacial streams flowing in tunnels beneath the melting ice. When the surrounding glacier melts away, these stream-bed deposits are left behind on the ground surface as winding, snake-like ridges that often run for miles across the landscape. They represent important glaciofluvial deposits." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Glacial Landforms: Fluvioglacial Deposits, p. 32)

Eskers are distinguished by several extraordinary hydrological and morphological characteristics:

  • Subglacial Pressurized Pipe Flow (R-Channels): Subglacial meltwater streams flow through semicircular tunnels melted upward into the basal ice, termed Röthlisberger channels (R-channels). Because water is confined under immense glaciostatic overburden pressure ($P_w \approx \rho_i g H$), it flows at high velocities ($>1 - 3\text{ m/s}$) and can even flow uphill across bedrock ridges, driven by the hydraulic gradient of the overlying ice surface slope!
  • Stratification & Sorting: Unlike true glacial till (which is unsorted and unstratified), esker sediments are water-laid, stratified, and well-sorted. They exhibit prominent climbing ripples, planar and trough cross-bedding, and imbricated gravel clasts.
  • Post-Depositional Collapse Faulting: When the confining ice walls melt away, lateral lateral support is removed, causing the flanks of the esker ridge to slump downward along normal gravity faults to attain the natural subaerial angle of repose of sand and gravel ($30^\circ - 35^\circ$).
  • Scale & Continuity: Eskers range from a few meters to over $40 - 50\text{ meters}$ in height, $50 - 200\text{ meters}$ in width, and can extend as segmented beaded ridges for tens to hundreds of kilometers (e.g., the Munro Esker in Ontario, Canada, extends $>250\text{ km}$).

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 12)
12. Esker is a :
(A) Thoroughly graded bed
(B) Deposit formed by wind action
(C) Glaciofluvial deposit  ✔
(D) Alluvial deposit rich in clay matter
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Glaciofluvial deposit
In glacial sedimentology and geomorphology (K.M. Bangar, p. 32; Sam Boggs Jr., p. 280; Arthur L. Bloom, p. 396; Benn & Evans, 2010), an esker is universally classified as a stratified, meltwater-laid Glaciofluvial (fluvioglacial) deposit.
(A) Thoroughly graded bed Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Graded beds (where grain size fines systematically upward from coarse sand to fine clay) are classic deposits of subaqueous turbidity currents (turbidites, Bouma sequence $T_a$ division) in deep marine or lacustrine basins. Eskers are characterized by cross-bedding and gravel lenses formed by pulsating meltwater streams, not thoroughly graded beds.
(B) Deposit formed by wind action Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Deposits formed by wind action (aeolian deposits) include sand dunes, sand sheets, and loess blankets. Eskers are high-energy fluvial gravel and sand deposits formed beneath glacial ice sheets, having no connection to wind transport.
(C) Glaciofluvial deposit Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: An esker is the definitive geological manifestation of a glaciofluvial deposit. It consists of sorted and stratified sand and gravel deposited by flowing meltwater streams confined within subglacial or englacial ice tunnels.
(D) Alluvial deposit rich in clay matter Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Alluvial deposits rich in clay are floodplain overbank silts, oxbow lake plugs, or backswamp muds. Eskers are coarse, highly permeable bedload deposits dominated by gravel, pebbles, and coarse-to-medium sand; fine clay is washed away down-ice into proglacial lakes by vigorous meltwater flow.

Engineering & Economic Significance of Glaciofluvial Eskers

Eskers are of enormous economic and civil engineering importance in glaciated shield terrains (e.g., Canada, Scandinavia, northern USA):

  • Prime Aggregate Source: Esker sands and gravels are naturally washed, sorted, and free of deleterious organic clays, making them the premier source of coarse aggregate for concrete production, asphalt paving, and railway ballast.
  • Artesian Groundwater Aquifers: Due to extremely high hydraulic conductivity ($K > 10^{-3} - 10^{-2}\text{ m/s}$), eskers buried beneath impermeable glacial clays form exceptionally prolific regional artesian aquifers.
  • Natural Highway & Railway Corridors: Because eskers are elevated, well-drained, porous ridges rising above wet, swampy muskeg and peat bogs in subarctic terrains, early pioneers and modern highway engineers utilize them as natural, elevated foundation routes for roads and railways.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What hydraulic condition allows subglacial meltwater streams to flow uphill over bedrock topographic divides, depositing eskers that cross mountain ridges?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Pipe flow under hydrostatic pressure governed by the hydraulic gradient of the sloping upper surface of the ice sheet.
    Explanation: Water in subglacial R-channels is pressurized by the weight of the ice. The hydraulic potential $\Phi = z + \frac{P_w}{\rho_w g}$ is determined by the ice surface slope (which is $\approx 11\times$ more influential than bedrock bed slope), forcing water to flow uphill over subglacial hills.
  • Practice Q2: Distinguish between an "Esker" and a "Kame".
    ✔ Correct Answer: An esker is a linear, sinuous ridge deposited in a subglacial channel; a kame is an isolated mound or conical hill deposited in an ice-surface crevasse or moulin delta.
    Explanation: Both are glaciofluvial, but eskers represent continuous stream-bed conduits, whereas kames represent localized pond or moulin delta accumulations.
  • Practice Q3: What sedimentary structure is commonly observed on the flanking margins of an esker, and what causes it?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Gravity-induced normal faults and slumping with dipping beds, caused by the melting of supporting lateral ice walls.
    Explanation: When the ice tunnel walls melt away, lateral confining stress is lost, causing the gravel layers along the sides to collapse downward to the natural angle of repose.
  • Practice Q4: What is a "beaded esker"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An esker consisting of a chain of periodic, bulbous mounds or beads connected by narrow ridges, reflecting annual pulses of sediment deposition at a retreating subglacial portal.
    Explanation: De Geer (1897) demonstrated that each bead represents a summer meltwater fan deposited where the tunnel exited into a proglacial lake, recording annual ice-front retreat rates.
  • Practice Q5: Why are eskers predominantly formed during periods of glacial stagnation or retreat rather than during active glacial advance?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Active advancing ice shears, deforms, and destroys delicate subglacial sediment tunnels; preservation requires stagnant or retreating ice.
    Explanation: If the glacier is actively flowing rapidly, basal shear stresses crush and disperse tunnel deposits into till. Eskers survive when ice becomes dynamically stagnant ($v \approx 0$) during deglaciation.
  • Practice Q6: What paleocurrent feature in an esker reveals the direction of subglacial meltwater flow?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Cross-bedding foreset dip directions and upstream pebble imbrication ($a(p)b(i)$ fabrics).
    Explanation: Fluvial cross-bedding dips down-current, while flat pebbles imbricate dipping up-current into the meltwater flow.
  • Practice Q7: In mineral exploration, how are eskers utilized in "drift prospecting" for diamondiferous kimberlites and gold deposits?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Heavy mineral sampling of esker gravels reveals indicator minerals (pyrope garnet, chromite, gold grains) that can be traced upstream to source kimberlite pipes.
    Explanation: Subglacial streams wash vast volumes of basal till, concentrating resistant heavy indicator minerals within the esker gravel matrix.
  • Practice Q8: What is a "Sandur", and how does it relate genetically to an esker?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A sandur is a broad proglacial outwash plain formed where meltwater streams emerging from subglacial tunnels (eskers) discharge and spread over open land.
    Explanation: Eskers represent the feeder channels within the ice; sandurs represent the unconfined braided alluvial fans deposited beyond the ice terminus.
