1. Theoretical Foundation: Defining Compressive (Crushing) Strength

In continuum mechanics and rock engineering, Compressive Strength (commonly termed crushing strength) is defined as the maximum compressive force expressed per unit area that a stone or rock specimen can withstand without crushing, collapsing, or rupturing under gradually applied axial load:

Strength Parameter Mode of Force Application Failure Mechanism Typical Relative Value for Rock
COMPRESSIVE STRENGTH (Option C) Axial Opposing Compressive Load pushing grains inward. Shear rupture along conjugate shear planes / microcrack coalescence. HIGHEST ($\mathbf{100\%}$ Baseline: 50 – 300 MPa).
Shear Strength (Option A) Parallel tangential forces sliding adjacent planes. Overcoming cohesion ($c$) and internal friction ($\sigma \tan \phi$). Intermediate ($~10\text{--}25\%$ of compressive strength).
Tensile Strength (Option B) Opposing pulling-apart forces stretching rock. Direct Mode-I opening of Griffith microcracks. LOWEST ($~5\text{--}10\%$ of compressive strength).
Bending (Flexural) Strength (Option D) Transverse beam bending load (modulus of rupture). Tensile fiber failure at the outermost convex surface. Low to moderate ($~10\text{--}15\%$ of compressive strength).
"The crushing strength or compressive strength of a stone is the maximum force per unit area which a stone can bear without crushing or rupturing. It is tested on a universal testing machine by crushing cubical or cylindrical specimens until complete failure occurs. A good building stone should have a crushing strength of not less than 1000 kg/cm² (100 MPa)."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Properties of Building Stones, p. 286)
$$\text{Definition: } \sigma_c = \frac{P_{\text{max}}}{A} \quad \left[\text{Units: } \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \equiv \text{MPa} \equiv \frac{\text{kgf}}{\text{cm}^2} \times 0.09806\right]$$ $$\mathbf{Maximum\ Force\ Per\ Unit\ Area\ Without\ Rupturing} \equiv \mathbf{Compressive\ Strength\ (Option\ C)}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 107
What is the maximum force expressed per unit area, which a stone can withstand without rupturing ?
A Shear strength
B Tensile strength
C Compressive strength
D Bending strength
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION C (Compressive strength)
OPTION C • CORRECT
Compressive strength

By standard engineering definition (IS: 1121 Part I; Bangar, p. 286; Mahapatra), the maximum compressive load per unit cross-sectional area that a building stone can withstand before collapsing, crushing, or rupturing is formally defined as its Compressive strength (or crushing strength).

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Shear strength

Shear strength is the maximum shearing or sliding stress along a plane that a stone can resist, governed by cohesion and angle of internal friction ($\tau = c + \sigma \tan \phi$), not the general crushing capacity per unit area.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Tensile strength

Tensile strength is the resistance of stone against pulling or tearing apart forces, which in rocks is extremely low (only 5% to 10% of compressive strength).

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Bending strength

Bending (transverse) strength measures resistance against flexural bending moments when supported at ends and loaded in the center, not pure compressive rupture.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What is the minimum crushing strength required for stones used in heavy masonry structures according to Indian Standard codes?
Answer: 100 MPa ($1000\text{ kg/cm}^2$) for heavy structures; 35 MPa for normal building works.
IS: 1905 dictates minimum crushing strength thresholds to ensure safety against structural failure.
2. What shape of laboratory specimen is mandated by IS: 1121 for testing compressive strength of natural stone?
Answer: Either cubes ($70\text{ mm} \times 70\text{ mm} \times 70\text{ mm}$) or cylinders ($L/D = 1.0\text{ to } 2.0$).
Cube specimens must be dressed flat with parallel faces within 0.1 mm tolerance to prevent eccentric edge loading.
3. What is the angle ($\theta$) of the shear rupture failure plane relative to the major principal stress ($\sigma_1$) in Mohr-Coulomb failure?
Answer: $\theta = 45^\circ + \phi / 2$ (typically $55^\circ\text{--}65^\circ$ to the horizontal platen).
Failure planes form inclined shear cones governed by the internal friction angle ($\phi$) of the rock material.
4. What is the conversion factor between MPa ($\text{N/mm}^2$) and $kg/cm^2$?
Answer: $1\text{ MPa} \approx 10.197\text{ kg/cm}^2$ (or $1\text{ kg/cm}^2 \approx 0.098\text{ MPa}$).
For quick estimation: $100\text{ MPa} \approx 1000\text{ kg/cm}^2$.
5. What happens to the compressive strength of a rock if the rate of loading is increased significantly?
Answer: The apparent compressive strength increases (dynamic strength is higher than static strength).
Fast loading gives microcracks insufficient time to propagate, yielding artificially higher peak strengths (IS codes specify 0.5 to 1.0 MPa/sec).
6. Which building stone has the highest crushing strength among common Indian stones?
Answer: Basalt (Deccan Trap) and Fine-grained Dolerite (up to 300-350 MPa).
Dense ophitic micro-crystalline plagioclase-pyroxene intergrowth provides colossal mechanical resistance.
7. What is ductile versus brittle failure in rocks under compression?
Answer: Brittle rocks fracture suddenly with little plastic strain; ductile rocks undergo substantial plastic flow before failure without sudden loss of strength.
Under shallow crustal conditions, intact rocks behave in a brittle manner; deep confining pressure drives the brittle-ductile transition.
8. What is Young's Modulus ($E$) of a rock in compressive stress testing?
Answer: The ratio of axial stress to axial strain within the linear elastic range ($E = \Delta \sigma / \Delta \epsilon$).
Young's modulus represents elastic stiffness; fresh granite has $E \approx 40\text{--}70\text{ GPa}$.
