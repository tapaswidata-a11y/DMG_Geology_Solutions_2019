1. Theoretical Foundation: Grain Size Skewness & Mathematical Formulation

In sediment granulometry, skewness ($Sk$) measures the degree of asymmetry of a grain-size frequency distribution curve relative to a normal (Gaussian) distribution. In Krumbein's phi ($\phi$) scale, where coarse particles have lower/negative values and fine particles have higher positive values ($\phi = -\log_2 d_{\text{mm}}$), skewness is calculated using Folk and Ward's (1957) Graphic Skewness ($Sk_I$):

Skewness Class Mathematical Criterion ($Sk_I$) Grain Size Distribution Character Typical Geological Environment & Cause
NEGATIVE SKEWNESS (Coarse-skewed) (Option C) $Sk_I < -0.10$ (down to $-1.0$) Excess coarse material; frequency curve has an elongated tail extending toward negative phi (coarse size). High-energy beaches; sediments dominated by coarse organic detritus (mollusc shells, coral/skeletal bioclasts).
Symmetrical (Near-Symmetrical) $-0.10 \le Sk_I \le +0.10$ Balanced Gaussian distribution; mean equals median. Aeolian dune sands; perfectly sorted beach sands without shell lags.
Positive Skewness (Fine-skewed) $Sk_I > +0.10$ (up to $+1.0$) Excess fine material; frequency curve has an elongated tail extending toward positive phi (fine size: silt/clay). Fluvial river sands (silt trapped in interstices), quiet lagoons, turbidity currents.
"When a sediment is dominantly composed of or enriched in organic detritus (such as coarse mollusc shell fragments, foraminifera, and echinoderm debris), it introduces an abundant coarse fraction that cannot be winnowed away by waves. On the phi scale, this creates a prominent coarse tail, causing the distribution to exhibit pronounced negative skewness. Friedman (1961, 1967) demonstrated that negative skewness is the most reliable criterion separating beach sediments containing shell detritus from positively skewed river sands."
— G.M. Friedman, Dynamic processes and statistical parameters compared for size frequency distribution of beach and river sands (Journal of Sedimentary Petrology, 1967)
$$\text{Folk and Ward (1957) Inclusive Graphic Skewness:}$$ $$Sk_I = \frac{\phi_{16} + \phi_{84} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{84} - \phi_{16})} + \frac{\phi_5 + \phi_{95} - 2\phi_{50}}{2(\phi_{95} - \phi_5)}$$ $$\text{Coarse Organic Detritus Tail } \implies \phi_5 \ll \phi_{50} \implies \mathbf{Sk_I < 0\ (Negative\ Skewness\ \equiv Option\ C)}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 100
If sediment is dominantly compose of organic detritus, it is known to indicate :
A Complex skewness
B Positive skewness
C Negative skewness
D No skewness
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION C (Negative skewness)
OPTION C • CORRECT
Negative skewness

By classical sediment granulometry (Friedman, 1961, 1967; Folk, 1968; Boggs), when sediment contains dominant organic detritus (shell debris, bioclasts, skeletal fragments), these large biogenic particles form an extensive coarse-grained tail. In phi ($\phi$) units, a tail towards coarse sizes corresponds to lower/negative values, mathematically resulting in negative skewness.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Complex skewness

'Complex skewness' is not a recognized statistical parameter in sedimentary geology; polymodal distributions are termed bimodal or polymodal, while skewness is strictly scalar (positive, negative, or zero).

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Positive skewness

Positive skewness reflects an excess of fine-grained tail (silt and clay grade particles), typical of quiet fluvial channels or settling basins, the exact opposite of coarse shell detritus.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
No skewness

'No skewness' ($Sk = 0$) indicates a perfectly symmetrical Gaussian distribution, which occurs only in well-sorted mono-mineralic sands where both coarse and fine tails are equally balanced.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. Why do river sands characteristically display positive skewness, while beach sands display negative skewness?
Answer: Rivers deposit a tail of fine silt/clay as currents wane; beach wave backwash winnows out fines, leaving coarse lag and bioclastic debris.
Friedman's boundary plot ($Sk$ vs Mean size) uses this exact distinction to identify ancient depositional environments.
2. In Folk and Ward's skewness classification, what range of values corresponds to 'strongly coarse-skewed'?
Answer: $Sk_I$ from $-0.30$ to $-1.00$
Values from $-0.10$ to $-0.30$ are coarse-skewed; values below $-0.30$ are strongly coarse-skewed (negative).
3. What is kurtosis ($K_G$) in grain-size analysis?
Answer: A measure of the peakedness of the frequency distribution curve relative to normal (mesokurtic).
Excessively peaked curves are leptokurtic ($K_G > 1.0$); flat-topped curves are platykurtic ($K_G < 1.0$).
4. What is the mean grain size ($M_z$) formula defined by Folk and Ward?
Answer: $M_z = \frac{\phi_{16} + \phi_{50} + \phi_{84}}{3}$
The graphic mean uses the 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles from the cumulative frequency curve.
5. What does sorting ($\sigma_I$, standard deviation) measure in a sediment sample?
Answer: The uniformity or spread of grain sizes around the mean.
$\sigma_I < 0.35\phi$ is very well sorted; $\sigma_I > 2.0\phi$ is very poorly sorted.
6. If a sediment distribution has $\text{Mean} < \text{Median} < \text{Mode}$ on the millimeter scale, what skewness does it possess?
Answer: Negative Skewness (tail to coarse on phi scale).
When the mean is pulled away from the median toward the coarse tail, the skewness is negative on the phi scale.
7. What is a coquina or lumachelle in relation to bioclastic organic detritus?
Answer: A limestone composed entirely of cemented coarse organic shell detritus.
Coquina represents the ultimate lithified manifestation of negatively skewed bioclastic sediment.
8. What heavy mineral placer sands along the Odisha coast exhibit negative skewness?
Answer: Monazite-ilmenite-garnet heavy mineral beach placers (e.g., Chhatrapur / Gopalpur).
Severe storm wave winnowing concentrates heavy, dense mineral grains, leaving a negatively skewed coarse grain-size tail.
