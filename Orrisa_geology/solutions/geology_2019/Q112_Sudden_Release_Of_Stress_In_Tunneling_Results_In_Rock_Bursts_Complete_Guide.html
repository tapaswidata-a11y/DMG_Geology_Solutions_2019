1. Theoretical Foundation: Geomechanics of Rock Bursts in Deep Excavations

In deep underground civil tunneling and mining excavations (typically at depths exceeding 500 to 1000 meters), rocks exist under colossal lithostatic confining stresses ($\sigma_v = \rho g z$). When an opening is blasted or bored, the confining support is instantly removed, redistributing stress into severe concentrations around the excavation periphery. The sudden explosive release of stored elastic strain energy in massive, brittle rocks causes violent rock ejection known as a Rock Burst:

Tunneling Phenomenon Physical / Mechanical Mechanism Speed of Occurrence Geological Pre-Conditions
ROCK BURST (Strainburst) (Option B) Sudden, violent explosive release of accumulated elastic strain energy as tangential stress ($\sigma_\theta$) exceeds rock strength. Instantaneous (Microseconds to seconds; gunshot-like explosion). Deep overburden (>500 m); hard, brittle, massive crystalline rocks (granites, quartzites, gneisses) with zero open joints.
Rock Fall (Option A) Gravitational detachment of loose, jointed blocks from the tunnel roof. Rapid gravity fall. Shallow tunnels; heavily fractured, blocky, intersecting joint sets.
Squeezing Ground Time-dependent ductile inward plastic creep of overstressed rock. Slow (Days to months). Weak, ductile rocks under high stress (shales, phyllites, fault gouge).
Water Gush (Option D) Sudden inrush of groundwater through karst channels or fault zones. Sudden flooding. Karst limestone cavities or permeable water-bearing shear zones.
"In deep tunnels driven through hard, brittle, massive rocks (like quartzites, granites, or traps), the rock is subjected to colossal in-situ stresses. When the tunnel is excavated, the rock around the boundary expands into the opening. If the elastic strain energy stored in the rock is released suddenly and violently, slabs of rock fly off the walls and roof with explosive force, accompanied by a sound like a cannon shot. This dangerous phenomenon is known as a rock burst."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Tunnels and Underground Works, p. 305)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 112
The sudden release of stress in rocks during tunneling will normally result in :
A Rock fall
B Rock bursts
C Blockage
D Water gush
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (Rock bursts)
OPTION B • CORRECT
Rock bursts

By unanimous rock mechanics consensus (Hoek & Brown; Barton; Bangar, p. 305; Mahapatra), the sudden, violent, explosive release of stored strain energy in high-stress rock environments during underground tunneling produces rock bursts (spontaneous ejection and shattering of rock slabs from the excavation boundary).

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Rock fall

A rock fall is the passive falling of unkeyed, loosened rock blocks under gravity along pre-existing joints, not a dynamic explosion caused by the sudden release of high in-situ strain energy.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Blockage

'Blockage' is an informal descriptive consequence of collapsed material in a tunnel, not the geomechanical stress-release process itself.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Water gush

A water gush is a hydrogeological inrush caused by encountering a confined, pressurized aquifer or flooded fault zone, entirely independent of strain energy release in brittle rocks.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. In which famous Indian deep gold mining area were rock bursts a catastrophic historical hazard claiming numerous miners' lives?
Answer: The Kolar Gold Fields (KGF) in Karnataka (Champion Reef Mine down to >3200 m depth).
At depths exceeding 3 km in massive amphibolitic schists, explosive rock bursts registered as local earthquakes on seismographs.
2. What preventive drilling technique is used to alleviate stress concentrations and prevent rock bursts ahead of a tunnel face?
Answer: Destress blasting (or stress-relief drilling).
Drilling large-diameter pilot relief holes or detonating small cushion charges fractured the rock ahead, dissipating strain energy harmlessly.
3. What modern specialized rock reinforcement bolt is designed to absorb colossal kinetic energy during a rock burst without snapping?
Answer: Yielding rockbolts (e.g., D-Bolt, Cone Bolt, or Garford bolt).
Yielding bolts can stretch 15% to 20% plastically while maintaining high load resistance, dissipating the kinetic shock of ejected rock.
4. What is a microseismic monitoring system in deep tunneling?
Answer: A network of acoustic geophones that record high-frequency acoustic emissions generated by propagating microcracks prior to a rock burst.
Spiking microseismic event rates provide early warning of an imminent strainburst, allowing evacuation of tunnel crews.
5. What is the difference between a strainburst and a fault-slip rock burst?
Answer: A strainburst is local explosive spalling at the tunnel skin; a fault-slip burst is an induced earthquake caused by shearing along a nearby major fault.
Fault-slip bursts generate massive seismic magnitudes ($M_L > 2.0\text{--}3.5$) that can destroy hundreds of meters of tunnel.
6. Which Himalayan tunnel project experienced severe rock bursts during excavation through high-cover quartzite massifs?
Answer: The Rohtang (Atal) Tunnel and Nathpa Jhakri Hydroelectric Tunnel (Himachal Pradesh).
Overburden exceeding 1500 to 1900 m in brittle crystalline quartzites triggered intense strainbursting during drilling.
7. Why are heavily jointed, fractured rocks LESS prone to rock bursts than completely intact, massive rocks?
Answer: Joint planes allow the rock mass to deform, slip, and relieve stress gradually, preventing the storage of high elastic strain energy.
Massive, unjointed rocks behave like a giant loaded spring, holding immense strain until catastrophic failure occurs.
8. What is 'overbreak' in tunneling?
Answer: The volume of rock excavated beyond the neat designed perimeter profile of the tunnel.
Rock bursts, unfavorable joint dips, and excessive blasting charges dramatically increase overbreak, requiring costly concrete backfilling.
