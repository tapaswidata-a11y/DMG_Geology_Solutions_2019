1. Theoretical Foundation: Relief Displacement in Aerial Photography

In a true vertical aerial photograph, terrain features are imaged through a central perspective projection centered at the camera lens. Unlike an orthogonal map where scale is uniform and projection is vertical everywhere, vertical aerial photographs undergo relief displacement (also known as height displacement).

Relief displacement ($d$) is defined as the shift or displacement in the photographic position of an elevated point relative to its true datum position, caused purely by the height of the object above the reference datum. For a vertical aerial photograph, the mathematical formula for relief displacement is (Wolf & Dewitt; Lillesand & Kiefer):

$$d = \frac{r \cdot h}{H}$$

where:

  • $d$ = Relief displacement on the photograph or ground reference scale.
  • $r$ = Radial distance from the nadir point (or principal point) to the top of the elevated object.
  • $h$ = Physical height of the vertical object above the datum.
  • $H$ = Flying height of the aircraft above the same datum ($H \gg h$).

From this governing equation, fundamental geometric laws of relief displacement are established:

Photogrammetric Property Mathematical Dependence Physical Meaning
Direct Proportionality to Radial Distance ($r$) $$d \propto r \quad (\text{for constant } h \text{ and } H)$$ An object situated further from the nadir point experiences proportionally greater relief displacement than an identical object closer to nadir.
Direct Proportionality to Object Height ($h$) $$d \propto h \quad (\text{for constant } r \text{ and } H)$$ A taller tower exhibits greater relief displacement than a shorter tower at the same radial distance.
Inverse Proportionality to Flying Height ($H$) $$d \propto \frac{1}{H}$$ Higher altitude flights compress perspective distortions and minimize relief displacement.
Radial Directionality Radially outward from the Nadir Point Points elevated above datum displace outward away from nadir; depressions displace inward toward nadir.
Zero Displacement at Nadir $$r = 0 \implies d = 0$$ At the exact nadir point, the line of sight is purely vertical; no relief displacement occurs.

Step-by-Step Analytical Proof for Towers A and B:

We are given two towers, $A$ and $B$, with identical heights ($h_A = h_B = h$) situated diametrically opposite across the nadir point:

  • Radial distance of Tower $A$ from nadir: $r_A = 3\text{ km}$.
  • Radial distance of Tower $B$ from nadir: $r_B = 5\text{ km}$.
  • Aircraft flying height is constant: $H_A = H_B = H$.

Applying the relief displacement formula:

$$d_A = \frac{r_A \cdot h}{H} = \frac{3 \cdot h}{H}$$ $$d_B = \frac{r_B \cdot h}{H} = \frac{5 \cdot h}{H}$$ $$\frac{d_A}{d_B} = \frac{r_A}{r_B} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6 \implies d_A = 0.6 \cdot d_B < d_B$$

Therefore, the height displacement of A will be less than that of B ($\mathbf{d_A < d_B}$, Option A). Furthermore, because both towers are elevated above the ground datum, both displacements point radially outward away from the nadir point (away from each other, refuting Option D).

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 127
A and B are two towers of equal height diametrically opposite on either side of the nadir point at 3 km and 5 km distances. Then, which one of the following statements is correct ?
A Height displacement of A will be less than that of B
B Height displacement of B will be less than that of A
C Height displacement of A and B is equal
D Height displacement of A and B will be towards each other
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION A (Height displacement of A will be less than that of B)
OPTION A • CORRECT
Height displacement of A will be less than that of B

By the relief displacement formula $d = \frac{r \cdot h}{H}$, relief displacement is directly proportional to the radial distance ($r$) from the nadir point. Since Tower A is located at $r_A = 3\text{ km}$ and Tower B at $r_B = 5\text{ km}$, and both possess identical heights $h$, $d_A = \frac{3h}{H} < d_B = \frac{5h}{H}$. Hence, the height displacement of Tower A is strictly less than that of Tower B.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Height displacement of B will be less than that of A

This contradicts the governing linear relationship $d \propto r$. Tower B is further from the nadir point ($5\text{ km} > 3\text{ km}$); therefore, Tower B must experience greater, not lesser, relief displacement.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Height displacement of A and B is equal

Equal height displacement would only occur if both towers were located at identical radial distances ($r_A = r_B$). Since $3\text{ km} \ne 5\text{ km}$, their displacements cannot be equal.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Height displacement of A and B will be towards each other

Relief displacement for any feature elevated above ground datum acts radially outward away from the nadir point. Because the towers lie on opposite sides of nadir, their tops lean outward in opposite directions (away from each other), never towards each other.

1. On a vertical aerial photograph taken from a flying height of $H = 1200\text{ m}$ above flat ground, the image of a chimney has its base at $r_b = 60\text{ mm}$ and top at $r_t = 65\text{ mm}$ from the principal point. What is the height of the chimney?
Answer: Approximately $92.3\text{ meters}$.
$$h = \frac{d \cdot H}{r_t} = \frac{(65 - 60)\text{ mm} \cdot 1200\text{ m}}{65\text{ mm}} = \frac{5 \cdot 1200}{65} \approx 92.31\text{ m}$$.
2. From which photographic point is relief displacement radially directed in a truly vertical aerial photograph?
Answer: The Nadir Point (which coincides identically with the Principal Point in a vertical photograph).
In tilted aerial photographs, relief displacement radiates from the nadir point, whereas tilt displacement radiates from the isocenter.
3. Does relief displacement represent an error or an advantage in photogrammetry?
Answer: It is a geometric distortion for 2D planimetric mapping, but it is the essential advantage that creates stereoscopic parallax and enables 3D height measurement.
Without relief displacement, stereoscopic 3D terrain perception and elevation extraction would be impossible.
4. What happens to the relief displacement of a hill if the aircraft flying height ($H$) is doubled while maintaining camera focal length?
Answer: The relief displacement is halved ($d \propto 1/H$).
Flying at higher altitude minimizes perspective distortion across the photographic frame.
5. If a geological fault scarp is located directly at the nadir point of a vertical aerial photo, what is its photographic relief displacement?
Answer: Exactly zero ($d = 0$).
Because radial distance $r = 0$, $d = (0 \cdot h) / H = 0$. The camera looks straight down along the scarp face.
6. In a tilted photograph with tilt angle $\theta$, how do the Principal Point ($P$), Isocenter ($I$), and Nadir ($N$) align?
Answer: They lie along the principal line in the order $P \to I \to N$, separated by distance $f \tan(\theta/2)$ between $P$ and $I$, and $f \tan(\theta/2)$ between $I$ and $N$.
The isocenter $I$ is the origin of tilt displacement, while nadir $N$ is the origin of relief displacement.
7. What is the fundamental difference between the geometry of an aerial photograph and a topographic map?
Answer: An aerial photograph has central perspective projection with scale varying by relief; a map has orthogonal projection with uniform scale.
Orthorectification mathematically transforms central perspective aerial photos into map-accurate orthogonal images.
8. What is stereoscopic parallax ($p$) in aerial photography?
Answer: The apparent displacement of the position of an object from one photograph to the next in an overlapping flight strip caused by aircraft motion ($p = x - x'$).
Differential parallax ($\Delta p$) is directly proportional to terrain elevation differences: $\Delta h = \frac{\Delta p \cdot H'}{B + \Delta p}$.
