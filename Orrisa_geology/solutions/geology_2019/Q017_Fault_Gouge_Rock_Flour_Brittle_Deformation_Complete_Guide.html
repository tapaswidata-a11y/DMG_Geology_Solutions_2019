Fault Rocks & The Brittle-Ductile Transition in Crustal Shear Zones

In structural geology and fault mechanics, fault gouge (and finely pulverized rock flour) is an unhardened, non-cohesive or cohesive, fine-grained powder-like rock material produced by mechanical abrasion, frictional grinding, and cataclastic crushing of wall rocks along a fault zone during active fault displacement. Fault gouge and rock flour are definitively produced under brittle deformation regimes in the upper crust.

"During faulting under shallow crustal conditions, the rocks along the fault plane are crushed and pulverized due to intense friction. When the rock is reduced to a fine, clay-like flour or powder, it is called fault gouge or rock flour. When the crushed fragments are angular and larger, the rock is called fault breccia. These cataclastic rocks are the classic products of brittle deformation in the upper crust." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Faults: Fault Rocks & Breccias, p. 215)

The structural character of fault rocks changes systematically with crustal depth, governed by the Brittle-Ductile (Frictional-Viscous) Transition (Sibson, 1977; Scholz, 2002):

  • Upper Crust ($<10 - 15\text{ km}$, $T < 300^\circ\text{C}$): Brittle Regime (Frictional Flow / Cataclasis): Deformation is dominated by microcracking, frictional sliding, grain rotation, and mechanical pulverization. Cohesion is lost during failure. This produces non-foliated cataclastic fault rocks:
    • Fault Breccia: Visible angular clasts ($>2\text{ mm}$) constituting $>30\%$ of the rock, set in a crushed matrix.
    • Fault Gouge / Rock Flour: Ultrafine pulverized powder (clay-sized particles $<0.1\text{ mm}$) constituting $>70\%$ of the rock, often resembling soft, unlithified gouge clay.
    • Pseudotachylyte: Glassy, friction-melted rock formed during seismic slip.
  • Middle to Lower Crust ($>10 - 15\text{ km}$, $T > 300^\circ\text{C}$): Ductile Regime (Plastic Flow): Crystal-plasticity, dislocation creep, and dynamic recrystallization dominate without loss of macroscopic continuity. Rocks deform without brittle fracturing, forming strongly foliated and lineated mylonites and ultramylonites.

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 17)
17. Rock flour or gouge is closely linked to :
(A) Ductile deformation
(B) Brittle deformation  ✔
(C) Flow of incompetent beds
(D) Collapse structures
✔
Correct Answer: (B) Brittle deformation
In structural geology, fault mechanics, and rock physics (K.M. Bangar, p. 215; Marland P. Billings, p. 150; Richard H. Sibson, 1977; Davis & Reynolds, 1996), fault gouge and rock flour are fundamentally the mechanical products of Brittle deformation in the upper crust.
(A) Ductile deformation Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Ductile deformation operates at deeper crustal levels ($T > 300^\circ\text{C}$) via crystal-plastic mechanisms (dislocation creep, grain-boundary sliding, dynamic recrystallization) without loss of material continuity. Ductile shear zones produce mylonites with ribbon quartz and porphyroclasts, never uncohesive crushed gouge or rock flour.
(B) Brittle deformation Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: Rock flour and fault gouge are diagnostic cataclastic products of brittle deformation. Under shallow, high-frictional stress conditions, rock undergoes mechanical microfracturing, grain comminution, and grinding along fault planes, pulverizing solid rock into a fine, unlithified powder.
(C) Flow of incompetent beds Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: The flow of incompetent beds (such as ductile squeezing of shale or salt between competent sandstone layers during folding) is a classic manifestation of ductile plastic flow, producing thickened fold hinges (similar folds) rather than brittle fault gouge.
(D) Collapse structures Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Collapse structures refer to sinkholes, caldera drop blocks, or cave roof collapses. While localized impact breccia occurs, the term "fault gouge / rock flour" is specifically linked to tectonic brittle fault shear planes.

Hydrogeological & Seismic Role of Fault Gouge

Fault gouge plays a pivotal role in earthquake physics and petroleum reservoir compartmentalization:

  • Fault Zone Hydrogeology (Barrier vs. Conduit): Freshly crushed, clay-rich fault gouge experiences extreme grain-size reduction, causing its permeability to plummet by $4 - 6$ orders of magnitude ($K < 10^{-18} - 10^{-20}\text{ m}^2$). Consequently, a gouge-lined fault core acts as an impermeable sealing barrier to fluid flow across the fault, trapping hydrocarbons or compartmentalizing groundwater aquifers, while the surrounding fractured damage zone acts as a high-permeability conduit parallel to the fault.
  • Frictional Stability (Rate-and-State Friction): The velocity-dependence of friction in fault gouge governs earthquake nucleation. Velocity-weakening gouge ($a - b < 0$) promotes unstable stick-slip seismic ruptures (earthquakes), whereas velocity-strengthening gouge ($a - b > 0$, e.g., montmorillonite/talc clays) promotes stable, aseismic fault creep.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What is "Pseudotachylyte", and under what specific deformation conditions does it form?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A dark, glassy to microcrystalline rock formed by extreme frictional melting of rock along a fault plane during rapid seismic earthquake slip ($v pprox 1 ext{ m/s}$).
    Explanation: Under seismic slip rates, frictional heat generation along brittle fault planes exceeds heat conduction, melting wall rocks at $T > 1000^\circ ext{C}$; rapid quenching produces glassy pseudotachylyte (the "fossil earthquake").
  • Practice Q2: Distinguish between "Cataclasite" and "Mylonite" in thin-section petrography.
    ✔ Correct Answer: Cataclasites have non-foliated random fabrics formed by brittle microcracking and grain crushing; Mylonites have strong planar foliation formed by crystal-plastic flow and dynamic recrystallization.
    Explanation: Cataclasites show shattered, angular grains with microfractures; mylonites show ductile ribbon quartz, core-and-mantle structures, and subgrain rotation.
  • Practice Q3: At what approximate temperature and crustal depth does quartz transition from brittle cataclasis to ductile crystal-plastic deformation?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Approximately $300^\circ ext{C}$ at a depth of $10 ext{ to } 15 ext{ kilometers}$ (in typical continental crust).
    Explanation: Below $300^\circ ext{C}$, quartz fractures brittly; above $300^\circ ext{C}$, dislocation glide and climb become active, allowing quartz to deform plastically. (Feldspar requires $T > 450^\circ - 500^\circ ext{C}$).
  • Practice Q4: What is the "Shale Gouge Ratio" (SGR) used in the petroleum industry?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The percentage of clay/shale smeared into the fault zone from sliced wall-rock stratigraphy, used to predict fault seal capacity against hydrocarbon leakage.
    Explanation: An $SGR > 20 - 30\%$ typically indicates that the fault gouge will form a continuous, impermeable capillary membrane seal holding back substantial oil or gas columns.
  • Practice Q5: What are "slickenlines" and "slickensides" on a fault surface?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Slickensides are polished fault planes; slickenlines are linear grooves and striations carved on the fault surface indicating the direction of slip.
    Explanation: Asperity ploughing during brittle shear polishes the slip surface, and mineral fiber steps (calcite/quartz) provide the absolute sense of movement (hanging-wall direction).
  • Practice Q6: In glacial geology, what is "glacial rock flour", and how does it relate mechanically to fault gouge?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Extremely fine silt- and clay-sized particles pulverized by the mechanical abrasion of bedrock beneath moving ice, identical in brittle cataclastic origin to fault rock flour.
    Explanation: Both form by mechanical grinding and crushing of rock fragments under high frictional normal stress, generating light-scattering rock flour that gives glacial meltwater lakes their turquoise/milky color.
  • Practice Q7: In geotechnical tunneling, why does intersecting an active fault gouge zone present a catastrophic engineering hazard?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Fault gouge has near-zero cohesion when wet, behaves as "running ground" or swelling clay, and can trigger massive water and mud inrushes into the tunnel.
    Explanation: Saturated gouge loses all shear strength under decompression, flowing like liquid mud into the tunnel excavation.
  • Practice Q8: What is "core-and-mantle structure" in shear zone petrography?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A relict porphyroclast core surrounded by a mantle of dynamically recrystallized fine subgrains, diagnostic of transitional ductile shear.
    Explanation: Formed during subgrain rotation recrystallization (SGR) at the brittle-ductile transition zone.
