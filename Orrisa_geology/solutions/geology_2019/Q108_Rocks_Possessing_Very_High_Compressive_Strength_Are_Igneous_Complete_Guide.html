1. Theoretical Foundation: Micro-Structural Controls on Compressive Strength

The compressive strength of any rock mass is fundamentally determined by three primary petrographic parameters: (1) interlocking crystalline fabric, (2) mineral hardness and bonding, and (3) void volume (porosity and microcracks). On this basis, Igneous Rocks possess the highest compressive strength among all major rock classes:

"Among all geological formations, unweathered igneous rocks possess very high compressive strength. Rocks like basalt, dolerite, granite, and diorite have strengths between 1500 and 3000 kg/cm² (150 to 300 MPa). This massive strength stems from their completely crystalline, interlocking fabric of hard silicates (quartz, feldspar, pyroxene) that grew together during solidification from magma, leaving virtually no pore voids."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Properties of Building Stones, pp. 288–289)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 108
Which rocks possess very high compressive strength ?
A Igneous
B Sedimentary
C Metamorphic
D Both sedimentary and metamorphic
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION A (Igneous)
OPTION A • CORRECT
Igneous

Fresh, unweathered igneous rocks (basalt, granite, dolerite, diorite) possess very high compressive strength (routinely exceeding 150 to 300 MPa). Their direct crystallization from silicate melts produces a tightly interlocking mosaic of hard minerals with virtually zero primary porosity ($< 1\%$).

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Sedimentary

Sedimentary rocks (sandstone, shale, limestone) possess low to moderate compressive strength (10 to 80 MPa) because they consist of clastic grains held together by weak cements with high porosity (10% to 30%).

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Metamorphic

While massive non-foliated metamorphic rocks like quartzite have high strength, the vast majority of metamorphic rocks are foliated (slates, phyllites, schists) and suffer from pronounced directional planes of weakness that shear easily under stress.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Both sedimentary and metamorphic

Sedimentary rocks have the lowest compressive strengths among intact rocks; grouping them with high strength is completely inaccurate.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. Why is fresh Deccan Trap basalt widely regarded as the finest construction aggregate in Western and Central India?
Answer: It possesses colossal crushing strength (>250 MPa), high toughness, low water absorption (<0.5%), and excellent bitumen affinity.
Basalt's fine-grained, interlocking ophitic and intersertal fabric gives it exceptional resistance to traffic abrasion and impact.
2. Which sedimentary rock can occasionally reach high compressive strength comparable to granites?
Answer: Pure orthoquartzite (silica-cemented quartzarenite)
When silica completely fills all pore spaces as syntaxial quartz overgrowths, sandstone converts into a tough, impermeable orthoquartzite.
3. What is the effect of chemical weathering on the compressive strength of granitic rocks?
Answer: Weathering can reduce compressive strength by 80% to 95% (from 200 MPa down to <10 MPa in saprolite).
Hydrolysis alters hard interlocking feldspars into soft, porous kaolinite clay, destroying rock integrity.
4. What is the Deere-Miller Engineering Classification based on?
Answer: Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) and the Modulus Ratio ($E_t / \sigma_c$).
Deere and Miller (1966) plot rock strength classes (A to E) against modulus ratios (High, Medium, Low) for structural rock classification.
5. Why are foliated metamorphic rocks (like mica schists) dangerous as dam abutments despite being crystalline?
Answer: Because mica alignment creates continuous planes of weakness with very low shear strength along foliation.
Water lubrication along schistosity can trigger catastrophic foundation sliding under reservoir thrust.
6. Which volcanic igneous rock possesses LOW compressive strength due to high vesicular porosity?
Answer: Pumice and Scoria (UCS typically < 10-20 MPa).
Extreme trapped gas bubble porosity (>60-80%) makes pumice so weak and light that it floats on water.
7. How does grain size affect the compressive strength within the igneous gabbro-dolerite-basalt family?
Answer: Fine-grained basalt and dolerite are stronger than coarse-grained gabbro.
The Hall-Petch effect in rocks: smaller grain size increases grain boundary area, impeding microcrack propagation and boosting strength.
8. What is the crushing strength threshold below which a rock is classified as 'weak' in engineering practice?
Answer: Less than 25 MPa (ISRM / British Standard)
Rocks with UCS < 25 MPa can be gouged with a geological pick and represent weak foundation conditions.
