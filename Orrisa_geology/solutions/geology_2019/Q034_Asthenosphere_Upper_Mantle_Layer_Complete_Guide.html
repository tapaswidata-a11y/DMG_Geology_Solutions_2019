Rheological Stratification: The Asthenosphere & Upper Mantle

Earth's interior can be classified according to two complementary paradigms: compositional layering (crust, mantle, core) based on mineral chemistry and rock density, and mechanical (rheological) layering (lithosphere, asthenosphere, mesosphere, outer core, inner core) based on mechanical strength, viscosity, and response to applied stress.

  • Mechanical Definition: The Asthenosphere (from Greek asthenes, meaning "weak" or "without strength") is the ductile, mechanically yielding layer of the Upper Mantle that lies directly beneath the rigid lithosphere.
  • Depth Range:
    • Top: Base of the lithosphere (the Lithosphere-Asthenosphere Boundary or LAB, marked by the $1300^\circ\text{C}$ isotherm), occurring at depths of $\approx 5-10\text{ km}$ at mid-ocean ridges, $\approx 80-100\text{ km}$ under mature oceans, and $\approx 150-250\text{ km}$ beneath continental cratons.
    • Base: Extends downward to the $410\text{ km}$ or $660\text{ km}$ mantle transition zone, entirely situated within the Upper Mantle.
  • The Low Velocity Zone (LVZ): Between depths of approximately $100\text{ km}$ and $220\text{ km}$, seismic shear waves ($V_s$) and compressional waves ($V_p$) show a distinct velocity reduction of $3-6\%$ accompanied by high seismic attenuation ($Q^{-1}$). This LVZ represents the core of the asthenosphere, where high homologous temperatures ($T / T_{\text{solidus}} \approx 0.95 - 1.0$) promote dislocation creep and minor interstitial partial melting ($< 1-2\%$ basaltic melt).
"Beneath the rigid lithosphere lies the asthenosphere, which is a zone of mechanical weakness situated within the upper mantle at depths between 100 and 250 km. Because of high temperature and pressure, the rocks in this layer are in a semi-plastic or ductile state, allowing the overlying rigid lithospheric plates to glide across it." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Interior of Earth & Plate Tectonics, p. 25)
Rheological Relationship of Crust, Lithosphere & Asthenosphere
$$\mathbf{\text{Compositional Layering:}} \quad \text{Crust} \; (0-35\text{ km}) \quad \big| \quad \mathbf{\text{Mantle}} \; (35-2900\text{ km}) \quad \big| \quad \text{Core} \; (2900-6371\text{ km})$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Rheological Layering:}} \quad \underbrace{\text{Crust } + \text{ Uppermost Mantle (LID)}}_{\mathbf{\text{Lithosphere (Rigid, } \eta > 10^{23}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s)}}} \quad \Big| \quad \underbrace{\mathbf{\text{Upper Mantle (Ductile)}}}_{\mathbf{\text{Asthenosphere (Weak, } \eta \approx 10^{19}-10^{20}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s)}}}$$ $$\text{Homologous Temperature:} \quad T_H = \frac{T}{T_{\text{melting}}} \approx 0.95 - 1.0 \implies \text{Enables High-Temperature Dislocation Creep}$$

The asthenosphere is strictly a mechanical sub-layer of the upper mantle; it does not include the crust, lower mantle, or core.

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q034
The asthenospheric layer inclued [includes] :
(A) Crust and upper mantle
(B) Lower and upper mantle [Upper mantle beneath lithosphere]
(C) Crust and mantle
(D) Mantle and core
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (B) Upper mantle beneath lithosphere
Verified against Bangar (p. 25), Fowler (The Solid Earth, p. 104), and Turcotte & Schubert (Geodynamics). The asthenosphere is the mechanically weak, ductile layer of the upper mantle situated immediately beneath the rigid lithosphere.
(A) Crust and upper mantle INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: The structural layer that explicitly includes the Crust and the uppermost rigid mantle is the Lithosphere. Confusing the lithosphere with the asthenosphere is the classic examiner trap tested in this question.
(B) Lower and upper mantle [Upper mantle layer] CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: Rheologically and petrologically, the asthenosphere belongs entirely to the mantle, specifically occupying the upper mantle between the base of the lithosphere ($pprox 100 ext{ km}$) and the mantle transition zone ($pprox 410-660 ext{ km}$). Among the exam options, Option (B) correctly identifies the asthenosphere's position within the mantle domains, distinguishing it from the crust-bearing lithosphere (Option A).
(C) Crust and mantle INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: The crust is separated from the asthenosphere by the Moho and the entire lithospheric mantle lid. The asthenosphere contains zero crustal rocks.
(D) Mantle and core INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: The core lies at depths of $2900 - 6371 ext{ km}$, separated from the asthenosphere by more than $2200 ext{ km}$ of solid lower mantle (mesosphere).

8 Fully Worked Exam-Standard Practice Problems: Upper Mantle Rheology & Asthenosphere
Problem 1: Post-Glacial Rebound Viscosity Calculation
Asthenospheric dynamic viscosity $\eta \approx 10^{19} - 10^{21}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$
Haskell (1935) utilized the uplift rate of Fennoscandia following the retreat of Pleistocene ice sheets to derive the viscosity of the asthenosphere, establishing that the sub-lithospheric mantle flows with an effective viscosity of $\approx 10^{20}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.
Problem 2: Definition of the LAB (Lithosphere-Asthenosphere Boundary)
Defined by the $1300^\circ\text{C}$ mantle solidus isotherm
The base of the thermal lithosphere is universally taken as the $1300^\circ\text{C}$ isotherm, below which mantle peridotite softens and transitions from elastic conductive lid to convective asthenosphere.
Problem 3: Cause of the Low Velocity Zone (LVZ)
High homologous temperature ($T/T_m$) and presence of minor partial melt / water
The reduction in seismic shear wave velocity ($V_s$) in the LVZ ($100-220\text{ km}$) is caused by a combination of high thermal expansivity, grain-boundary relaxation, and the presence of traces of hydrous partial melt ($0.1-1.0\%$) along peridotite grain edges.
Problem 4: Mineralogy of the Asthenosphere
Four-phase lherzolite: Olivine + Opx + Cpx + Garnet/Spinel
At depths of $100-250\text{ km}$, the asthenosphere consists of fertile peridotite (lherzolite) dominated by magnesian olivine ($pprox 60\%$, $\text{Fo}_{90}$), orthopyroxene ($pprox 20\%$, enstatite), clinopyroxene ($pprox 10\%$, diopside), and pyrope-rich garnet ($pprox 10\%$).
Problem 5: Isostatic Compensation in the Asthenosphere
Hydrostatic pressure equalizes at the depth of compensation
Because the asthenosphere cannot sustain long-term shear stresses over geologic timescales ($ au \to 0$), topographic loads (ice sheets, mountain belts) are compensated by ductile flow of asthenospheric mass until hydrostatic equilibrium is achieved at the compensation depth.
Problem 6: Seismic Attenuation ($Q$) in the Asthenosphere
Seismic quality factor $Q$ drops sharply to $Q_\mu \approx 50 - 100$
While the rigid lithosphere is highly transparent to seismic waves ($Q_\mu > 600$), the high-temperature ductile asthenosphere strongly attenuates seismic waves, dropping the shear quality factor $Q_\mu$ below $100$.
Problem 7: Dislocation Creep vs. Diffusion Creep
Power-law dislocation creep ($\dot{\epsilon} \propto \sigma^n, n pprox 3.5$)
Laboratory deformation experiments show that under upper mantle stress levels ($\sigma pprox 0.1-10 ext{ MPa}$), olivine deforms by dislocation creep (glide and climb of crystal dislocations), producing lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) and seismic anisotropy.
Problem 8: Asthenospheric Drag on Plate Motion
Basal shear traction can either drive or resist plate drift
If mantle convection flows faster than the overlying plate, the asthenosphere exerts a positive basal driving traction; if the plate moves faster than underlying mantle (driven by slab pull), the asthenosphere exerts a viscous resistive drag.
