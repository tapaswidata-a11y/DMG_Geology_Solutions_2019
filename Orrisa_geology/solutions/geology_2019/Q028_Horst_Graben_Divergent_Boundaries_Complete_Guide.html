Extensional Geodynamics: Horst, Graben & Divergent Plate Margins

In global geotectonics and structural geology, extensional stress regimes are characterized by a stress state where the maximum principal stress is vertical ($\sigma_1 = \sigma_v = \rho g z$), while the minimum principal stress is horizontal ($\sigma_3 = \sigma_h$). Under Andersonian faulting theory, this stress configuration exclusively triggers conjugate normal faulting dipping at $\approx 60^\circ$. The pairing and spatial organization of these conjugate normal faults produces Horst and Graben structures.

  • Graben (Rift Valley): A central crustal block that is downthrown relative to the flanking blocks along two parallel, inward-dipping normal faults. Grabens serve as major sedimentary basins (e.g., East African Rift, Rhine Graben, Narmada-Tapi Graben).
  • Horst: An upstanding crustal block that remains relatively elevated (upthrown) between two outward-dipping normal faults. Horsts typically form linear fault-block mountain ranges.
  • Tectonic Environment: Because horsts and grabens require horizontal lithospheric extension ($\Delta L > 0$), they are fundamentally confined to divergent plate boundaries (continental rifts, nascent oceanic basins, and mid-ocean ridge spreading axes) and back-arc extensional basins.
"When two normal faults dip towards each other and the central block between them is downthrown, the resulting trench-like structure is called a graben or rift valley. When two normal faults dip away from each other and the central block is left standing higher than the sides, the structure is called a horst. These extensional structures are typical of divergent boundaries where the earth's crust is being pulled apart." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Faults: Graben and Horst, p. 119) & Haakon Fossen, Structural Geology (p. 172)
Andersonian Mechanics of Extensional Tectonics
$$\sigma_1 = \sigma_v \quad (\text{Vertical Lithostatic Load}); \quad \sigma_3 = \sigma_h \quad (\text{Horizontal Extension})$$ $$\text{Normal Fault Dip Angle } (\theta) = 45^\circ + \frac{\phi}{2} \approx 60^\circ \quad (\text{where } \phi \approx 30^\circ \text{ is internal friction angle})$$ $$\text{Crustal Extension Ratio } (\beta) = \frac{L_{\text{final}}}{L_{\text{initial}}} = \frac{1}{\cos \theta} > 1.0$$

As the crust stretches, conjugate normal faults tilt and rotate (domino / rollover geometry), dropping graben floors and exhuming horst basements.

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q028
Horst and Graben structures are mainly confined to:
(A) Transform boundaries
(B) Subduction zones
(C) Collision zones
(D) Divergent boundaries
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (D) Divergent boundaries
Verified against Bangar (p. 119), Fossen (Structural Geology, p. 172), and Van der Pluijm & Marshak (Earth Structure, p. 430). Horst and graben structures are formed by conjugate normal faulting under extensional stress regimes, characteristically developed along divergent plate margins.
(A) Transform boundaries INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Transform plate boundaries are conservative plate boundaries dominated by horizontal strike-slip displacement (e.g., San Andreas Fault). While localized transtensional pull-apart basins (rhomb grabens) can occur along releasing bends, the regional, pervasive horst-and-graben terranes are not characteristic of transform boundaries.
(B) Subduction zones INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Subduction zones are convergent margins characterized by regional horizontal compression ($\sigma_1$ horizontal), subduction megathrusts, deep oceanic trenches, and accretionary wedges dominated by imbricate reverse and thrust faulting, directly opposing the extensional mechanics of horsts and grabens.
(C) Collision zones INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Continental collision zones (e.g., Indo-Eurasian collision in the Himalayas) represent the most intense compressional regimes on Earth, generating monumental crustal thickening, regional fold-and-thrust belts, and low-angle thrust nappes (such as the Main Central Thrust and Main Boundary Thrust), rather than horst-graben networks.
(D) Divergent boundaries CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: Divergent boundaries are defined by horizontal tensional stress and crustal pulling-apart ($\sigma_3$ horizontal). Under these conditions, the crust thins and breaks along conjugate normal faults dipping at $\approx 60^\circ$. The down-faulted troughs form grabens (axial rift valleys) and the intervening raised shoulders form horsts. Global examples include the Mid-Atlantic Ridge axial valley, the East African Rift System, and the Basin and Range province.

8 Fully Worked Exam-Standard Practice Problems: Extensional Tectonics & Normal Faulting
Problem 1: Calculation of Horizontal Crustal Extension (Heave)
Total Heave $H = 2.0\text{ km}$
A symmetrical graben is bounded by two normal faults dipping at $60^\circ$. Each fault exhibits a dip slip of $S_d = 2.0\text{ km}$. Calculate the total horizontal extension (heave) accommodated across the graben.
$$H_{\text{single}} = S_d \cdot \cos \delta = 2.0 \cdot \cos(60^\circ) = 2.0 \cdot 0.5 = 1.0\text{ km}$$ $$H_{\text{total}} = 2 \times 1.0\text{ km} = 2.0\text{ km}$$
Problem 2: Vertical Throw Calculation
Vertical Throw $T = 1.732\text{ km}$
For the normal fault in Problem 1 ($S_d = 2.0\text{ km}, \delta = 60^\circ$), calculate the vertical subsidence (throw) of the graben floor.
$$T = S_d \cdot \sin \delta = 2.0 \cdot \sin(60^\circ) = 2.0 \cdot 0.8660 = 1.732\text{ km}$$
Problem 3: The Satpura Range as a Structural Horst
Horst block between Narmada and Tapi grabens
In central Indian tectonics, the Satpura Mountain Range acts as an upthrown horst block bounded to the north by the Narmada Graben fault system and to the south by the Tapi Graben fault system.
Problem 4: McKenzie Stretching Factor ($\beta$)
$\beta = 1.25$ indicates $25\%$ lithospheric extension
The McKenzie pure shear model defines $\beta = t_0 / t_f$, where $t_0$ is initial crustal thickness ($40\text{ km}$) and $t_f$ is stretched crustal thickness ($32\text{ km}$). $\beta = 40/32 = 1.25$, driving initial syn-rift fault subsidence followed by post-rift thermal subsidence.
Problem 5: Listric Normal Faulting & Rollover Anticlines
Curvature of fault at depth forces rollover hanging-wall collapse
A normal fault that flattens with depth into a horizontal decollement is termed listric. Slip along a curved listric fault causes the hanging wall to collapse into the fault, creating an anticlinal trap termed a rollover anticline.
Problem 6: Bouguer Gravity Anomaly Across a Graben
Prominent negative Bouguer gravity anomaly
Because grabens trap thick sequences of low-density syn-rift sedimentary rocks ($\rho \approx 2.2-2.4\text{ g/cm}^3$) relative to the surrounding crystalline basement ($\rho \approx 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$), grabens produce strong negative Bouguer gravity anomalies.
Problem 7: Andersonian Principal Stress Orientation for Rifting
$\sigma_1$ vertical, $\sigma_2$ along rift strike, $\sigma_3$ perpendicular to rift
In continental rifting, the maximum principal stress is the overburden weight ($\sigma_1$ vertical), the intermediate stress $\sigma_2$ parallels the graben axis, and the minimum stress $\sigma_3$ is horizontal and orthogonal to the rift axis.
Problem 8: Thermal Subsidence Phase in Divergent Basins
Exponential cooling and contraction of upwelled asthenosphere
Following active rifting (graben formation), lithospheric stretching subsides and the upwelled asthenospheric mantle cools conductively ($S \propto \sqrt{t}$), causing long-term regional thermal subsidence that forms broad sag basins over older grabens.
