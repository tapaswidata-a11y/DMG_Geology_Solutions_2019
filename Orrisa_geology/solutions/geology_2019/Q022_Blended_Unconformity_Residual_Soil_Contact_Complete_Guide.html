Stratigraphic Anatomy of Unconformities & Residual Contacts

An unconformity represents a substantial hiatus or gap in the geologic rock record, marking a prolonged period of non-deposition, subaerial exposure, and weathering/erosion before younger sedimentary strata are deposited. While most unconformities are marked by a sharp, knife-edge erosional contact, a special and critically diagnostic category occurs when ancient in-situ subaerial weathering produced a deep residual soil mantle that was preserved beneath the transgressing younger strata—termed a blended unconformity.

"Where younger sedimentary rocks are deposited directly over an ancient land surface consisting of thick residual soil that grades imperceptibly downwards into the underlying crystalline bedrock, the boundary between the two formations is transitional and fuzzy rather than a sharp plane. Such an unconformity is called a blended unconformity. It indicates that the old weathered mantle was preserved without being stripped away by erosion before the deposition of younger sediments." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Chapter 8: Unconformities, p. 225)

In classical stratigraphic taxonomy (Dunbar & Rodgers, 1957; Billings, 1972; Boggs, 2011), unconformities are classified into four primary geometrical and genetic categories:

  • Blended Unconformity: A contact where younger sedimentary strata rest upon an ancient weathered paleosol/saprolite profile that grades transitionally into the underlying parent rock. The contact is blurred, transitional, and mechanically interlocking.
  • Nonconformity: An unconformity where layered sedimentary or volcanic rocks rest upon an eroded, unweathered surface of older crystalline plutonic (granitic) or metamorphic (schist, gneiss) basement rocks, separated by a sharp contact.
  • Angular Unconformity: An unconformity where younger, flatter-lying strata overlie older tilted, folded, or deformed strata with a discordant angular relationship (e.g., Hutton's classic unconformity at Siccar Point).
  • Disconformity: An unconformity between parallel sedimentary layers where the contact is a sharp, irregular erosional surface, but bedding planes above and below are parallel.

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 22)
22. A contact of younger sediments deposited over a thick residual soil that grades into underlying bedrock is called :
(A) Discontinuity
(B) Non-conformity
(C) Blended unconformity  ✔
(D) Angular unconformity
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Blended unconformity
In stratigraphy and historical geology (K.M. Bangar, p. 225; Marland P. Billings, p. 230; Dunbar & Rodgers, 1957; Sam Boggs Jr., p. 415), an unconformable boundary where younger sediments overlie an in-situ residual soil that grades into underlying bedrock is specifically termed a Blended unconformity.
(A) Discontinuity Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: "Discontinuity" is a general geotechnical term describing any physical break or fracture in a rock mass (such as joints, faults, or bedding planes). It is not the formal stratigraphic designation for a residual soil unconformity.
(B) Non-conformity Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A nonconformity is an unconformity where bedded sedimentary rocks lie upon clean, unweathered, deeply eroded plutonic igneous rocks (granite) or metamorphic rocks (gneiss) across a sharp, distinct erosional surface, devoid of a transitional residual soil zone.
(C) Blended unconformity Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: When a deep ancient regolith/paleosol mantle is preserved during marine or fluvial transgression, the younger basal sediments mix with the loose topsoil, creating a gradational contact that blends downward into saprolite and solid bedrock. This is the exact, verbatim textbook definition of a blended unconformity.
(D) Angular unconformity Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: An angular unconformity is defined by structural angular discordance, where younger strata overlie older folded or tilted strata at an angle, marked by a sharp truncational plane.

Paleopedology & Saprolite Profiles in the Stratigraphic Record

Blended unconformities are of paramount importance in paleoclimatology and mineral exploration:

  • Paleosol Recognition: The transitional zone preserves fossil soil horizons (paleosols), featuring root traces, pedogenic caliche nodules, and illuvial clay cutans that preserve ancient atmospheric conditions ($p ext{CO}_2$, precipitation).
  • Paleoplacer Deposits: Chemical leaching of the bedrock during residual weathering often leaves dense, resistant minerals (gold, cassiterite, diamonds) concentrated directly within the blended basal sediment wedge.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What geochemical profile in a blended unconformity confirms that the transitional zone represents an in-situ paleosol rather than an unweathered sedimentary conglomerate?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Downward increasing Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA) toward the top surface, along with depletion of mobile cations ($Ca^{2+}, Na^+, K^+$) and enrichment in residual $Al_2O_3$ and $Fe_2O_3$.
    Explanation: In-situ chemical leaching strips mobile bases from feldspars, leaving kaolinite/bauxite near the surface grading into unweathered granite at depth.
  • Practice Q2: What is a "saprolite"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Chemically weathered bedrock that retains the original crystalline rock textures, structures, and relict fabric, but has lost coherence and density.
    Explanation: Saprolite forms the $C$-horizon of deep weathering profiles, often underlying the basal sediments of a blended unconformity.
  • Practice Q3: How does a "buttress unconformity" (onlap unconformity) develop?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Younger sedimentary beds onlap and terminate progressively against steep, rugged paleotopographic paleocliffs of older rock.
    Explanation: Instead of horizontal planar layering, strata terminate at high angles against the buried mountain flank.
  • Practice Q4: In civil engineering and tunneling, why do blended unconformities present severe excavation hazards?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Unpredictable transitions from competent fresh bedrock to completely decomposed, water-saturated saprolite soil cause sudden collapse and crown cave-ins.
    Explanation: Tunneling machines encountering an unexpected deep weathering pocket experience sudden loss of face support.
  • Practice Q5: What is the difference between a Disconformity and a Paraconformity?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A disconformity shows visible physical erosional relief (valleys/channels); a paraconformity is a flat bedding plane with no visible physical erosion, identified solely by fossil gaps.
    Explanation: Paraconformities represent submarine hiatal surfaces where sedimentation ceased without subaerial stream gouging.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "basal conglomerate", and where is it found relative to an unconformity?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A conglomerate layer situated at the very base of the younger sedimentary sequence directly above an unconformity, composed of pebbles derived from the underlying older rocks.
    Explanation: Transgressing waves rework the weathered surface, concentrating resistant quartz and quartzite pebbles into a basal conglomerate.
  • Practice Q7: Can an unconformity act as a petroleum trap?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Yes; permeable sandstone beds truncated beneath an impermeable shale unconformity form world-class "truncation traps" (e.g., Prudhoe Bay, Alaska).
    Explanation: The overlying impermeable transgressive shale seals the porous, dipping reservoir beds below.
  • Practice Q8: What is Hutton's classic "Great Unconformity" at Siccar Point, Scotland?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A classic angular unconformity where gently dipping Devonian Old Red Sandstone overlies near-vertical, folded Silurian greywackes.
    Explanation: James Hutton used this in 1788 to demonstrate deep geological time ("no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end").
