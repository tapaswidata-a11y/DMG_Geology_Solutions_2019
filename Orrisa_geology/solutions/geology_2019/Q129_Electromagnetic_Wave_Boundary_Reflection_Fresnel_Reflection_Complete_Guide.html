1. Theoretical Foundation: Fresnel Reflection and Boundary Electrodynamics

When an electromagnetic (EM) plane wave propagating through a medium of refractive index $n_1$ impinges upon a smooth, planar boundary separating it from a second dielectric medium of refractive index $n_2$, classical electrodynamics (governed by Maxwell's boundary equations) dictates that the incident wave divides into two components:

  • A reflected wave that turns back into the original incident medium at angle $\theta_r = \theta_i$ (Law of Reflection).
  • A transmitted (refracted) wave that penetrates and propagates through the second medium at refracted angle $\theta_t$ governed by Snell's Law ($n_1 \sin \theta_i = n_2 \sin \theta_t$).

The mathematical and physical description of how the incident energy is partitioned between reflection and transmission as a function of incidence angle ($\theta_i$), refractive indices ($n_1, n_2$), and polarization state was formulated by Augustin-Jean Fresnel in 1823, and is universally designated as Fresnel Reflection (and the Fresnel Equations):

$$\text{Perpendicular (s-polarized / TE): } r_s = \frac{n_1 \cos\theta_i - n_2 \cos\theta_t}{n_1 \cos\theta_i + n_2 \cos\theta_t} = -\frac{\sin(\theta_i - \theta_t)}{\sin(\theta_i + \theta_t)}$$ $$\text{Parallel (p-polarized / TM): } r_p = \frac{n_2 \cos\theta_i - n_1 \cos\theta_t}{n_2 \cos\theta_i + n_1 \cos\theta_t} = \frac{\tan(\theta_i - \theta_t)}{\tan(\theta_i + \theta_t)}$$

The fraction of reflected power (reflectance, $R$) is given by $R_s = |r_s|^2$ and $R_p = |r_p|^2$. At normal incidence ($\theta_i = 0^\circ$):

$$R = \left(\frac{n_2 - n_1}{n_2 + n_1}\right)^2$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 129
“An Electromagnetic wave falls on to a boundary between two less homogeneous media with different refractive indices and a part of the wave is reflected back to the incident medium and the rest is transmitted on to the second media.” This phenomenon is known as :
A Fresnel reflection
B Fresnel refraction
C Snell’s law
D Spectral reflectance
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION A (Fresnel reflection)
OPTION A • CORRECT
Fresnel reflection

The stated definition is the verbatim textbook definition of Fresnel reflection (Hecht; Born & Wolf; Lillesand & Kiefer). It describes the fundamental electromagnetic boundary-value problem where impedance mismatch between two media of differing refractive indices ($n_1 \ne n_2$) forces a portion of the incident wave energy to be reflected back into the incident medium while transmitting the remainder.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Fresnel refraction

"Fresnel refraction" is an informal/non-standard phrase. The transmitted component is governed by Fresnel transmission coefficients ($t_s, t_p$). Because the question emphasizes the reflected wave component returning to the incident medium, the universal scientific term for this boundary phenomenon is Fresnel reflection.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Snell’s law

Snell's Law ($n_1 \sin\theta_i = n_2 \sin\theta_t$) specifies only the geometric direction of the refracted ray. It does not predict or compute how much energy is reflected back versus transmitted, nor does it account for wave polarization.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Spectral reflectance

Spectral reflectance ($\rho_\lambda$) is an empirical remote sensing measure describing the percentage of incident light reflected by a natural target (like green leaves, water, or granite) across individual wavelength bands. It is not the foundational wave-optics boundary principle defined in the question.

1. What is the normal-incidence Fresnel reflectance ($R$) at the boundary between air ($n_1 = 1.0$) and pure water ($n_2 = 1.333$)?
Answer: Approximately $0.0204$ (or $2.04\%$).
$$R = \left(\frac{1.333 - 1.0}{1.333 + 1.0}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{0.333}{2.333}\right)^2 \approx (0.1427)^2 \approx 0.0204$$. Roughly $98\%$ of the light is transmitted into the water column.
2. What is Brewster's angle ($\theta_B$) for light incident from air ($n_1 = 1.0$) onto a flat water surface ($n_2 = 1.333$)?
Answer: Approximately $53.1^\circ$.
$$\theta_B = \arctan\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right) = \arctan(1.333) \approx 53.1^\circ$$. At this angle, p-polarized reflection drops to zero.
3. Under what optical condition does total internal reflection occur according to Fresnel formulas?
Answer: When light travels from an optically denser medium to a rarer medium ($n_1 > n_2$) at an angle of incidence exceeding the critical angle ($\theta_i \ge \theta_c = \arcsin(n_2/n_1)$).
Beyond the critical angle, the refracted wave becomes an evanescent wave decaying exponentially along the boundary, yielding $100\%$ reflection.
4. How does surface roughness modify pure specular Fresnel reflection in remote sensing?
Answer: By the Rayleigh roughness criterion ($h > \lambda / (8 \cos\theta)$), microscopic surface roughness breaks coherent specular reflection into diffuse Lambertian scattering.
Mirror-like Fresnel reflection only occurs on smooth water or polished mineral faces; natural soils exhibit diffuse scattering.
5. What causes "sun glint" in optical satellite imagery over oceans?
Answer: Direct specular Fresnel reflection of sunlight into the satellite sensor's aperture from wind-roughened ocean wave facets.
Sun glint saturates optical detectors, obscuring chlorophyll and ocean color information.
6. In radar remote sensing, how does the dielectric constant of moist soil affect Fresnel reflection?
Answer: Water has a high dielectric constant ($\epsilon_r \approx 80$) compared to dry soil ($\epsilon_r \approx 3\text{--}4$); increasing soil moisture dramatically elevates Fresnel reflectivity and radar backscatter.
This physical dependence enables spaceborne mapping of soil moisture by SAR and microwave radiometers.
7. Why is the reflected light at Brewster's angle purely linearly polarized?
Answer: Because at Brewster's angle, the reflected and transmitted rays are perpendicular ($\theta_i + \theta_t = 90^\circ$); electric dipoles oscillating parallel to the reflection plane have zero radiation along their axis, extinguishing the p-wave.
Only the s-polarized component (oscillating perpendicular to the plane of incidence) is reflected.
8. What is the complex refractive index ($\tilde{n} = n + i k$) and how does the extinction coefficient ($k$) relate to Fresnel reflection?
Answer: The imaginary part $k$ represents optical absorption; high values of $k$ (as in metals and metallic sulfides) produce very high Fresnel reflectivity even at normal incidence.
$$R = \frac{(n-1)^2 + k^2}{(n+1)^2 + k^2}$$; this explains the bright metallic luster of pyrite and galena under reflected-light ore microscopy.
