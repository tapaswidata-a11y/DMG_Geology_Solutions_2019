Kinematics & Andersonian Mechanics of Normal Faulting

In structural geology and fault mechanics, a normal fault (historically termed a gravity fault) is a dip-slip fault where the hanging wall has moved downward relative to the footwall. Normal faults are the fundamental structural hallmark of crustal extension (rifting, basin-and-range extension, and mid-ocean ridge spreading centers).

"In a dip-slip fault, the block lying above the inclined fault plane is called the hanging wall, and the block lying below it is called the footwall. When the hanging wall has moved down relative to the footwall, the fault is called a normal fault. Normal faults are produced by tensional forces in the Earth's crust that tend to stretch and pull the rocks apart." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Chapter 7: Faults, p. 209)

The mechanical physics of normal faulting is formalized by Anderson's Theory of Faulting (1905, 1951), based on the principle that the Earth's surface is a free surface that can support zero shear stress, requiring one of the three principal stress axes to be vertical:

Andersonian Stress State for Normal Faulting (Anderson, 1905)
$$\sigma_1 \text{ (Maximum Principal Compressive Stress) is VERTICAL } (\sigma_v = \rho g z)$$ $$\sigma_2 \text{ (Intermediate Principal Stress) is HORIZONTAL (Parallel to Fault Strike)}$$ $$\sigma_3 \text{ (Minimum Principal Compressive Stress) is HORIZONTAL (Extensional Direction)}$$ $$\text{Theoretical Fault Dip Angle: } \theta = 45^\circ + \frac{\phi}{2} \approx 60^\circ \quad (\text{for typical rock friction } \phi = 30^\circ)$$

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 19)
19. If the hanging wall in a fault is in downthrown side, it is called :
(A) Reverse fault
(B) Normal fault  ✔
(C) Tear fault
(D) Hinge fault
✔
Correct Answer: (B) Normal fault
In structural geology, fault mechanics, and tectonic analysis (K.M. Bangar, p. 209; Marland P. Billings, p. 142; Haakon Fossen, p. 338; E.M. Anderson, 1905), a fault where the hanging wall occupies the downthrown side is universally defined as a Normal fault.
(A) Reverse fault Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: In a reverse fault, the hanging wall has moved upward (upthrown) relative to the footwall under horizontal compressional stress ($\sigma_1$ horizontal), causing crustal shortening and duplicating stratigraphic layers in vertical boreholes.
(B) Normal fault Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: By fundamental definition, whenever the hanging wall block moves downward along the dip of the fault plane relative to the footwall, the fault is termed a normal fault. This movement accommodates crustal extension and produces a gap (omission) of strata in vertical drill holes.
(C) Tear fault Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A tear fault is a localized, near-vertical strike-slip fault that bounds or accommodates differential movement within an advancing thrust sheet or allochthonous nappe, displaying horizontal slip rather than hanging-wall downthrow.
(D) Hinge fault Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A hinge fault (or rotational fault) is a fault where displacement increases progressively away from a stationary pivot or hinge axis where displacement is zero; movement involves rotational torque rather than uniform dip-slip translation.

Structural Signatures of Normal Faulting in Boreholes & Seismic Sections

Normal faulting imparts unmistakable structural signatures across subsurface geological data:

  • Stratigraphic Omission (Missing Beds): A vertical borehole drilled through a normal fault plane encounters an abrupt stratigraphic gap—beds that should be present between the hanging wall and footwall are omitted.
  • Crustal Lengthening: Measured horizontal line length across a normal fault terrain increases by $\Delta L = \sum ext{Heave}$, directly quantifying regional tectonic extension.
  • Rollover Anticlines & Growth Strata: Listric normal faults (fault planes that curve to shallower dips at depth) force the hanging-wall block to rotate and collapse, forming a rollover anticline with growth stratigraphy that thickens toward the fault plane.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A continental rift valley is traversed by a conjugate set of 10 normal faults, each dipping at $\delta = 60^\circ$ and having an average throw $T = 150.0 ext{ m}$. Calculate: (a) the heave per fault, and (b) the total regional horizontal tectonic extension ($\Delta L$) accommodated by the fault system.
    ✔ Worked Answer: (a) $ ext{Heave} = 86.6 ext{ m}$; (b) $\Delta L = 866.0 ext{ meters}$.
    Derivation:
    (a) For each fault: $ ext{Heave } H = rac{T}{ an \delta} = rac{150.0}{ an 60^\circ} = rac{150.0}{\sqrt{3}} pprox 86.60 ext{ m}$.
    (b) Total regional crustal lengthening:
    $$\Delta L = n imes H = 10 imes 86.60 ext{ m} = 866.0 ext{ meters}$$
  • Practice Q2: What is a "Horst" and a "Graben"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A graben is a central downthrown block bounded by conjugate normal faults dipping toward each other; a horst is an uplifted block bounded by outward-dipping normal faults.
    Explanation: Classic grabens include the Rhine Graben and East African Rift; classic horsts include the Black Forest and Vosges mountains.
  • Practice Q3: Why do conjugate normal faults typically intersect at an angle of $60^\circ$ in pristine rock?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because according to Coulomb-Mohr fracture criterion with internal friction $\phi pprox 30^\circ$, failure occurs along planes inclined at $ heta = 45^\circ + \phi/2 = 60^\circ$ to $\sigma_3$.
    Explanation: The dihedral angle between conjugate fault planes bisected by $\sigma_1$ is $2(45^\circ - \phi/2) = 60^\circ$.
  • Practice Q4: In oil and gas drilling, if a well penetrates a fault and certain reservoir sandstone beds are completely missing, what type of fault was crossed?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A Normal fault.
    Explanation: Normal faults produce stratigraphic omission (gap); reverse faults produce stratigraphic repetition (the same bed is drilled twice).
  • Practice Q5: What is a "listric normal fault"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A normal fault whose fault surface is concave-upward, dipping steeply near the surface and flattening to subhorizontal at depth.
    Explanation: Listric faults sole into a subhorizontal basal detachment (décollement) in passive continental margins and extensional basins.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "detachment fault" (or core complex fault)?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A regional, low-angle ($\delta < 30^\circ$) normal fault accommodating extreme crustal extension, exhuming lower crustal metamorphic core complexes.
    Explanation: Common in the Basin and Range province (USA) and Aegean Sea, juxtaposing unmetamorphosed brittle upper-crustal rocks against ductile mylonitic gneisses.
  • Practice Q7: What is an "antithetic normal fault" vs. a "synthetic normal fault"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A synthetic fault dips in the same direction as the master fault; an antithetic fault dips in the opposite direction.
    Explanation: Antithetic faults form within the hanging-wall block to accommodate bending strain during rollover folding.
  • Practice Q8: What is "growth faulting" in deltaic environments?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A syn-sedimentary normal fault that moves concurrently with deposition, resulting in dramatically thicker sedimentary beds on the downthrown hanging wall.
    Explanation: Ubiquitous in Cenozoic delta provinces (e.g., Niger Delta, Gulf of Mexico, Krishna-Godavari Basin), forming prime structural-stratigraphic petroleum traps.
