1. Theoretical Foundation: Mechanical Meaning of Toughness & Impact Testing

In mechanical engineering and stone testing, toughness is defined as the ability of a material to absorb kinetic energy and resist fracturing, shattering, or disintegrating under sudden impact blows. While crushing strength measures resistance to slow, static loads, Impact Testing is conducted specifically to determine a stone's Toughness:

Laboratory Test Method Dynamic Stress Imposed Primary Property Measured Standard Apparatus & Reference
IMPACT TEST (Option D) Sudden dynamic impact blow (falling hammer). ★ TOUGHNESS ★ (Option D) (Resistance to shattering under repeated shock loads). Page Impact Testing Machine / Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) (IS: 2386 Part IV).
Crushing Test Slow, gradually increasing static compressive load. Compressive (Crushing) Strength Universal Testing Machine / Compression Press (IS: 1121 Part I).
Abrasion Test Frictional rubbing and abrasive grinding. Hardness & Wear Resistance Los Angeles Abrasion / Deval Attrition Machine (IS: 2386 Part IV).
Soundness Test Cyclic immersion in sodium or magnesium sulfate salt solutions. Durability (Frost & Weathering resistance) Sodium Sulfate Soundness Apparatus (IS: 2386 Part V).
"The resistance of a stone to impact or shattering under a sudden blow is known as its toughness. It is determined by the impact test using a Page Impact Machine. A steel hammer weighing 2 kg is dropped on a cylindrical stone core from successive heights increasing by 1 cm until the specimen breaks. The height of the blow in centimeters that causes failure is taken as the toughness index of the stone."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Properties of Building Stones, p. 288)
$$\text{Page Impact Toughness Test: } \text{Toughness Index } (T) \equiv \text{Drop Height of 2 kg Hammer at Fracture (in cm)}$$ $$\text{Aggregate Impact Value (AIV): } \text{AIV } (\%) = \frac{W_2 (\text{Fines passing 2.36 mm sieve})}{W_1 (\text{Total sample weight})} \times 100\%$$ $$\mathbf{Impact\ Testing\ of\ Stone} \implies \mathbf{Determines\ TOUGHNESS\ (Option\ D)}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 111
The impact testing of a stone is done to know the:
A Durability
B Porosity
C Density
D Toughness
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION D (Toughness)
OPTION D • CORRECT
Toughness

By standard civil engineering and building stone testing codes (IS: 2386 Part IV; ASTM D3; Bangar, p. 288; Mahapatra), impact testing is conducted specifically to measure the toughness (impact resistance against dynamic shattering) of stones and road aggregates.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Durability

Durability (weathering resistance) is determined by the soundness test (sodium sulfate cycles), freezing-and-thawing tests, or water absorption tests, NOT by impact testing.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Porosity

Porosity is determined through water absorption saturation methods or helium pycnometry, measuring volumetric voids rather than dynamic shock response.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Density

Density is measured by mass-to-volume displacement (pycnometer or water immersion), an intrinsic mass property unrelated to mechanical impact blows.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. In the Page Impact Test, what is the weight of the falling hammer and from what height does the test start?
Answer: The hammer weighs 2.0 kg and drops initially from a height of 1.0 cm.
The drop height increases by 1 cm after each blow (1 cm, 2 cm, 3 cm...) until the specimen fractures.
2. What toughness index value classifies a stone as 'high toughness' according to standard engineering specifications?
Answer: Greater than 19 cm ($T > 19$).
$T > 19$ is high toughness; 13 to 19 is medium toughness; $< 13$ is poor/low toughness.
3. In the Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) test (IS: 2386 Part IV), how many blows are delivered and by what hammer weight?
Answer: 15 blows by a 13.5 to 14.0 kg hammer dropping from a height of 380 mm (38 cm).
AIV is the percentage of crushed fines passing through a 2.36 mm IS sieve after 15 standard blows.
4. What is the maximum permissible Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) for aggregates used in bituminous concrete road wearing courses?
Answer: Maximum 24% (IRC guidelines specify $\le 24\%$ or $\le 30\%$ for base courses).
Lower AIV values signify higher toughness and resistance to shattering under vehicle wheel impacts.
5. Why does dolerite have higher toughness than coarse granite even though both are igneous?
Answer: Dolerite possesses an ophitic texture where flexible plagioclase laths are enclosed in pyroxene, absorbing impact energy without propagating cracks.
Granite contains large brittle quartz crystals that cleave and shatter readily under impact blows.
6. What test determines the resistance of stone to wear caused by mutual rubbing of aggregates against each other?
Answer: The Attrition Test (Deval Attrition Test)
Frictional rubbing of stones against stones is attrition; rubbing of stones against external steel balls/abrasives is abrasion.
7. What is the relationship between toughness and brittleness in rocks?
Answer: Toughness and brittleness are inverse properties; high toughness implies low brittleness.
Brittle rocks fracture with minimal strain energy absorption, whereas tough rocks deform plastically and dissipate energy before failing.
8. What is the Charpy or Izod test used for?
Answer: Measuring the notched impact energy of construction materials (steels and structural composites).
Charpy tests measure the kinetic energy absorbed by a swinging pendulum hammer breaking a notched specimen.
