1. Theoretical Foundation: Physicochemical Basis of Liquid Immiscibility

In chemical thermodynamics and igneous petrology, liquid immiscibility refers to the thermodynamic phenomenon where a single, homogeneous silicate melt cools until it encounters a two-phase liquid boundary (the solvus), causing it to split or unmix into two coexisting, distinct liquids of different composition that cannot dissolve in one another:

Magmatic System Parent Homogeneous Melt Separated Immiscible Liquid 1 Separated Immiscible Liquid 2 Geological / Ore Deposit Consequence
Silicate-Sulfide System Sulfur-saturated basic magma Silicate Melt ($SiO_2$-rich, Fe-poor) Dense Sulfide Melt ($Fe\text{-}Ni\text{-}Cu\text{-}PGE$ liquid) World-class magmatic sulfide deposits (Sudbury, Canada; Norilsk, Russia; Kambalda).
Silicate-Carbonate System $CO_2$-rich alkaline silicate melt Alkaline Silicate Melt (Nephelinite / Ijolite) Carbonate Melt ($Ca\text{-}Mg\text{-}Na$ rich, $SiO_2$-poor) Origin of Carbonatites and associated alkaline ring complexes (Oldoinyo Lengai, Amba Dongar).
Silicate-Iron Oxide System Extreme Fe-rich tholeiitic differentiate Silica-rich Felsic Melt (Granitic / Rhyolitic) Iron-rich Mafic Melt (Magnetite-Apatite liquid) Kiruna-type magnetite-apatite deposits; Skaergaard intrusion late-stage splits.
"Liquid immiscibility is the process by which a single homogeneous liquid phase splits upon cooling into two distinct liquid phases of different compositions that are mutually insoluble, just like oil and water. In silicate systems, this solvus unmixing produces conjugate liquids such as an iron-rich basic melt and a silica-rich felsic melt, or a silicate melt and a sulfide ore melt."
— Edwin Roedder, Silicate Liquid Immiscibility in Magmas (Evolution of the Igneous Rocks, 1979)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 90
Liquid imissibility generally results from :
A Mixing of two liquids of two different composition
B Mixing of two liquids of same composition
C Unmixing of two liquids of different composition
D Unmixing of two liquids of same composition
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION C (Unmixing of two liquids of different composition)
OPTION C • CORRECT
Unmixing of two liquids of different composition

By definition in physical chemistry and petrology, liquid immiscibility is the unmixing (separation or exsolution) of a parent homogeneous liquid into two coexisting liquids having different chemical compositions (e.g., a sulfide liquid vs. a silicate liquid, or a carbonate liquid vs. a silicate liquid) because they are mutually insoluble below their solvus temperature.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Mixing of two liquids of two different composition

This defines magma mixing or hybridization, which is the exact opposite of immiscibility. Mixing combines two melts into one; immiscibility unmixes one melt into two.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Mixing of two liquids of same composition

Mixing identical liquids produces the same liquid without any change, neither creating immiscibility nor generating differentiated phases.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Unmixing of two liquids of same composition

Physically impossible. A liquid cannot 'unmix' into two phases of identical composition; if the compositions were identical, there would be no chemical boundary or interface between them.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What economic ore deposits owe their origin fundamentally to sulfide liquid immiscibility?
Answer: Magmatic Nickel-Copper-PGE Sulfide Deposits (e.g., Sudbury, Norilsk-Talnakh, Bushveld, Kambalda)
When mafic magma becomes sulfur-saturated, an immiscible sulfide liquid droplets exsolve, scavenge chalcophile metals (Ni, Cu, Pt, Pd), and sink to form massive basal ore pools.
2. What phase boundary in a temperature-composition phase diagram bounds the region of two immiscible liquids?
Answer: The Solvus (Two-Liquid Dome)
Above the solvus, the liquid is completely miscible and homogeneous; below the solvus, it unmixes into conjugate liquids.
3. What petrographic evidence was found in Apollo lunar basalts proving silicate liquid immiscibility?
Answer: High-silica (potassic granite) and high-iron (pyroxferroite) glassy globules in late-stage mesostasis.
Edwin Roedder and Paul Weiblen (1970) proved immiscibility in lunar samples where residual melt split into immiscible felsic and mafic glasses.
4. How does sulfur saturation occur in a basic magma leading to sulfide liquid unmixing?
Answer: By crustal sulfur assimilation, cooling, silica contamination, or fractional crystallization.
Adding crustal sulfur or adding silica drastically lowers sulfide solubility, triggering immediate immiscible sulfide droplet exsolution.
5. What is the Partition Coefficient ($D$) of Platinum Group Elements (PGEs) between immiscible sulfide melt and silicate melt?
Answer: Extremely high ($D_{PGE} \approx 10^3\text{ to } 10^5$)
PGEs strongly partition into the sulfide melt phase, concentrating platinum and palladium by thousands of times relative to silicate magma.
6. What famous layered mafic intrusion in Greenland exhibits late-stage Fe-rich vs. Si-rich liquid immiscibility?
Answer: The Skaergaard Intrusion
Extreme fractional crystallization in the Skaergaard closed magma chamber drove the final melt into the two-liquid immiscibility field.
7. What is the difference between solid solution exsolution and liquid immiscibility?
Answer: Exsolution occurs in the solid state below the solidus; liquid immiscibility occurs entirely within the liquid melt state above the solidus.
Perthite forms by solid-state exsolution; sulfide dropplets and carbonatite melts unmix as distinct liquids above the solidus.
8. What texture is formed when immiscible droplets of sulfide or carbonate are trapped in rapidly solidified volcanic rocks?
Answer: Variolitic, globular, or ocellar texture
Immiscible liquid droplets form spherical globules (ocelli or varioles) preserving the liquid-liquid emulsion texture.
