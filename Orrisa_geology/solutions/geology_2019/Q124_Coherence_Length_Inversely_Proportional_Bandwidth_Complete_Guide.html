1. Theoretical Foundation: Temporal Coherence and Coherence Length

In electromagnetic wave theory, physical optics, and radar interferometry (InSAR), coherence measures the degree of correlation between the phase and amplitude of a wave field at different spatial points or times. Temporal coherence describes how monochromatic and phase-predictable a propagating wave remains over time.

No physical light or radar source is purely monochromatic; real waves consist of a wavepacket containing a finite spread of frequencies known as the spectral bandwidth ($\Delta \nu$ in Hertz or $\Delta \lambda$ in meters). The coherence time ($\tau_c$) is the average temporal duration over which the wave packet maintains a stable phase relationship, given by the Fourier transform uncertainty principle:

$$\tau_c \approx \frac{1}{\Delta \nu}$$

The coherence length ($L_c$) represents the propagation distance along the beam path over which the wave packet maintains a strong, predictable phase and amplitude relationship:

$$L_c = c \cdot \tau_c = \frac{c}{\Delta \nu}$$

where $c$ is the speed of light in the medium and $\Delta \nu$ is the spectral bandwidth in frequency. In terms of central wavelength ($\lambda_0$) and wavelength spread ($\Delta \lambda$):

$$\text{Since } \nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} \implies |\Delta \nu| \approx \frac{c}{\lambda_0^2} \Delta \lambda \implies L_c \approx \frac{\lambda_0^2}{\Delta \lambda}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 124
The coherence length over which there is a strong relationship between amplitudes is:
A Directly proportional to the bandwidth
B Inversely proportional to the bandwidth
C The square of the bandwidth
D The inverse of square of bandwidth
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (Inversely proportional to the bandwidth)
OPTION B • CORRECT
Inversely proportional to the bandwidth

By the fundamental Fourier uncertainty relationship between time and frequency ($\Delta t \cdot \Delta \nu \approx 1$), the coherence time is $\tau_c = 1/\Delta \nu$. Multiplying by wave propagation velocity $c$ yields $L_c = c/\Delta \nu$. As spectral bandwidth increases, phase de-correlation occurs over shorter spatial distances, making coherence length strictly inversely proportional to bandwidth.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Directly proportional to the bandwidth

This implies that broadening the frequency spectrum increases coherence length, which is physically impossible. A broader spectrum contains many interfering frequency components that rapidly destroy phase alignment.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
The square of the bandwidth

Incorrect mathematical relationship. Coherence length relates to bandwidth via an inverse first-order power law ($(\Delta \nu)^{-1}$), not a direct quadratic power law ($(\Delta \nu)^2$).

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
The inverse of square of bandwidth

Incorrect exponent. While coherence length is in the denominator ($1/\Delta \nu$), it depends on the first power of bandwidth, not the second power ($1/(\Delta \nu)^2$).

1. Calculate the coherence length of a gas laser operating at $\lambda = 632.8\text{ nm}$ with a spectral linewidth $\Delta \lambda = 10^{-5}\text{ nm}$.
Answer: Approximately $40\text{ meters}$.
$$L_c = \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta \lambda} = \frac{(632.8 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})^2}{10^{-14}\text{ m}} \approx 40.04\text{ m}$$.
2. What happens to the interference fringe visibility in a Michelson interferometer when the path difference exceeds $L_c$?
Answer: The fringe visibility drops to zero, and the interference pattern completely washes out into uniform background illumination.
When optical path difference $\Delta d > L_c$, the interfering wavepackets no longer overlap in phase.
3. What is the difference between temporal coherence and spatial coherence?
Answer: Temporal coherence describes phase correlation at the same point in space across different time instants (linked to spectral bandwidth $\Delta \nu$); spatial coherence describes phase correlation across different points across the wavefront (linked to source angular size).
A point source has high spatial coherence; a strictly monochromatic source has high temporal coherence.
4. In Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), why does chirping a transmitted pulse provide high range resolution without sacrificing power?
Answer: Chirping linearly sweeps the frequency across a wide bandwidth ($\Delta f$), allowing matched filter pulse compression into a very narrow effective pulse width $\tau = 1/\Delta f$.
Radar slant range resolution is given by $\Delta R = \frac{c}{2 \Delta f}$, depending solely on bandwidth.
5. Why do white-light interferometers require the two optical arms to be matched to within a few micrometers?
Answer: Because broad white-light spectrum ($\Delta \lambda \approx 300\text{ nm}$) results in a tiny coherence length ($L_c \approx 1\text{--}2\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).
Fringes only exist when the optical path difference is smaller than this micro-scale coherence length.
6. What is "coherence loss" or "decorrelation" in repeat-pass satellite radar interferometry (InSAR)?
Answer: The degradation of radar phase correlation between two acquisition dates caused by vegetation growth, wind, moisture changes, or excessive spatial baseline separation.
When temporal or geometric decorrelation occurs ($|\gamma| \to 0$), interferometric phase noise destroys elevation or deformation signals.
7. What is the critical baseline in SAR interferometry?
Answer: The maximum cross-track perpendicular baseline distance beyond which spectral shift decorrelation causes total loss of interferometric coherence ($|\gamma| = 0$).
The critical baseline is inversely proportional to radar wavelength and receiver bandwidth.
8. What fundamental theorem relates the spatial coherence of an incoherent extended optical source to the Fourier transform of its intensity distribution?
Answer: The Van Cittert-Zernike Theorem.
It explains how an incoherent source (like a distant star) produces high spatial coherence on Earth across small baseline separations.
