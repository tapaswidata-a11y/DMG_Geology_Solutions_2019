Mechanical Weathering: Exfoliation, Spheroidal Spalling & Rock Fabric

Weathering processes break down bedrock through physical disintegration and chemical decomposition. Among physical weathering phenomena, exfoliation (also termed onion-skin weathering or flaking) is the process whereby curved, concentric outer shells or sheets of rock spall and peel away from the core of a rock mass.

  • Structural Requirement: Exfoliation operates with maximum efficiency in massive, unstratified, isotropic rocks (such as massive granites, granodiorites, gabbros, and thick homogeneous sandstones). Because massive rocks lack pre-existing planar weaknesses (bedding planes, cleavage, or schistosity), internal thermal and mechanical stresses cannot be dissipated along planar fractures. Instead, stresses accumulate isotropically until concentric curvilinear failure surfaces develop parallel to the external rock surface.
  • Ineffectiveness in Layered / Foliated / Jointed Rocks:
    • In layered (stratified) or foliated (schists, slates, gneisses) rocks, weathering is guided almost entirely along pre-existing laminae and foliation planes, causing splitting into flat slabs (slabbing/fissility) rather than curved concentric exfoliation shells.
    • In heavily jointed rocks, block disintegration along intersecting joint planes isolates angular boulders without producing the massive, curved exfoliated domes characteristic of massive plutons.
  • Driving Mechanisms: Exfoliation in massive rocks is driven by two synergistic processes:
    1. Thermal Insolation & Diurnal Fluctuations: Rock is an exceptionally poor conductor of heat. Intense solar radiation rapidly expands the outer few centimeters of a rock surface, while the interior remains cold and unexpanded. Differential expansion creates severe shear stresses parallel to the surface, causing curved flakes to spall off.
    2. Chemical Spheroidal Weathering: Water penetrates micro-fissures, hydrolyzing feldspars into clay minerals (kaolinite). The formation of clay increases volume by $10-20\%$, exerting outward expansive pressure that pops off concentric shells around a core-stone.
"Exfoliation: The peeling off of curved concentric shells or layers from the surface of a rock mass is called exfoliation. It is particularly effective in massive rocks like granites and massive sandstones. In massive rocks devoid of bedding planes or joints, daily temperature variations produce differential expansion between outer and inner layers, causing the rock to peel like the coats of an onion." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Weathering of Rocks: Exfoliation & Spheroidal Weathering, p. 20)
Thermal Stress & Volumetric Expansion in Massive Rocks
$$\sigma_{\text{thermal}} = \frac{E \cdot \alpha_T \cdot \Delta T}{1 - \nu} \quad (\text{Biaxial Thermal Stress at Rock Surface})$$ $$\Delta V_{\text{hydration}} = \frac{V_{\text{clay}} - V_{\text{feldspar}}}{V_{\text{feldspar}}} \times 100\% \approx +15\% \text{ to } +25\%$$

Where $E$ is Young's modulus ($pprox 50\text{ GPa}$ for granite), $\alpha_T$ is thermal expansion coefficient ($pprox 8 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$), $\Delta T$ is diurnal temperature swing ($30-40^\circ\text{C}$ in arid regions), and $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio ($0.25$). Thermal stresses can exceed $60-80\text{ MPa}$, easily surpassing the tensile strength of granite ($5-10\text{ MPa}$).

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q036
The process of weathering by exfoliating activity is very effective in:
(A) Layered rocks
(B) Massive rocks
(C) Jointed rocks
(D) Foliated rocks
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (B) Massive rocks
Verified against Bangar (p. 20), Mahapatra (Textbook of Geology, p. 45), and Mukherjee (p. 28). Exfoliation is uniquely effective in massive, isotropic rocks (e.g., granites and massive sandstones) because the absence of bedding or foliation allows thermal and hydration stresses to spall off concentric curved shells.
(A) Layered rocks INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: In layered (sedimentary) rocks, weathering stresses are immediately accommodated along pre-existing bedding planes. As water freezes or salts crystallize, the rock separates into flat, tabular slabs parallel to the strata, precluding the formation of curved concentric exfoliation shells.
(B) Massive rocks CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: Massive rocks lack continuous planes of weakness such as bedding, cleavage, or dense joints. Consequently, thermal gradients (insolation) and chemical hydration volume changes cannot dissipate along planes; instead, they generate uniform, curved tensile stresses parallel to the external rock surface. This causes outer curved slabs to detach sequentially, producing the classic exfoliation domes, tors, and core-stones seen across massive granite plutons globally.
(C) Jointed rocks INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: In heavily jointed rocks, weathering proceeds predominantly via block disintegration. Freeze-thaw cycles (frost wedging) and plant roots wedge apart discrete, angular blocks bounded by joint sets, yielding angular scree and talus rather than continuous peeling of curved shells.
(D) Foliated rocks INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Foliated metamorphic rocks (schists, slates, phyllites) possess high directional anisotropy due to parallel-aligned platy phyllosilicate minerals (micas, chlorite). Weathering stresses exploit these planes of minimum tensile strength, producing fissility and jagged platy flakes, completely inhibiting concentric curved exfoliation.

8 Fully Worked Exam-Standard Practice Problems: Weathering Mechanics & Rock Fabrics
Problem 1: Calculation of Ice Crystallization Pressure in Joints
Maximum theoretical frost wedging pressure $P \approx 200\text{ MPa}$ at $-22^\circ\text{C}$
When confined water freezes, it expands by $9\%$. At $-22^\circ\text{C}$, the ice-water phase equilibrium pressure reaches $\approx 210\text{ MPa}$, far exceeding the compressive strength of any known rock ($100-200\text{ MPa}$), violently forcing joint blocks apart.
Problem 2: Role of Chemical Alteration in Spheroidal Weathering
Hydrolysis of plagioclase into smectite/kaolinite creates expansive lattice stress
Chemical weathering attacks the edges and corners of joint blocks faster than flat faces ($3\times$ faster at corners due to high surface-area-to-volume ratio), rounding angular blocks into spheroids while clay expansion pops off concentric outer layers.
Problem 3: Thermal Conductivity of Granite
Low conductivity ($k \approx 2.5 - 3.0\text{ W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$) concentrates thermal stress at surface
Because granite conducts heat poorly, diurnal solar heating penetrates less than $5-10\text{ cm}$. The exterior shell heats to $50^\circ\text{C}$ while the interior remains at $20^\circ\text{C}$, inducing severe thermal shear stresses that detach surface flakes.
Problem 4: Identification of Bornhardts in Peninsular India
Massive Archean granitic domes shaped by sheet exfoliation
The bornhardts and whalebacks of Hyderabad, Hampi, and Ramanagara (Karnataka) are massive crystalline inselbergs developed in Archean Closepet and Peninsular Granites via deep chemical weathering followed by stripping and sheet exfoliation.
Problem 5: Goldich Dissolution Series & Mineral Resistance
Olivine weathers fastest; Quartz weathers slowest
S.S. Goldich (1938) established that mineral susceptibility to chemical weathering is the inverse of Bowen's reaction series: Olivine $\to$ Augite $\to$ Hornblende $\to$ Biotite $\to$ K-feldspar $\to$ Muscovite $\to$ Quartz.
Problem 6: Exfoliation vs. Sheeting Terminology
Exfoliation is small-to-medium scale (thermal/chemical); sheeting is large-scale (lithostatic unloading)
While both involve spalling of curved slabs parallel to topography, sheeting refers to deep-seated stress relief fractures ($1-10\text{ m}$ thick) across whole mountains, whereas exfoliation encompasses thin surface peeling ($1-10\text{ cm}$) driven by insolation and hydration.
Problem 7: Weathering in Foliated Metamorphic Schists
Mechanical splitting along schistosity planes (fissility)
In mica schists, the continuous orientation of biotite and muscovite creates planes of zero cohesion. Moisture and oxidation attack these cleavage sheets, causing the rock to crumble into thin, pencil-like or platy shards rather than peeling concentrically.
Problem 8: Tafoni and Cavernous Weathering
Sub-surface granular disintegration beneath a case-hardened crust
In arid and coastal environments, salt crystallization and moisture evaporation hollow out rounded cavities (tafoni) beneath a sun-hardened iron/silica-rich exterior rind, particularly on massive sandstone and granite cliffs.
