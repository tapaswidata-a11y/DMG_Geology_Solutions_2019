Structural Control on Drainage Networks: The Trellis Pattern

A trellis drainage pattern is a geometric drainage network characterized by a system of primary subparallel master streams flowing along the strike of alternating bands of resistant and non-resistant folded or tilted rock strata, joined at approximately right angles ($90^\circ$) by short tributary streams flowing down the dip and anti-dip slopes. The resulting network strongly resembles a garden trellis used to support climbing vines.

"In areas where the rocks have been folded into parallel anticlines and synclines, or where homoclinal beds with alternating hard and soft layers crop out, a network of parallel or subparallel streams develops along the strike and dip directions. The master streams flow in strike valleys along the soft rocks, while short tributaries flow down the dip slopes at right angles to join them. This pattern is called a trellis drainage pattern." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Drainage Patterns & Structural Geology, p. 27)

The development of a trellis drainage network is strictly governed by the differential erosion of structurally deformed bedrock:

  • Structural Alignment: Characteristic of folded mountain belts with parallel plunging anticlines and synclines (e.g., the Appalachian Valley and Ridge Province; Zagros Fold Belt) and homoclinal ridges (cuestas and hogbacks).
  • Subsequent Master Streams: The long, primary parallel rivers develop as subsequent streams that excavate strike-valleys along weak, easily erodible strata (such as shales, marls, and weathered limestones).
  • Consequent & Obsequent Tributaries: Short secondary streams flow perpendicularly down the structural dip slopes (consequent streams) or down the steep scarp faces opposite to dip (obsequent streams), joining the strike streams at nearly $90^\circ$ angles.
  • Water Gaps & Wind Gaps: Where transverse superposed or antecedent master rivers breach resistant quartzite or sandstone ridges, narrow transverse gorges termed water gaps are created. If an incising strike stream captures the upper course of a transverse river via stream piracy, the abandoned transverse gap becomes a dry wind gap.

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 4)
4. A network of parallel or subparallel streams developed along strike and dip directions are known as :
(A) Dendritic
(B) Radial
(C) Trellis  ✔
(D) Domal
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Trellis
In structural geomorphology and photogeology (K.M. Bangar, p. 27; Arthur L. Bloom, p. 272; W.D. Thornbury, p. 122), a stream network where parallel streams follow strike and dip directions joining at right angles is universally termed a Trellis pattern.
(A) Dendritic Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A dendritic drainage pattern resembles the branching form of an oak or maple tree, where tributaries join at acute angles ($<90^\circ$). Dendritic drainage develops on horizontally bedded, homogeneous sedimentary rocks or isotropic massive igneous rocks (granite) where there is no structural control and rock resistance is uniform in all directions.
(B) Radial Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A radial drainage pattern develops when streams flow outward in all directions like spokes from a central topographic high, such as an isolated volcanic cone, volcanic crater rim, or elevated structural dome.
(C) Trellis Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: The trellis drainage pattern is explicitly defined by subparallel master streams flowing along strike valleys in weak strata, connected to parallel tributaries draining down structural dip slopes and scarp slopes, meeting at nearly $90^\circ$ right angles.
(D) Domal Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: "Domal" is not an internationally recognized canonical drainage pattern name. Streams draining structural domes exhibit either radial drainage (diverging outward from the center) or annular drainage (ring-like concentric circular channels following weak strata around an eroded structural dome).

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: How does a geologist distinguish in the field or on satellite imagery between a "Trellis" pattern and a "Rectangular" pattern?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Trellis patterns display alternating bands of differing rock types (lithologic control), whereas rectangular patterns develop across uniform lithologies cut by orthogonal joint sets.
    Explanation: Both patterns feature $90^\circ$ tributary junctions. However, trellis drainage is characterized by long, parallel strike ridges (hogbacks/cuestas) separated by strike valleys in folded strata. In rectangular drainage, the streams abruptly bend at right angles following joint and fault planes without lithologically controlled topographic ridges.
  • Practice Q2: What is a "water gap", and what is a "wind gap"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A water gap is a transverse notch through a resistant ridge containing an active stream; a wind gap is an abandoned, dry notch left behind after river piracy.
    Explanation: Water gaps are formed when superposed or antecedent streams cut perpendicularly across resistant strike ridges. If a subsequent strike stream captures the river upstream of the gap, flow through the ridge ceases, leaving a dry structural saddle termed a wind gap.
  • Practice Q3: In what type of drainage network does "river piracy" (stream capture) occur with the highest frequency?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Trellis drainage networks in folded strata.
    Explanation: Subsequent streams eroding rapidly along soft, non-resistant shale strike-valleys have lower base levels than transverse consequent streams crossing resistant quartzite ridges, allowing subsequent streams to easily breach interfluves and capture transverse streams.
  • Practice Q4: What does an "annular" drainage pattern diagnose in tectonic geomorphology?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A breached, deeply dissected structural dome or igneous pluton with concentric rings of alternating hard and soft sedimentary rock strata.
    Explanation: As an uplifted dome is eroded, streams etch circular subsequent valleys into weak concentric strata, forming ring-like channels (annular drainage) around the central crystalline core.
  • Practice Q5: What stream pattern is produced when streams flow in opposite directions away from a linear mountain divide?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Parallel or subparallel drainage.
    Explanation: On steep, elongated structural dip-slopes or regional fault scarps, streams flow down the slope in uniformly parallel paths without substantial branching.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "barbed" (or hooked) drainage pattern, and what tectonic phenomenon does it reveal?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Tributaries joining a master stream at acute upstream angles; diagnoses stream reversal caused by tectonic tilting or glacial damming.
    Explanation: Under normal conditions, tributaries join flowing downstream. A barbed pattern indicates that tectonic uplift or fault tilting reversed the flow direction of the master river while its tributaries maintained their original courses.
  • Practice Q7: In morphometric analysis, how does the Bifurcation Ratio ($R_b$) of a trellis drainage basin compare to a dendritic basin?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Trellis basins often exhibit anomalously high bifurcation ratios ($R_b > 5 - 10$) between specific stream orders due to structural elongation.
    Explanation: While dendritic networks in homogeneous rocks have stable bifurcation ratios between $3$ and $5$, strongly elongated trellis networks with parallel tributaries show high $R_b$ values.
  • Practice Q8: Why are trellis drainage basins particularly prone to delayed, attenuated hydrograph flood peaks compared to circular dendritic basins?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because tributary runoff travels along elongated, narrow strike valleys and arrives at the master stream at staggered intervals.
    Explanation: In circular dendritic basins, tributaries are equidistant from the basin mouth, causing simultaneous runoff concentration and sharp flood peaks. In elongated trellis basins, the elongation stretches time-to-peak.
