1. Theoretical Foundation: Solar Blackbody Radiation and Wien's Displacement Law

The Sun is the preeminent primary source of electromagnetic radiation for passive optical remote sensing. For thermodynamic calculations, the Sun radiates closely as an ideal blackbody radiator with an effective surface photosphere temperature of approximately $T_{\text{Sun}} \approx 5778\text{ K} \approx 5800\text{ K}$.

According to Planck's Radiation Law, the spectral radiant exitance ($M_\lambda$) of a blackbody across wavelength $\lambda$ is given by:

$$M_\lambda(T) = \frac{2\pi h c^2}{\lambda^5 \left[\exp\left(\frac{h c}{\lambda k_B T}\right) - 1\right]}$$

By differentiating Planck's equation with respect to wavelength ($\frac{dM_\lambda}{d\lambda} = 0$) and finding the wavelength corresponding to maximum spectral radiant exitance, Wilhelm Wien derived Wien's Displacement Law (1893):

$$\lambda_{\text{max}} \cdot T = A \implies \lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{A}{T}$$

where $A$ is Wien's displacement constant ($A \approx 2897.8\text{ }\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K} \approx 2898\text{ }\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}$). Substituting the Sun's effective photosphere temperature ($T \approx 5800\text{ K}$ to $5270\text{ K}$):

$$\text{At } T = 5800\text{ K}: \quad \lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{2898\text{ }\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{5800\text{ K}} \approx \mathbf{0.50\text{ }\mu\text{m}} \text{ (Blue-Green)}$$ $$\text{At } T = 5270\text{ K}: \quad \lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{2898\text{ }\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{5270\text{ K}} \approx \mathbf{0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}} \text{ (Green-Yellow visible)}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 133
The maximum sun’s radiation occurs around :
A 0.4 um wave length
B 0.55 wave length
C 0.7 um wavelength
D None of these
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (0.55 wave length [0.55 µm])
OPTION B • CORRECT
0.55 wave length (0.55 µm)

By Wien's displacement law ($\lambda_{\text{max}} = A/T$), for a solar surface temperature of $\approx 5500\text{--}5800\text{ K}$, the peak spectral emission is centered around $\mathbf{0.48\text{ to } 0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}}$ (the green-yellow visible portion of the electromagnetic spectrum). In standard geology and remote sensing literature (Lillesand & Kiefer; Bangar, p. 208), this peak is cited as $0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ (or $550\text{ nm}$).

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
0.4 um wave length

$0.4\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ marks the violet boundary between ultraviolet and visible light. While solar irradiance is substantial here, it lies on the steep rising limb of Planck's curve and is significantly lower than the peak at $0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
0.7 um wavelength

$0.7\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ ($700\text{ nm}$) marks the red boundary where visible light transitions into the near-infrared (NIR). Beyond $0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}$, solar spectral irradiance decreases monotonically with increasing wavelength.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
None of these

Incorrect, because Option B is the recognized standard answer.

1. Using Wien's displacement law ($\lambda_{\text{max}} = 2898/T$), calculate the peak emission wavelength of the Earth's surface assuming a mean temperature of $T = 288\text{ K}$ ($15^\circ\text{C}$).
Answer: Approximately $10.06\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ (in the thermal infrared band).
$$\lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{2898}{288} \approx 10.06\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$; this falls inside the $8\text{--}14\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ atmospheric window.
2. Why does the human eye have its peak photopic visual sensitivity at approximately $555\text{ nm}$ ($0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}$)?
Answer: Evolutionary adaptation to the peak spectral emission of sunlight penetrating the Earth's atmosphere.
Terrestrial organisms evolved optical receptors tuned precisely to where solar photon flux is most abundant.
3. What is the crossover wavelength where reflected solar radiation equals emitted terrestrial thermal radiation?
Answer: Approximately $3.0\text{ to } 4.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ (in the mid-infrared band).
Below $3.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$, reflected solar radiation dominates; above $4.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$, emitted terrestrial thermal radiation dominates.
4. What is the Stefan-Boltzmann Law and how does total solar exitance scale with surface temperature?
Answer: $M = \sigma T^4$; total emitted radiant energy increases with the fourth power of absolute temperature.
Doubling the surface temperature increases total radiant emission by a factor of $2^4 = 16$.
5. What is the value of the Solar Constant ($S_0$) at the mean Earth-Sun distance (1 AU)?
Answer: Approximately $1361\text{ W/m}^2$ (measured at top of atmosphere by satellite radiometers).
Solar constant variations over the 11-year sunspot cycle are small (~0.1%).
6. In Wien's law, why is it called a "displacement" law?
Answer: Because as temperature increases, the peak of the blackbody radiation curve is "displaced" toward shorter and shorter wavelengths ($\lambda_{\text{max}} \propto 1/T$).
A heating iron bar transitions from infrared (invisible) to dull red ($700\text{ nm}$), bright orange, and finally white-hot ($0.5\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).
7. What are the Fraunhofer absorption lines observed in the solar spectrum?
Answer: Dark absorption lines produced by cooler atomic gases in the Sun's outer chromosphere absorbing specific resonant wavelengths (e.g. Sodium D-lines at $589\text{ nm}$, Hydrogen H-$\alpha$ at $656\text{ nm}$).
Fraunhofer lines allow remote spectroscopic determination of the chemical composition of the Sun.
8. What fraction of total solar energy falls within the visible spectrum ($0.4\text{--}0.7\text{ }\mu\text{m}$)?
Answer: Approximately $43\%$ to $45\%$.
Nearly half of all solar photons fall inside the narrow visible slice centered at $0.55\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
