1. Theoretical Foundation: The Spatial Interpolation Process

In earth science, environmental geology, mining engineering, and GIS, financial and logistical constraints make it impossible to collect physical measurements at every point across a continuous landscape. Instead, scientists collect field data at a discrete set of known sample locations (e.g. exploration boreholes, groundwater monitoring wells, seismic shotpoints, or weather stations).

By international GIS definition (Chang; Burrough & McDonnell; Isaaks & Srivastava), the computational and mathematical process of using data points with known values to estimate values at unknown, unmeasured points in any given area is called Spatial Interpolation (Option B):

$$\hat{z}(x_0, y_0) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \cdot z(x_i, y_i)$$

where $\hat{z}(x_0, y_0)$ is the estimated attribute value at the unknown target location $(x_0, y_0)$, $z(x_i, y_i)$ are the known sample values, and $\lambda_i$ are mathematical weights assigned to each sample point (typically subject to $\sum \lambda_i = 1$).

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 139
The process of using data points with known values to estimate values at unknown points in any given area is called :
A Spatial Prediction
B Spatial interpolation
C Spatial Adjustment
D Spatial Reflection
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (Spatial interpolation)
OPTION B • CORRECT
Spatial interpolation

By verbatim textbook and standard GIS definition (Chang, Ch. 15; Burrough & McDonnell; Bangar, p. 217), the exact process of taking known geographic sample data points and estimating values at unmeasured locations within the bounds of the study area is called Spatial Interpolation.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Spatial Prediction

While geostatistics sometimes uses "spatial prediction" informally as an umbrella term, "spatial interpolation" is the precise, formal technical term universally used in GIS and spatial analysis curricula for estimating unknown points from known sample points.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Spatial Adjustment

Spatial adjustment refers to rubbersheeting, edge-matching, and geometric coordinate transformation of digitized vector linework to align with georeferenced base features. It does not estimate surface attribute values.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Spatial Reflection

Fabricated distractor mixing wave optics ("reflection") with spatial terminology; has no meaning in spatial analysis.

1. What is the fundamental difference between Spatial Interpolation and Spatial Extrapolation?
Answer: Interpolation estimates values *inside* the spatial boundary bounded by the control points; extrapolation estimates values *outside* the convex hull of known points (which carries extreme uncertainty).
Interpolation is statistically constrained by surrounding points; extrapolation diverges rapidly.
2. What distinguishes an "Exact" interpolator from an "Inexact" interpolator?
Answer: An exact interpolator predicts values that match the original sample values identically at the sample locations ($\hat{z}(x_i) = z(x_i)$); an inexact interpolator smooths the surface and may predict different values at sample points.
IDW and Splines are exact; Trend surface analysis is inexact.
3. What is the effect of increasing the power parameter ($p$) in Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW)?
Answer: Higher power values (e.g. $p=3$ or $4$) concentrate influence almost entirely on the immediately adjacent nearest neighbor, making the surface steeper around points; lower power ($p=1$) smooths the surface.
The standard default value in GIS software is $p=2$ (inverse distance squared).
4. Why is Kriging widely regarded as superior to deterministic methods in mining ore evaluation?
Answer: Because Kriging models the spatial continuity structure (semivariogram), accounts for clustering of sample boreholes (declustering effect), and outputs an objective Kriging Variance map quantifying estimation uncertainty.
Mining companies use the kriging error map to identify drill-hole gaps before sinking expensive shafts.
5. What is "Cokriging"?
Answer: A geostatistical technique that interpolates a primary sparsely sampled variable (e.g. soil heavy metal concentration) by utilizing cross-correlation with a densely sampled, cheaper secondary variable (e.g. airborne radiometrics or elevation).
Cokriging leverages the cross-semivariogram between two correlated spatial variables.
6. What causes the "Bull's-Eye" effect in IDW interpolated surfaces?
Answer: As the estimation point approaches an isolated extreme high or low sample point, distance $d \to 0$, causing weight $1/d^p \to \infty$ and creating concentric circular contour rings.
Bull's-eyes are artificial artifacts of distance weighting without directional derivative constraints.
7. What is the Cross-Validation technique used to evaluate the accuracy of spatial interpolation?
Answer: "Leave-One-Out" cross-validation: temporarily remove one sample point, estimate its value using the remaining $n-1$ points, compute residual error ($z_i - \hat{z}_i$), and repeat for all $n$ points to calculate RMSE.
The method yielding the lowest RMSE is selected as the optimal interpolation model.
8. What is Universal Kriging used for in geological terrain modeling?
Answer: For spatial datasets that exhibit a non-stationary regional deterministic trend (drift) along with local random spatial correlation.
Universal Kriging separates the global trend (e.g. regional fault dip) from local stationary semivariance residuals.
