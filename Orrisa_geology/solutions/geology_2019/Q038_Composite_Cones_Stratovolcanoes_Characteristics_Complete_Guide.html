Volcanic Geomorphology: Stratovolcanoes & Composite Cone Architecture

Volcanic landforms constructed around central vents display distinct topographic profiles governed by the rheology of their erupted products. When a volcano erupts alternately between explosive pyroclastic fragmentation and effusive lava flows, it constructs a monumental edifice known as a Composite Cone (or Stratovolcano).

  • Internal Stratification: A composite cone is constructed from alternating layers (strata) of pyroclastic tephra (ash, cinders, bombs, lapilli) and solidified lava flows. This interstratified architecture is the explicit source of the term stratovolcano.
  • Topographic Profile: Stratovolcanoes exhibit a classic, majestic concave-upward conical profile:
    • Summit slopes: Very steep ($30^\circ - 35^\circ$), supported by the angle of repose of coarse pyroclastic debris and viscous lava spines.
    • Basal flanks: Flatter and gentler ($5^\circ - 10^\circ$), built by far-reaching viscous lava flows, lahars (volcanic mudflows), and ash falls.
  • Magmatic Composition: Primarily intermediate to felsic magmas (andesite, dacite, and minor rhyolite). The viscous andesitic lava acts as a natural mortar, cementing the loose pyroclastic layers and granting the cone exceptional structural elevation (frequently reaching $3000-6000\text{ m}$ height).
  • Global Setting: Stratovolcanoes dominate convergent plate boundaries (subduction zones) around the Pacific Circum-Pacific Belt (Ring of Fire). Renowned global examples include Mount Fuji (Japan), Mount Mayon (Philippines), Mount Rainier (USA), Mount Vesuvius (Italy), and Mount Cotopaxi (Ecuador).
"Composite Cones: These cones are composed of alternate layers of pyroclastic material and solidified lava flows. Composite cones with marked stratification are called stratovolcanoes. They are the most picturesque and large-sized volcanoes. Examples include Mt. Vesuvius, Mt. Fuji, and Mt. Mayon." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Volcanoes: Volcanic Cones, p. 48)
Slope Equilibrium & Volume of a Symmetrical Stratovolcano
$$\text{Summit Slope: } \theta_{\text{summit}} \approx 30^\circ - 35^\circ \approx \phi_{\text{repose of cinders}}$$ $$\text{Basal Slope: } \theta_{\text{base}} \approx 5^\circ - 10^\circ$$ $$V_{\text{cone}} = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 H \quad (\text{Geometric volume of conical edifice})$$

Where $R$ is basal radius ($10-20\text{ km}$) and $H$ is elevation above baseline ($2-4\text{ km}$). The alternating competent lava sheets prevent catastrophic structural collapse of loose pyroclastic beds.

Official Examination Question · OPSC 2019 Paper-II (GGM - 2/2019) · Q038
Composite cones are the characteristics of :
(A) Strato volcanoes
(B) Cinder cone volcanoes
(C) Shield volcano
(D) Lava domes
✓
CORRECT OPTION: (A) Strato volcanoes
Verified against Bangar (p. 48), Tarbuck & Lutgens (Earth Science), and Philpotts & Ague (Principles of Igneous Petrology). Composite cones are by definition the physical volcanic edifices termed stratovolcanoes, characterized by alternating strata of pyroclastic debris and solidified lava.
(A) Strato volcanoes CORRECT VERIFIED
Forensic Analysis: The term stratovolcano is directly derived from its stratified internal architecture: alternating strata (composite layers) of pyroclastic tephra (ash, lapilli, cinders) and viscous lava flows. These composite layers build steep, symmetrical, monumental mountains with concave slopes, forming the classic composite cones recognized worldwide (such as Mount Fuji and Mount Mayon).
(B) Cinder cone volcanoes INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Cinder cones (scoria cones) are constructed purely from loose pyroclastic debris (cinders and lapilli) ejected into the air that rain down around the vent at their natural angle of repose ($pprox 30-33^\circ$). They are small (rarely exceeding $300-400 ext{ m}$ height), monogenetic, and lack the alternating lava sheets that define composite cones.
(C) Shield volcano INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Shield volcanoes (e.g., Mauna Loa in Hawaii) are built almost exclusively by successive flows of fluid basaltic lava with very little to no pyroclastic material. Because fluid basalt spreads thin over immense distances, shield volcanoes develop extremely broad, low-angle convex slopes ($2^\circ-8^\circ$) resembling a warrior's shield, rather than steep composite cones.
(D) Lava domes INCORRECT
Forensic Analysis: Lava domes (tholoids) form when degassed, highly viscous rhyolitic or dacitic lava is squeezed out of a vent without explosive dispersal, piling up into a steep-sided bulbous dome. They lack conical symmetry and do not display alternating pyroclastic and lava layering.

8 Fully Worked Exam-Standard Practice Problems: Volcanic Geomorphology & Stratocones
Problem 1: Volume Calculation of a Symmetrical Stratocone
Edifice Volume $V = 314.16\text{ km}^3$
A symmetrical stratovolcano has a basal diameter of $D = 20\text{ km}$ (radius $R = 10\text{ km}$) and rises to an elevation of $H = 3.0\text{ km}$. Approximate its volume using a cone formula.
$$V = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 H = \frac{1}{3} \pi (10)^2 (3.0) = 100 \pi = 314.16\text{ km}^3$$
Problem 2: Angle of Repose of Volcanic Cinders
Angle of repose is $\approx 33^\circ$
Loose scoria and volcanic cinders have internal friction angles between $32^\circ$ and $36^\circ$. Gravity forces dry granular debris to slide until it stabilizes at its angle of repose ($pprox 33^\circ$), establishing the summit slope of stratovolcanoes and cinder cones.
Problem 3: Hydrothermal Alteration & Edifice Instability
Acid sulfate fluids alter competent volcanic rock into weak smectite clays
Circulating hydrothermal fluids within stratovolcanoes convert resistant lavas into water-saturated clays, causing major internal weakening that predisposes the volcanic cone to catastrophic sector collapse and gigantic debris avalanches.
Problem 4: Lahars (Volcanic Mudflows) on Stratovolcanoes
Mobilization of loose summit tephra by melting snow/ice or heavy rainfall
Stratovolcanoes generate deadly lahars because loose, fine-grained pyroclastic ash on steep slopes easily mixes with water (from crater lake breaches, rain, or glacial melt), creating slurries with the density of wet concrete that travel at $>50\text{ km/h}$.
Problem 5: Barren Island Volcano, India
Only active stratovolcano in the Indian subcontinent
Barren Island in the Andaman Sea is an active composite stratovolcano situated along the volcanic arc produced by the subduction of the Indian Plate beneath the Burma Microplate.
Problem 6: Parasitic Cones on Flanks
Adventive cones fed by radial dykes branching off the central conduit
When the main conduit of a high stratovolcano becomes clogged by solidified magma plugs, lateral magma pressure fractures the cone flanks via radial dykes, erupting on the mountain sides to form parasitic cinder cones.
Problem 7: Magmatic Evolution of Stratovolcanoes
Fractional crystallization and crustal assimilation produce calc-alkaline suites
Subduction-derived hydrous basaltic parental magmas undergo extensive fractionation and assimilation of continental crust within crustal magma chambers, evolving from basaltic andesite to andesite, dacite, and rhyolite (the classic calc-alkaline series).
Problem 8: Somma Volcano Morphology
A new stratocone growing within the collapsed caldera of an older volcano
A somma volcano (named after Monte Somma, Italy) occurs when a younger stratovolcano cone (Vesuvius) builds inside the caldera rim of a partially destroyed ancient composite cone.
