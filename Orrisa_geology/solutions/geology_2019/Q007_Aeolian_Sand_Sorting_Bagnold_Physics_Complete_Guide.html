Physics of Aeolian Grain Transport & Granulometric Sorting

In physical geology and sedimentology, aeolian deposits (wind-blown sediments) exhibit the most exquisite and precise grain-size sorting observed anywhere in the natural geological world. The physical reason why well-sorted aeolian accumulations (dunes, ergs, and sand sheets) are overwhelmingly composed of sand (specifically medium-to-fine quartz sand, $0.125 - 0.50\text{ mm}$) is governed by the fundamental fluid mechanics of wind transport formalized by Brigadier Ralph A. Bagnold (1941, The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes).

"Wind is a remarkably efficient sorting agent. Because air has a very low density compared to water, it cannot transport coarse pebbles and gravels except by rolling them along the ground, while fine dust and silt are carried far away in turbulent suspension to form loess. The intermediate size fraction—consisting of medium to fine sand—moves by hopping and bouncing (saltation) and accumulates into dunes. Therefore, well-sorted aeolian deposits are chiefly composed of sand." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Geological Work of Wind, p. 23)

Wind moves sediment via three sharply distinct physical transport modes, dictated by grain diameter ($D$):

  • Surface Creep / Traction ($D > 0.5 - 2.0\text{ mm}$, Coarse Sand & Granules): Particles too heavy to be lifted into the air. They are pushed and rolled along the surface by the kinetic impacts of landing saltating grains ($20 - 25\%$ of total aeolian bedload).
  • Saltation ($D \approx 0.125 - 0.50\text{ mm}$, Medium to Fine Sand): Grains are lifted by fluid aerodynamic drag and Bernoulli lift, ascending into parabolic trajectories before crashing back to the surface at shallow angles ($10^\circ - 16^\circ$). This impact dislodges and ejects new grains into saltation. Saltation accounts for $75 - 80\%$ of all aeolian sand transport and produces near-perfectly sorted quartz sand deposits ($\sigma_\phi < 0.35 - 0.50$).
  • Suspension ($D < 0.05\text{ mm}$, Silt and Clay Dust): Particles small enough that turbulent upward eddies in the wind exceed their gravitational terminal settling velocity ($w_s$). Silt and clay are carried thousands of kilometers downwind in high-altitude dust storms, bypassing dune fields entirely and settling across distant humid margins as unstratified loess blankets.
Bagnold's Fluid Threshold Shear Velocity for Grain Entrainment (Bagnold, 1941)
$$u_{*t} = A \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\rho_s - \rho}{\rho} \cdot g \cdot D}$$

Where $u_{*t}$ is the critical fluid threshold shear velocity, $A \approx 0.1$ is an empirical constant, $\rho_s \approx 2650\text{ kg/m}^3$ is quartz grain density, $\rho \approx 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$ is air density, $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$, and $D$ is grain diameter. Crucially, as $D$ decreases below $0.08\text{ mm}$ (silt and clay), cohesive electrostatic forces and the smooth viscous sublayer cause $u_{*t}$ to sharply increase, making fine sand ($0.1 - 0.25\text{ mm}$) by far the most easily entrained grain size on Earth!

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 7)
7. Well sorted aeolian deposits are chiefly composed of :
(A) Clay
(B) Sand  ✔
(C) Sand and Silt
(D) Silt and Clay
✔
Correct Answer: (B) Sand
In standard university sedimentology (Bagnold, 1941; K.M. Bangar, p. 23; Sam Boggs Jr., p. 268; Folk, 1974), well-sorted aeolian accumulations (dunes, ergs, sand sheets) are fundamentally and chiefly composed of Sand (medium-to-fine quartz sand).
(A) Clay Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Clay particles ($<0.002\text{ mm}$) possess strong electrostatic surface charges (cohesion) and lie within the laminar viscous sublayer, making them nearly impossible for wind to entrain directly from dry mudflats. Once entrained, clay remains in high turbulent suspension over thousands of kilometers and never accumulates as local, well-sorted bedforms.
(B) Sand Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: Because air has very low viscosity and low density, wind acts as a hyper-selective sorting filter. Grains of medium-to-fine sand ($0.125 - 0.50\text{ mm}$) move predominantly by saltation, accumulating in vast dune fields and ergs as exceptionally well-sorted, clean sand deposits, while finer silt and clay are winnowed away into suspension.
(C) Sand and Silt Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: While poorly sorted desert wadi wash may contain sand and silt, well-sorted aeolian deposits are remarkably devoid of silt. Silt particles are winnowed out by continuous aeolian saltation bombardment and lifted into atmospheric suspension.
(D) Silt and Clay Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Silt and clay mixtures constitute aeolian loess and atmospheric dust deposits. Loess is typically a massive, unstratified, moderately sorted silt deposit ($0.02 - 0.05\text{ mm}$) rather than the quintessential "well-sorted aeolian deposit" represented by dune sands.

High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A granulometric sieving analysis of an aeolian dune sand yields the following cumulative percentiles on the Wentworth phi scale: $\phi_{16} = 2.10$, $\phi_{84} = 2.70$, $\phi_5 = 1.80$, $\phi_{95} = 3.00$. Calculate the Folk and Ward Graphic Standard Deviation (sorting coefficient, $\sigma_I$) and classify its sorting quality.
    ✔ Worked Answer: $\sigma_I = 0.332$ (Very Well Sorted).
    Derivation:
    Using the Folk and Ward (1957) Inclusive Graphic Standard Deviation formula:
    $$\sigma_I = \frac{\phi_{84} - \phi_{16}}{4} + \frac{\phi_{95} - \phi_5}{6.6}$$ $$\sigma_I = \frac{2.70 - 2.10}{4} + \frac{3.00 - 1.80}{6.6} = \frac{0.60}{4} + \frac{1.20}{6.6} = 0.150 + 0.1818 = 0.3318$$
    Since $\sigma_I < 0.35$, the sediment is classified as Very Well Sorted, a hallmark of aeolian dune sands.
  • Practice Q2: Why does the threshold shear velocity curve ($u_{*t}$) reach an absolute minimum for grains of size $0.10 - 0.15\text{ mm}$ (fine sand) and increase sharply for smaller silt and clay particles?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because cohesive electrostatic forces (van der Waals attraction) bind fine clay/silt particles together, and their small diameter hides them beneath the laminar viscous sublayer.
    Explanation: To lift a clay particle, wind must overcome not just gravity, but powerful inter-particle cohesion. For sand grains ($>0.1\text{ mm}$), cohesion is negligible and grains protrude into turbulent air flow, making fine sand the easiest grain size to transport.
  • Practice Q3: What is the classic angle of repose for dry quartz sand forming the slip-face (lee slope) of an aeolian sand dune?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Approximately $30^\circ \text{ to } 34^\circ$.
    Explanation: Sand saltating up the gentle windward stoss slope ($10^\circ - 15^\circ$) reaches the dune crest and avalanches down the lee side under gravity, maintaining the natural angle of internal friction of dry cohesionless sand ($32^\circ - 34^\circ$).
  • Practice Q4: Distinguish between a Barchan dune and a Parabolic dune based on horn orientation and environmental setting.
    ✔ Correct Answer: Barchan horns point downwind in arid, vegetation-free environments; Parabolic horns point upwind, anchored by coastal or semi-arid vegetation.
    Explanation: In barchans, the central dune body moves slower than the thin flanking horns. In parabolic dunes, coastal shrubs pin the horns while the blowout center migrates downwind.
  • Practice Q5: What microscopic surface texture under SEM distinguishes aeolian sand grains from subaqueous fluvial sand grains?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A frosted, pitted surface with dense crescentic percussion pits and upturned cleavage plates caused by violent grain-to-grain impacts in air.
    Explanation: The absence of a buoyant, cushioning water film allows unbuffered grain collisions in air, chipping microscopic pits that give grains a frosted appearance.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "Yardang", and how does it form?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A streamlined, sharp-crested, keel-shaped bedrock ridge aligned parallel to the prevailing wind, carved by wind abrasion and deflation.
    Explanation: Wind armed with saltating sand scours softer strata, leaving streamlined, aerodynamic ridges that minimize drag.
  • Practice Q7: Why do ancient aeolian sandstones (e.g., the Jurassic Navajo Sandstone in the USA or Permian Rotliegend in Europe) constitute superior hydrocarbon and groundwater aquifers?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Superb sorting, high roundness, lack of fine clay/silt matrix, and well-connected pore networks give them exceptionally high porosity ($20 - 30\%$) and permeability ($>1000\text{ mD}$).
    Explanation: Aeolian winnowing strips away impermeable clay minerals, creating uniform pore throats.
  • Practice Q8: What is "desert pavement" (or reg/serir), and how does it develop?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A closely packed, armor-like surface layer of coarse pebbles and rock fragments left behind after wind deflation has winnowed away all sand and silt.
    Explanation: Once the pavement forms, it protects the underlying fine material from further wind deflation, establishing a geomorphic steady state.
