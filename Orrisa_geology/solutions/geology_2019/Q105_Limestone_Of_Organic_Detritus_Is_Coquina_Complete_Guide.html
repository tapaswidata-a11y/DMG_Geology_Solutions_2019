1. Theoretical Foundation: Classification of Bioclastic Limestones

Limestones ($\text{CaCO}_3$) are divided genetically into allochemical (clastic/detrital), autochthonous organic (biogenic reefal), and orthochemical (chemically precipitated) rocks. A limestone composed almost entirely (typically $> 75\%$ by volume) of coarse, mechanically sorted, broken or whole organic detritus (shells of molluscs, brachiopods, corals, and skeletal debris) is defined as a Coquina:

Limestone Variety Dominant Allochem / Mineral Constituent Genetic Mechanism High-Yield Distinguishing Feature
COQUINA (Option B) Coarse Organic Shell Detritus (> 75%) (bivalves, gastropods, brachiopods). Mechanical accumulation and winnowing in high-energy littoral / beach surf zones. Loosely cemented, highly porous aggregate of seashell fragments; completely bioclastic.
Coquinite Organic Shell Detritus (> 75%) Complete deep burial cementation of coquina. Firm, thoroughly indurated, dense equivalent of coquina with occluded porosity.
Oolite / Oolitic Limestone (Option C) Ooids (Ooliths) (spherical concentric grains, < 2 mm). Inorganic chemical precipitation around a nucleus in agitated, warm, supersaturated shallows. Concentric radial carbonate lamination around quartz or shell seed; non-skeletal.
Spergenite (Option A) Ooids + Endothyrid foraminifera + Microfossil debris. Shallow marine calcarenite (Bedford / Indiana limestone). Fine-grained bioclastic-oolitic freestone with excellent dimension stone properties.
Calc-Tufa / Travertine (Option D) Microcrystalline calcite precipitated around plant matter. Inorganic freshwater precipitation from springs, streams, or waterfalls. Spongy, cellular, highly vesicular texture preserving plant leaf impressions.
"A limestone which consists predominantly of mechanically sorted, broken or whole organic detritus (especially shells of bivalves, gastropods, and brachiopods) held together by a small amount of calcite cement is called a coquina. In modern beach environments, such as along the coast of Florida, coquina forms porous deposits of shell gravel. When thoroughly cemented and hardened into a solid rock, it is termed coquinite."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Sedimentary Rocks, p. 138)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 105
A limestone which contains mainly of organic detritus is generally called as :
A Spergenite
B Coquina
C Oolite
D Calc-tufa
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (Coquina)
OPTION B • CORRECT
Coquina

By universal consensus in carbonate petrology (Dunham, 1962; Folk, 1962; Bangar, p. 138), a limestone composed predominantly or entirely of organic detritus (broken seashells, mollusc valves, and skeletal debris) is formally termed a coquina (from the Spanish word for cockle or small shell).

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Spergenite

Spergenite (or Salem/Bedford limestone) is a specific Mississippian calcarenite composed of dwarf foraminifera (Endothyra) and micro-ooids in sparite, not coarse organic shell debris.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Oolite

Oolite is a limestone composed of inorganic ooids (spherical sand-sized grains with concentric cortical laminae precipitated around nuclei), which are chemical in origin rather than skeletal organic detritus.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Calc-tufa

Calc-tufa is a porous, spongy, freshwater calcium carbonate deposit chemically precipitated around springs, waterfalls, and lake margins by the loss of carbon dioxide, not a marine accumulation of organic detritus.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. From which language is the word 'coquina' derived and what does it literally mean?
Answer: Spanish (meaning 'cockle' or 'shellfish').
Spanish settlers in St. Augustine, Florida, quarried this shell rock in the 17th century to build Castillo de San Marcos.
2. What is the Dunham (1962) classification term for a grain-supported limestone containing less than 1% mud?
Answer: Grainstone
Dunham categorized limestones by fabric: Mudstone (<10% grains), Wackestone (>10% grains, mud-supported), Packstone (grain-supported with mud), Grainstone (grain-supported, mud-free).
3. What is the difference between an ooid and a pisoid?
Answer: Ooids are less than 2 mm in diameter; pisoids are greater than 2 mm in diameter.
Pisoids form in calcretes, soils, and hypersaline lagoons, displaying larger concentric laminae.
4. What soft, white, porous, friable limestone is composed almost entirely of microscopic calcareous algae (coccoliths)?
Answer: Chalk (e.g., White Cliffs of Dover, UK)
Chalk is an ultra-fine biomicrite composed of disarticulated calcite plates (coccoliths) secreted by planktonic haptophyte algae.
5. What is a calcarenite?
Answer: A limestone consisting of sand-sized (1/16 mm to 2 mm) carbonate allochems (bioclasts, ooids, or peloids).
Calcarenite is the carbonate equivalent of sandstone; coarser is calcrudite; finer is calcilutite.
6. Why were cannonballs fired at the coquina walls of Castillo de San Marcos in 1702 unable to breach the fortress?
Answer: Because the porous, compressible shell fabric of coquina absorbed the kinetic energy like dense foam, embedding the cannonballs without shattering.
The high porosity and interlocking shell fragments gave coquina remarkable shock-absorbing engineering resilience.
7. What is an intraclast in Folk's carbonate classification?
Answer: A fragment of semi-consolidated carbonate sediment ripped up from within the same basin and redeposited.
Intraclasts are carbonate equivalents of clay galls (intrasparite or packstone/rudstone).
8. What is a peloid (or fecal pellet)?
Answer: An ovoid, structureless grain of microcrystalline carbonate mud produced predominantly by the fecal excretion of invertebrate organisms.
Gastropods and crustaceans ingest carbonate mud and excrete peloids that form peloidal limestones (pelsparite).
