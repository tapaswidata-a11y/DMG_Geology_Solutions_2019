Morphological Classification of Folds by Axial Plane Attitude

In structural geology (Fleuty, 1964; Billings, 1972; Ramsay, 1967; Bangar, p. 190), folds are rigorously categorized according to the dip angle of their axial plane (axial surface). When tectonic deformation is so severe that the axial plane is pushed over until it is subhorizontal to completely horizontal (dip $0^\circ - 10^\circ$), the fold is universally termed a recumbent fold.

"A recumbent fold is an overtilted fold in which the axial plane has been rotated into an essentially horizontal position (dip of axial plane is less than $10^\circ$ or nearly $0^\circ$). In a recumbent fold, the limbs are roughly horizontal, with one limb lying directly above the other. The lower limb is completely inverted (overturned), while the upper limb is normal. Recumbent folds are characteristic of regions of intense orogenic compression, such as the Alps and the Himalayas." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Chapter 7: Classification of Folds, p. 190)

A recumbent fold consists of two distinct structural components:

  • Normal Limb (Upper / Autochthonous): The upper limb, where the stratigraphic sequence maintains its original normal polarity (younger beds overlying older beds).
  • Inverted / Overturned Limb (Lower / Reverse): The lower limb lying beneath the subhorizontal axial surface, where the entire stratigraphic sequence has been upside-down inverted (older beds overlying younger beds).
  • Nappe Evolution: When tectonic compressional stress continues beyond recumbent folding, the inverted limb is sheared out and sheared along a low-angle thrust fault (thrust plane), translating the upper normal limb tens to hundreds of kilometers as an allochthonous nappe (thrust nappe).

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 21)
21. If the axial plane of the fold is horizontal, then it is said to be :
(A) Open fold
(B) Tight fold
(C) Recumbent fold  ✔
(D) Isoclinal fold
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Recumbent fold
In structural geology (K.M. Bangar, p. 190; Marland P. Billings, p. 48; M.J. Fleuty, 1964; Haakon Fossen, p. 242), a fold whose axial plane is horizontal (or nearly horizontal, dip $<10^\circ$) is universally defined as a Recumbent fold.
(A) Open fold Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: "Open fold" is an interlimb angle classification describing the degree of fold tightness ($70^\circ \le ext{interlimb angle} \le 120^\circ$). It specifies how tightly the limbs are squeezed, completely independent of the spatial orientation or dip of the axial plane (an open fold can be upright, inclined, or recumbent).
(B) Tight fold Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: "Tight fold" also refers to interlimb angle ($0^\circ \le ext{interlimb angle} \le 30^\circ$). It describes tight compressive squeezing of the limbs, not the inclination of the axial plane.
(C) Recumbent fold Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: By fundamental international definition, a recumbent fold is a fold whose axial plane has been overturned and rotated into a horizontal or subhorizontal orientation ($ ext{Dip } \le 10^\circ$), with the lower limb inverted beneath the upper normal limb.
(D) Isoclinal fold Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: An isoclinal fold is a fold where both limbs are mutually parallel (interlimb angle $0^\circ - 10^\circ$), dipping at the same angle in the same direction. An isoclinal fold can have a vertical axial plane (upright isocline), an inclined axial plane, or a horizontal axial plane (recumbent isocline). Thus, "isoclinal" defines limb parallelism, not a horizontal axial plane.

Tectonic Metamorphic Architecture of Recumbent Folds & Nappes

Recumbent folds are the fundamental building blocks of ductile collision orogens (e.g., the Penninic nappes of the Alps and the Higher Himalayan Crystalline nappes):

  • Inverted Metamorphic Sequences: In recumbent folds and thrust nappes, hot, high-grade crystalline rocks (kyanite/sillimanite gneisses) are thrust over cooler, low-grade rocks (garnet/biotite schists), producing the famous "inverted metamorphism" observed across the Main Central Thrust (MCT) zone of the Himalayas.
  • Facing and Polarity: Stratigraphic facing (younging direction) in the upper limb faces upwards toward the sky, whereas facing in the lower overturned limb points downwards into the Earth.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What primary sedimentary way-up structure can a field geologist use to confirm that they are standing on the overturned lower limb of a recumbent fold?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Inverted cross-bedding (truncated tops pointing down), upside-down graded bedding (coarse at top, fine at bottom), or inverted geopetal structures.
    Explanation: Because bedding is nearly horizontal in a recumbent fold, only way-up structures reveal whether the beds are right-side-up (upper limb) or upside-down inverted (lower limb).
  • Practice Q2: What structural feature forms when the thinned, sheared lower limb of a recumbent fold is completely cut out along a shear plane?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A thrust nappe (fold nappe / nappe de recouvrement).
    Explanation: Severe shear strain ruptures the middle inverted limb, transforming the recumbent anticline into an allochthonous thrust sheet (nappe).
  • Practice Q3: What is an "isoclinal recumbent fold"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fold that has both mutually parallel limbs (interlimb angle $pprox 0^\circ$) AND a horizontal axial plane.
    Explanation: Represents the culmination of extreme compressive orogenic shearing where limbs are parallel and stacked horizontally.
  • Practice Q4: In a recumbent fold, where is the hinge zone located relative to the limbs?
    ✔ Correct Answer: At the frontal nose (apex) of the fold where the upper normal limb wraps around into the lower inverted limb.
    Explanation: The hinge line is horizontal and forms the nose of the horizontally projecting fold.
  • Practice Q5: What is a "fenster" (or window) in a recumbent nappe terrain?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An erosional hole or window cut through an overlying allochthonous nappe, exposing the younger, autochthonous footwall rocks beneath.
    Explanation: A classic structural feature in the Alps and Himalayas where deep river valleys erode through nappe sheets.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "klippe"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An isolated, erosional remnant of an allochthonous recumbent nappe perched atop autochthonous footwall strata.
    Explanation: The counterpart to a fenster; represents an isolated mountain summit of older thrust rocks surrounded by younger footwall rocks.
  • Practice Q7: Can a recumbent fold be refolded by a later tectonic deformation event?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Yes; superposed deformation produces Type 2 (crescent/mushroom) or Type 3 (convergent/divergent) Ramsay interference patterns.
    Explanation: In polyphase orogenic belts, early recumbent folds ($F_1$) with horizontal axial planes are commonly refolded by upright $F_2$ folds, arching the axial surface.
  • Practice Q8: What is the difference between an "overturned fold" and a "recumbent fold"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: In an overturned fold, the axial plane is inclined ($10^\circ - 60^\circ$); in a recumbent fold, the axial plane has become virtually horizontal ($0^\circ - 10^\circ$).
    Explanation: A recumbent fold represents the extreme end-member of overturning where limbs lie subhorizontal.
