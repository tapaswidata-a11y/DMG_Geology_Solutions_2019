1. Theoretical Foundation: The Spectrum of Diagenesis

Diagenesis (from Greek dia = through/across, and genesis = origin/creation) encompasses all physical, chemical, and biological processes that affect a sedimentary deposit after its initial deposition and through its progressive burial and lithification, up to the lowest boundary of metamorphism ($T \approx 200\text{--}250^\circ\text{C}, P \approx 1\text{--}2\text{ kbar}$):

Diagenetic Realm Primary Physical / Chemical Process Effect on Porosity & Permeability Diagnostic Mineral Transformations
Mechanical Compaction Overburden loading expels pore water; grains reorient, rotate, and fracture. Primary porosity drops drastically (e.g., from 70% in mud to 20% in shale; 40% to 25% in sand). Dewatering; ductile grains (clay clasts, micas) squeeze into pseudo-matrix.
Chemical Cementation Precipitation of authigenic minerals from supersaturated interstitial pore fluids. Pore throats occluded; loose grains bonded into rigid, consolidated rock (lithification). Calcite cement, quartz overgrowths, hematite coatings, authigenic clays (kaolinite, illite).
Pressure Solution (Chemical Compaction) Intergranular pressure increases solubility at grain contact points. Grains interlock forming sutured, concavo-convex, or stylolitic contacts. Stylolites in carbonates; silica dissolved from contacts reprecipitates as quartz cement.
Dissolution & Leaching Circulation of acidic or undersaturated pore waters dissolves unstable framework grains. Creates secondary porosity (critical for petroleum and groundwater reservoirs). Leaching of feldspars and carbonate fossils leaving moldic and vuggy pores.
"Diagenesis refers to all the physical and chemical changes that sediments undergo after deposition, prior to metamorphism. The two most important processes of diagenesis are compaction (the squeezing together of grains under overburden weight) and cementation (the binding of grains by precipitated mineral matter such as calcite, silica, or iron oxide). Together, compaction and cementation convert loose, unconsolidated sediment into solid rock."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Sedimentary Rocks, p. 130)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 96
Compaction and cementation of sediments occur during :
A Transportation
B High temperature and pressure medium
C High water pressure
D Diagenesis
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION D (Diagenesis)
OPTION D • CORRECT
Diagenesis

By unanimous sedimentological definition (Boggs, 2014; Pettijohn; Bangar, p. 130), compaction and cementation are the hallmark processes of diagenesis. They occur post-depositionally during burial at low to moderate temperatures and pressures, transforming loose sediments into solid, indurated sedimentary rocks.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Transportation

Transportation is the movement of loose sediments by water, wind, or ice. It causes abrasion, rounding, and sorting, but cannot cause compaction or cementation, which require static burial.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
High temperature and pressure medium

High temperature and high pressure ($T > 200\text{--}300^\circ\text{C}, P > 2\text{ kbar}$) define metamorphism (e.g., recrystallization of sandstone into quartzite or shale into schist), exceeding the realm of diagenesis.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
High water pressure

Excessive pore water pressure (overpressuring) actually prevents compaction by holding grains apart, leading to undercompaction and fluid-escape structures rather than normal lithification.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What are the three most common mineral cements in sandstones?
Answer: Calcite ($CaCO_3$), Silica ($SiO_2$, as syntaxial quartz overgrowths), and Iron Oxides (Hematite/Goethite).
Calcite and quartz are the dominant authigenic bonding agents, with hematite imparting classic red-bed coloration.
2. What optical continuity is displayed by syntaxial quartz overgrowth cement in sandstone thin sections?
Answer: The authigenic quartz overgrowth precipitates in optical continuity with the host quartz grain, extinguishing simultaneously.
A thin 'dust ring' of iron oxide or fluid inclusions typically marks the boundary between the original rounded grain and the new euhedral crystal facet.
3. What is a stylolite?
Answer: A serrated, irregular, suture-like surface of intergranular pressure dissolution lined with insoluble clay and organic residue.
Stylolites form predominantly in limestones during chemical compaction under high overburden stress.
4. What is the difference between eodiagenesis, mesodiagenesis, and telodiagenesis (Choquette & Pray, 1970)?
Answer: Eodiagenesis occurs near-surface; mesodiagenesis occurs during deep burial; telodiagenesis occurs upon tectonic uplift and meteoric unroofing.
This tripartite classification tracks sediments through early shallow deposition, deep thermal burial, and final exhumation.
5. What is secondary porosity?
Answer: Porosity generated post-depositionally by the chemical dissolution of mineral grains (e.g., feldspars, carbonate bioclasts) or fracturing.
Secondary dissolution porosity provides huge reservoirs for hydrocarbons in deeply buried sandstones and limestones.
6. Which clay mineral is typically converted to illite during deep burial diagenesis at temperatures above $100^\circ\text{C}$?
Answer: Smectite (Smectite-to-Illite transition)
The illitization of smectite releases substantial water, contributing to abnormal pore fluid overpressuring in deep shale basins.
7. How does mechanical compaction affect clay sediments differently from well-sorted quartz sands?
Answer: Clays compact enormously (up to 60-80% volume reduction), while rigid quartz sands lose only ~15-20% porosity prior to chemical compaction.
Platy clay flakes collapse readily under overburden, whereas quartz frameworks resist crushing until high pressures trigger pressure solution.
8. What organic maturation parameter is used to gauge the thermal boundary between diagenesis and metamorphism?
Answer: Vitrinite Reflectance ($R_o \approx 2.0\text{--}2.5\%$) and Conodont Alteration Index (CAI = 5)
Vitrinite reflectance measures organic carbonization; values beyond 2.5% indicate the transition into the anchizone (lowest metamorphic grade).
