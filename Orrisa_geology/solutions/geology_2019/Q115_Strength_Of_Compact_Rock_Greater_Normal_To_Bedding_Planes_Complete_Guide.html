1. Theoretical Foundation: Transverse Isotropy & Strength Anisotropy in Rocks

Stratified sedimentary rocks (sandstones, shales, limestones) and foliated metamorphic rocks (slates, phyllites, schists, gneisses) exhibit pronounced mechanical anisotropy (specifically transverse isotropy). Their compressive strength ($\sigma_c$) varies systematically with the orientation angle ($\beta$) between the major principal compressive stress ($\sigma_1$) and the bedding or foliation planes. In compact stratified rocks, the compressive strength is always greatest when the applied compressive stresses act normal (perpendicular, $\beta = 90^\circ$) to the bedding planes:

Stress Orientation Relative to Bedding Angle $\beta$ (Between $\sigma_1$ and Bedding Plane) Dominant Failure Mechanism Relative Compressive Strength ($\sigma_c$)
STRESSES NORMAL TO BEDDING PLANES (Option A) $\beta = 90^\circ$ (Perpendicular) Shear fracture must cut across intact mineral grains and all strong layers; bedding planes are pressed tightly shut MAXIMUM STRENGTH ($\sigma_{c,\max}$)
Stresses Parallel to Bedding Planes (Option B) $\beta = 0^\circ$ (Parallel) Axial tensile splitting along weak bedding interfaces and Euler buckling of thin individual laminae High-to-Intermediate ($\approx 0.70\text{--}0.85\,\sigma_{c,90^\circ}$), susceptible to splitting
Stresses Inclined at $45^\circ$ to Bedding (Option C) $\beta = 45^\circ$ High resolved shear stress ($\tau = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_1 \sin 2\beta$) directly along weak bedding planes triggers slip Very Low Strength (Near the trough of the U-curve)
Stresses Inclined at $60^\circ$ (or $30^\circ$ to $\sigma_1$) (Option D) $\beta \approx 30^\circ\text{--}45^\circ$ to $\sigma_1$ Critical Mohr-Coulomb slip angle ($\beta_{\text{crit}} = 45^\circ - \phi_j/2 \approx 30^\circ$): pure sliding along bedding ABSOLUTE MINIMUM STRENGTH ($\sigma_{c,\min}$)
"In stratified and laminated rocks, the compressive strength depends strongly on the direction of loading relative to the bedding planes. The strength of a compact rock is always greatest when the compressive stresses are applied normal (perpendicular) to the bedding planes, because the compressive load closes the bedding planes rather than splitting them apart, forcing any rupture surface to shear across the full thickness of the rock layers."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Standard Publishers, Chapter 13: Engineering Properties of Rocks, p. 287)
Jaeger's Single Plane of Weakness Theory (1960) & Anisotropy Index
$$\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = \frac{2\left(c_j + \sigma_3 \tan\phi_j\right)}{\left(1 - \tan\phi_j \cot\beta\right)\sin 2\beta}$$ $$\text{Minimum Strength Angle: } \beta_{\min} = 45^\circ - \frac{\phi_j}{2} \approx 25^\circ\text{--}35^\circ \quad \text{(to }\sigma_1\text{)}$$ $$\text{Strength Anisotropy Ratio: } R_c = \frac{\sigma_{c(90^\circ)}}{\sigma_{c(\min)}}$$

Here $c_j$ and $\phi_j$ are the cohesion and friction angle of the bedding plane. When $\sigma_1$ is applied normal to bedding ($\beta = 90^\circ$), $\sin 2\beta = 0$, making sliding along the bedding plane impossible ($\sigma_1 \to \infty$ for bedding slip), so failure is governed entirely by the much higher intact rock matrix strength ($c_m, \phi_m$).

2. Engineering Application: Placing Stones on Their "Natural Bed"
  • Masonry & Dam Pier Construction ("Natural Bed" Rule): In civil engineering and stone masonry, dressed sedimentary and metamorphic blocks (sandstone, limestone, gneiss) must always be laid with their bedding planes horizontal (i.e., normal to the vertical compressive gravity load).
  • Consequence of Face-Bedding ($\beta = 0^\circ$): If stone blocks are placed with bedding planes vertical and parallel to load ("face-bedded"), moisture penetration and axial compressive stress cause tensile delamination, buckling, and spalling of outer rock sheets.
  • Consequence of Oblique Bedding ($\beta = 30^\circ\text{--}60^\circ$): Maximum resolved shear stress acts along the bedding planes, leading to premature shear sliding at a fraction ($20\%\text{--}50\%$) of the intact rock strength.

Official OPSC 2019 Question & Forensic Option Analysis
Q115. When is the strength of a compact rock always greater ?
(A) When stresses are normal to bedding planes
(B) When stresses are parallel to bedding planes
(C) When stresses are inclined at 45° to bedding planes
(D) When stresses are inclined at 60° to bedding planes
✔ Verified Correct Answer: Option (A) — When stresses are normal to bedding planes
A When stresses are normal to bedding planes CORRECT ANSWER
Forensic Verification: When compressive stresses act normal (perpendicular, $90^\circ$) to the bedding planes, the normal stress across the bedding interfaces is maximized while resolved shear stress along the bedding planes is zero ($\tau = 0$). The bedding planes are compressed tightly together, preventing delamination or slip, and forcing the rock to fail only by shearing across the strong intact mineral grains. Hence, compressive strength is always greatest normal to bedding.
B When stresses are parallel to bedding planes INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: When compressive stress acts parallel to bedding planes ($\beta = 0^\circ$), lateral Poisson expansion induces tensile stress perpendicular to the bedding planes, causing premature axial splitting (cleavage delamination) and buckling of individual laminae.
C When stresses are inclined at 45° to bedding planes INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: At $45^\circ$ inclination, the resolved shear stress ($\tau = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_1 \sin 90^\circ = 0.5\,\sigma_1$) reaches its theoretical maximum along the weak bedding planes, causing easy shear sliding and very low compressive strength.
D When stresses are inclined at 60° to bedding planes INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: When the normal to the bedding plane is at $60^\circ$ (meaning $\sigma_1$ is inclined at $30^\circ$ to the bedding plane, $\beta = 45^\circ - \phi/2$), Mohr-Coulomb frictional sliding occurs along the bedding plane at the absolute lowest stress on Donath's U-curve.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What is meant by placing a building stone on its "natural bed" in masonry construction?
Answer: Laying the stone so that its original sedimentary bedding planes are horizontal and perpendicular (normal) to the vertical line of compressive load.
This maximizes compressive strength and prevents exfoliation or splitting of outer stone faces.
2. How should stones be placed in a masonry arch or cornice?
Answer: In an arch, bedding planes should be oriented radial (so that tangential arch thrust acts normal to the bedding planes).
Because the line of thrust in an arch follows the curve of the ring, radial bedding ensures compressive forces always act perpendicular to the bedding planes.
3. At what angle $\beta$ between $\sigma_1$ and a weak cleavage plane does a slate exhibit its minimum compressive strength?
Answer: At $\beta = 45^\circ - \phi_j/2$, which typically lies between $25^\circ$ and $35^\circ$ to the direction of $\sigma_1$.
Proven experimentally by F.A. Donath (1961) on Martinsburg slate and theoretically by J.C. Jaeger (1960).
4. If a laminated sandstone has $\sigma_{c(90^\circ)} = 90\text{ MPa}$ and $\sigma_{c(30^\circ)} = 30\text{ MPa}$, what is its Strength Anisotropy Ratio ($R_c$)?
Answer: $R_c = 90 / 30 = 3.0$ (Moderately Anisotropic).
$R_c = \sigma_{c(90^\circ)} / \sigma_{c(\min)} = 90 / 30 = 3.0$.
5. Why is compressive strength at $\beta = 90^\circ$ consistently higher than at $\beta = 0^\circ$?
Answer: At $\beta = 90^\circ$, failure requires shearing across all layers; at $\beta = 0^\circ$, layers fail at lower stress by tensile axial splitting and buckling of thin laminae.
Even without sliding along bedding, parallel loading ($\beta = 0^\circ$) suffers from column buckling of individual laminations.
6. What happens to the strength anisotropy of a foliated rock as confining pressure ($\sigma_3$) is increased to very high levels?
Answer: Strength anisotropy decreases as high confining pressure suppresses slip along weak planes and induces ductile flow.
Under high $\sigma_3$, frictional resistance ($\sigma_n \tan\phi_j$) across bedding increases markedly, reducing the depth of the U-curve trough.
7. In point load testing ($I_{s(50)}$) of anisotropic rock cores, how is the Point Load Strength Anisotropy Index ($I_{a(50)}$) defined?
Answer: The ratio of mean $I_{s(50)}$ measured perpendicular to planes of weakness (axial test) to mean $I_{s(50)}$ measured parallel to planes of weakness (diametral test).
$I_{a(50)} = I_{s(50)\perp} / I_{s(50)\parallel}$ according to ISRM suggested methods.
8. How does bedding dip affect foundation bearing capacity beneath a dam?
Answer: Horizontal beds (normal to vertical dam weight) provide the highest vertical bearing capacity, though gently upstream-dipping beds ($10^\circ\text{--}30^\circ$) are best for resisting resultant inclined thrust.
Resultant force of dam weight ($\downarrow W$) and horizontal reservoir water thrust ($\rightarrow P$) is inclined downstream, so beds dipping gently upstream meet the resultant force nearly at $90^\circ$ (normal to bedding!).
