Geomorphology & Overdeepening Dynamics of Glacial Fiords

A fiord (or fjord, from Old Norse fjörðr) is a long, narrow, steep-sided, deep marine coastal inlet or drowned valley produced by the glacial carving of a U-shaped trough below sea level, followed by post-glacial marine inundation. Fiords represent the most dramatic coastal erosional landforms created by glacial activity.

"When a valley glacier terminates at the sea coast, it excavates a deep, steep-sided U-shaped valley whose floor is overdeepened far below the current sea level. With the retreat of the glacier and post-glacial rise in sea level, the ocean waters drown these deep glacial troughs. Such drowned, steep-walled, deep marine glacial valleys are called fiords (characteristic of Norway, Greenland, Alaska, and New Zealand)." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Glacial Landforms & Marine Inundation, p. 31)

The morphology and oceanographic hydrodynamics of a fiord are governed by several diagnostic characteristics:

  • Glacial Overdeepening Below Sea Level: Unlike subaerial rivers, which cannot erode below sea-level base level, a valley glacier is a solid viscoelastic mass driven by upstream ice surface slopes and immense head pressure. An advancing glacier continues to scour, abrade, and pluck its bedrock bed until the ice floats in seawater (the flotation criterion: water depth $D_w = H_{\text{ice}} \times \frac{\rho_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}} \approx 0.895 H_{\text{ice}}$). Consequently, fiord floors frequently extend to extreme depths (e.g., Sognefjord in Norway is $1308\text{ m}$ deep, while the surrounding peaks rise over $1000\text{ m}$ above sea level).
  • Shallow Seaward Threshold (Sill / Rock Bar): At the mouth of the fiord where it enters the open ocean, the water depth abruptly shoals to a shallow bedrock ridge or terminal moraine deposit. This sill forms where the glacier's buoyancy reduced effective basal stress, halting bedrock erosion, and dumping terminal moraine debris.
  • Hanging Valleys & Waterfalls: Flanking tributary glaciers, possessing far less ice volume than the trunk glacier, eroded their tributary valleys less deeply. Following ice melt, these tributary valleys hang perched hundreds of meters above the fiord water surface, discharging dramatic hanging waterfalls.
  • U-Shaped Parabolic Cross-Section: Glacial erosion transforms narrow, pre-glacial fluvial V-shaped canyons into broad, steep-walled, flat-bottomed U-shaped troughs with flat valley floors.

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 6)
6. Fiords are formed due to :
(A) Lacustrine process
(B) Wind action
(C) Glacial activity  ✔
(D) Alluvial activity
✔
Correct Answer: (C) Glacial activity
In physical geology and coastal geomorphology (K.M. Bangar, p. 31; Arthur L. Bloom, p. 378; Arthur Holmes, p. 556), fiords are universally defined as drowned, overdeepened U-shaped troughs created by glacial activity.
(A) Lacustrine process Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Lacustrine processes pertain to inland freshwater or saline lakes (such as lake-bed varve deposition, delta sedimentation, and shoreline wave cut). While glacial lakes (tarns, paternoster lakes, ribbon lakes) form in glaciated valleys, marine-drowned coastal fiords are connected to the open ocean and carved by valley glaciers, not formed by lake processes.
(B) Wind action Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Wind action (aeolian geomorphology) is restricted to sand dune construction, deflation hollows, yardangs, and loess deposition in arid terrains. Air lacks the physical density and abrasive energy required to excavate massive, kilometer-deep bedrock trenches into coastal mountain ranges.
(C) Glacial activity Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: A fiord is unequivocally the geomorphic product of glacial activity. Massive valley glaciers (ice streams) scour and overdeepen bedrock troughs below sea level; following deglaciation and eustatic sea-level rise, ocean water drowns the U-shaped trough, creating a classic fiord with sheer rock walls, hanging tributary valleys, and a shallow seaward sill.
(D) Alluvial activity Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: Alluvial activity refers to stream erosion and deposition. When a river valley is submerged by sea-level rise, it produces a ria (a V-shaped, branching dendritic inlet that deepens continuously seaward without an overdeepened basin or bedrock sill). True fiords require glacial ice erosion.

Oceanographic & Stratigraphic Signatures of Glacial Fiords

Because the seaward threshold (sill) severely restricts deep-water exchange with the open ocean, the hydrodynamics of fiords lead to distinct environmental and sedimentological signatures:

  • Anoxic Bottom Water & Sapropelic Muds: The shallow sill prevents incoming dense ocean currents from easily flushing the deep basin floor. In poorly ventilated fiords, organic matter sinking from surface waters consumes all dissolved oxygen, producing stagnant, anoxic bottom conditions ($H_2S$ generation) that preserve unbioturbated, finely laminated organic-rich black muds (sapropels).
  • Varved Glaciomarine Sediments: Proglacial meltwater streams discharge rock flour (silt and clay) forming seasonal glaciomarine couplets (varves) that provide ultra-high-resolution paleoclimatic records.
  • Iceberg Dropstones: Calving tidewater glaciers release icebergs carrying erratic pebbles and boulders, which melt and drop into the finely laminated basin sediments, creating piercing dropstone deformation structures.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1 (Numerical): A valley glacier with an ice thickness $H_{\text{ice}} = 900\text{ m}$ and average density $\rho_{\text{ice}} = 900\text{ kg/m}^3$ advances into the sea (seawater density $\rho_{\text{sw}} = 1025\text{ kg/m}^3$). According to Archimedes' flotation principle, calculate the maximum water depth at which the glacier maintains contact with the bedrock floor before it floats, thereby setting the maximum theoretical limit of glacial bedrock overdeepening.
    ✔ Worked Answer: Water depth $D_w \approx 790.2\text{ meters}$.
    Derivation:
    Flotation occurs when the weight of displaced seawater equals the total weight of the ice column:
    $$\rho_{\text{sw}} \cdot g \cdot D_w = \rho_{\text{ice}} \cdot g \cdot H_{\text{ice}}$$ $$D_w = H_{\text{ice}} \times \frac{\rho_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text{sw}}} = 900\text{ m} \times \frac{900}{1025} \approx 790.24\text{ m}$$
    Therefore, as long as water depth is less than $790.2\text{ m}$, the glacier remains grounded, continuing to erode and overdeepen its bedrock bed.
  • Practice Q2: What is the name of the shallow bedrock or moraine barrier located at the seaward mouth of a fiord, and what is its hydrodynamic consequence?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The "Sill" (or threshold); it restricts oceanic deep-water exchange, often resulting in stagnant, anoxic basin bottom waters.
    Explanation: The sill acts as a submarine dam separating the open ocean from the overdeepened inner fiord basin, trapping dense bottom water and preventing vertical tidal mixing.
  • Practice Q3: What is the morphological difference between a "Fiord" and a "Ria"?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fiord is a drowned glacial U-shaped valley with a steep cross-section, overdeepened floor, and shallow sill; a ria is a drowned fluvial V-shaped valley deepening continuously seaward without a sill.
    Explanation: Rias (common in Galicia, Spain and southwest Ireland) are dendritic river systems submerged by post-glacial sea-level rise, whereas fiords are glaciated chasms.
  • Practice Q4: Why do hanging valleys frequently flank the walls of glacial fiords?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because smaller tributary glaciers had much less ice discharge and erosive power, carving their valleys to a much shallower depth than the massive trunk glacier.
    Explanation: Glacial downcutting scales with ice discharge and basal shear stress. The trunk glacier excavated hundreds of meters deeper than the tributary glaciers, leaving the tributary mouths perched high above the main valley floor.
  • Practice Q5: What is the world's deepest known fiord, and what is its maximum water depth?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Skelton Inlet in Antarctica ($>1900\text{ m}$ deep) or Sognefjord in Norway ($1308\text{ m}$ water depth).
    Explanation: Sognefjord in western Norway penetrates $205\text{ km}$ inland with sheer rock cliffs rising over $1000\text{ m}$ above the water surface and a water depth of $1308\text{ m}$ at its central basin.
  • Practice Q6: What is a "skerry guard" (skjærgård) associated with fiord coastlines?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An archipelago of thousands of low, rounded glaciated rocky islands, reefs, and skerries flanking the seaward entrance to fiords.
    Explanation: Formed where continental ice sheets smoothed and grooved low crystalline bedrock plains before reaching the open sea (classic in Norway and Sweden).
  • Practice Q7: In glaciomarine sedimentary cores, how is a sudden iceberg calving event identified?
    ✔ Correct Answer: By a layer of Ice-Rafted Debris (IRD) containing coarse, angular gravel dropstones embedded in fine-grained marine muds (e.g., Heinrich events).
    Explanation: Large icebergs floating over fiords and oceans drop frozen debris as they melt, punching through underlying laminated sediments.
  • Practice Q8: Why are modern fiords vulnerable to catastrophic tsunamis triggered by subaerial rockslides?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Because the near-vertical, glacially oversteepened rock walls are prone to debuttressing failure, and the narrow confined water body amplifies wave runup to extreme heights.
    Explanation: Glacial retreat removes lateral support (debuttressing). When rock masses collapse into the narrow, deep fiord chasm, water is displaced into mega-tsunamis (e.g., 1958 Lituya Bay megatsunami, Alaska: $524\text{ m}$ wave runup).
