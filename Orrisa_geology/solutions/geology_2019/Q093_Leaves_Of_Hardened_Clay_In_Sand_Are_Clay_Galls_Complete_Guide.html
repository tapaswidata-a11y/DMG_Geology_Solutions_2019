1. Theoretical Foundation: Genesis & Morphology of Clay Galls

In clastic sedimentology, clay galls (also termed mud galls, mud rip-up clasts, or intraformational mud chips) are flat, lenticular, or leaf-like structures of hardened, desiccated clay embedded within beds of sandstone or siltstone. They represent intraformational clasts produced through a distinctive cyclical sedimentary process:

Genesis Stage Physical / Environmental Action Sedimentary Feature Formed
1. Low-Water Deposition Quiescent settling in temporary pools, floodplains, lagoons, or tidal mudflats during low-water stage. Thin lamina of coherent, fine-grained plastic mud/clay.
2. Subaerial Desiccation Subaerial exposure to sun and atmospheric drying; clay contracts, forming polygonal mudcracks. Hardened, curled-up thin flakes resembling dried leaves (leaves of clay).
3. Turbulent Inundation Sudden high-energy flood surge (crevasse splay, storm wave, or flash flood) carrying abrasive sand. Rips up the dried clay flakes, rolls and rounds them slightly, and deposits them in sand.
4. Compaction & Lithification Overburden pressure flattens the soft mud clasts into disc-like or lenticular shapes within the sandstone matrix. ★ CLAY GALLS (Option D) ★ (often weathering out into hollow cavities on exposed rock faces).
"Leaves of hardened clay deposited in a sand or similar sediment as flat or lens-shaped structures are called clay galls (or mud galls). They are formed when mud layers dry out under the sun, crack into curled flakes, and are subsequently torn up and swept away by sudden torrents of sand-laden water. In weathered sandstone outcrops, clay galls easily wash out, leaving characteristic pitted or hollow depressions."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Sedimentary Structures, p. 134)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 93
The leaves of hardened clay deposited in a sand or similar sediment as flat or lense shaped structure are called :
A Clay flakes
B Clay balls
C Clay stones
D Clay galls
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION D (Clay galls)
OPTION D • CORRECT
Clay galls

By standard textbook definition (Pettijohn, 1975; Bangar, p. 134; G.B. Mahapatra), the flat or lens-shaped fragments of hardened, desiccated clay leaves enclosed within a sandstone matrix are formally known as clay galls (or mud galls). They are classic indicators of shallow-water, ephemeral, or subaerially exposed environments.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Clay flakes

'Clay flakes' refers to the uncompacted, loose microscopic phyllosilicate platelets or desiccated surface crust fragments before being transported and incorporated into an indurated sandstone as galls.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Clay balls

Clay balls (specifically armored mud balls) are spherical or subspherical balls of sticky mud rolled along a streambed that pick up an outer armour of pebbles and sand grains. They are spherical, not flat or lens-shaped.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Clay stones

Claystone is an indurated, massive, non-fissile sedimentary rock composed entirely of clay-sized particles, not a discrete lenticular inclusion within sandstone.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What type of conglomerate is formed when a current tears up semi-consolidated clay galls from its own bed and redeposits them locally?
Answer: Intraformational Conglomerate (or Intraformational Mud-Pebble Conglomerate)
Intraformational conglomerates consist of clasts derived from within the same depositional basin, virtually contemporaneous with the host sediment.
2. What happens to clay galls on exposed weathered sandstone cliffs?
Answer: They weather and wash out rapidly, leaving hollow depressions (pitted sandstone or 'tafoni-like' cavities).
Clay is much softer and more easily eroded by rainwater than the surrounding quartz sandstone, creating a pitted outcrop surface.
3. What environmental condition is conclusively demonstrated by the presence of clay galls in a fluvial sandstone?
Answer: Episodic drying (subaerial exposure) followed by high-energy turbulent flooding.
Clay galls require subaerial desiccation to harden the mud flakes so they do not immediately disaggregate into suspension when flooded.
4. What is an armored mud ball?
Answer: A chunk of sticky clay rolled along a torrential streambed that becomes coated with pebbles, gravel, and coarse sand.
Armored mud balls form in flash floods and mountain streams where cohesive mud rolls across pebble-covered bars.
5. Why do clay galls orient themselves parallel to the bedding planes?
Answer: Hydrodynamic settling and post-depositional lithostatic compaction flatten them horizontally along bedding.
Flat disc-shaped clasts settle with their maximum projection area parallel to flow and are further flattened during burial compaction.
6. What is the difference between an intraformational and an extraformational clast?
Answer: Intraformational clasts originate from within the depositional basin; extraformational clasts are eroded from outside source rocks.
Clay galls are intraformational; granitic or quartzite pebbles transported from mountains are extraformational.
7. What is another common sedimentological term for clay galls in turbidites?
Answer: Rip-up clasts (or shale rip-ups)
High-velocity turbidity currents erode the underlying muddy sea floor, incorporating shale rip-up clasts into the basal $T_a$ division.
8. Which Gondwana formation in India frequently displays clay galls in cross-bedded river sandstones?
Answer: The Barakar Formation (Damodar and Mahanadi Valley basins)
Braided channel Barakar sandstones commonly carry intraformational shale clasts and clay galls derived from cannibalized overbank muds.
