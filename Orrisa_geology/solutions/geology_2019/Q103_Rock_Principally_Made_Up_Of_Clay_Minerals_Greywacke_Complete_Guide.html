1. Theoretical Foundation: Clay Minerals in Sedimentary Rocks

In sedimentary petrology, rocks containing significant proportions of clay minerals (hydrous aluminum phyllosilicates such as illite, smectite/montmorillonite, kaolinite, and chlorite) are classified based on their grain size and matrix abundance. Among the four options provided in Question 103 (Arkose, Siltstone, Greywacke, and Dolostone), a detailed comparative analysis reveals their mineralogical constitution:

Rock Name Grain Size Classification Primary Framework Grains Clay Minerals & Matrix Presence Petrological Evaluation
GREYWACKE (Wacke) (Option C) Sandstone ($2\text{ mm} - 1/16\text{ mm}$) Angular quartz, feldspar, rock fragments Abundant detrital & authigenic matrix (> 15% to 50%) composed principally of CLAY MINERALS, chlorite, and sericite. The defining feature that distinguishes greywacke from normal sandstones is its pervasive clay-mineral matrix.
Siltstone (Option B) Silt grade ($1/16 - 1/256\text{ mm}$) Micro-quartz ($SiO_2$) & feldspar silt particles Minor clay matrix between quartz silt grains; may grade into mudstone. Composed primarily of quartz silt grains, not clay minerals (pure clay rock is shale/claystone).
Arkose (Option A) Sandstone ($2\text{ mm} - 1/16\text{ mm}$) Feldspar (>25%) + Quartz Negligible matrix (<15%); clean arenite. Composed principally of feldspar and quartz, virtually free of clay.
Dolostone (Option D) Chemical / Carbonate rock Dolomite mineral crystals $[CaMg(CO_3)_2]$ Trace insoluble residue only. A magnesium carbonate rock; totally devoid of detrital clay minerals.
"Greywacke is a dark, tough, poorly-sorted sandstone consisting of angular grains of quartz, feldspar, and small rock fragments embedded in an abundant, fine-grained matrix (>15%). This matrix is principally made up of clay minerals, chlorite, sericite, and fine silica paste, giving the rock its characteristic dark greenish-grey color and muddy appearance ('dirty sandstone')."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Sedimentary Rocks, p. 136)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 103
Which of the following rocks is principally made up of clay minerals ?
A Arkose
B Siltstone
C Greywacke
D Dolostone
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION C (Greywacke) [Exam Context Analysis & Full Forensic Breakdown]
OPTION C • EXAM TARGET (BEST FIT AMONG CHOICES)
Greywacke

Among the four options provided, Greywacke is the rock universally defined in petrology textbooks (Bangar, p. 136; P.K. Mukherjee, p. 251) by its defining abundance of a fine-grained detrital matrix (15% to 50%) that is principally made up of clay minerals, chlorite, and sericite. While pure argillaceous rocks (shale/claystone) are absent from the options, Greywacke is the intended answer because the presence of this dominant clay matrix is its primary petrographic diagnostic criterion.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Arkose

Arkose is a clean arenite sandstone containing more than 25% feldspar. Its matrix is strictly less than 15% (typically <5%), making it deficient in clay minerals.

OPTION B • DISTRACTOR
Siltstone

Siltstone is defined granulometrically by silt-sized particles (1/16 mm to 1/256 mm). Mineralogically, detrital silt is dominated by micro-crystalline quartz and feldspar, NOT clay minerals. Clay-grade minerals ($< 1/256\text{ mm}$) define mudstone, claystone, and shale.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Dolostone

Dolostone is a non-clastic, chemical carbonate rock composed almost entirely of the carbonate mineral dolomite $[CaMg(CO_3)_2]$. It is completely devoid of silicate clay minerals.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. What rock is the 100% pure argillaceous equivalent composed almost exclusively of clay minerals?
Answer: Claystone (non-fissile) or Shale (fissile).
Claystones and shales consist of >66% clay-grade phyllosilicate minerals ($< 1/256\text{ mm}$).
2. What are the three primary clay mineral groups found in sedimentary rocks?
Answer: Kaolinite, Illite, and Smectite (Montmorillonite) groups.
Kaolinite reflects humid tropical leaching; illite reflects temperate/alkaline conditions; smectite reflects weathered volcanic ash.
3. What is the matrix threshold that separates arenites from wackes (greywackes) in modern sandstone classifications?
Answer: 15% matrix (Dott, 1964; Pettijohn, 1975)
Sandstones with <15% fine matrix are arenites; sandstones with 15% to 50% clay-silt matrix are wackes (greywackes).
4. What process generates 'pseudomatrix' in greywackes during burial diagenesis?
Answer: The mechanical flattening and squashing of soft, ductile shale/mud lithic clasts between rigid quartz grains.
Dickinson (1970) showed that deeply buried lithic grains deform into a pseudo-clay matrix resembling primary depositional mud.
5. Which sedimentary rock displays fissility (the tendency to split along thin, parallel laminar planes)?
Answer: Shale
Fissility is caused by the parallel preferred alignment of platy clay mineral flakes during mechanical compaction.
6. What is bentonite?
Answer: A highly plastic clay rock composed predominantly of smectite (montmorillonite) formed by the alteration of volcanic ash in water.
Bentonite swells dramatically in water and is widely used in drilling muds and civil engineering sealants.
7. Why is siltstone mineralogically dominated by quartz rather than clay minerals?
Answer: Quartz grains break down mechanically to silt size (the 'terminal quartz limit' of ~20 to 4 $\mu\text{m}$), whereas clay minerals form by chemical weathering.
Abrasion can crush quartz down to silt size, but cannot easily produce clay-grade quartz, so silt is mechanically stable quartz debris.
8. What is a 'marl'?
Answer: An intimate mixture of clay minerals and calcium carbonate (typically 35% to 65% carbonate and 35% to 65% clay).
Marls represent transitional deposits between pure pelagic carbonate limestones and terrigenous clay muds.
