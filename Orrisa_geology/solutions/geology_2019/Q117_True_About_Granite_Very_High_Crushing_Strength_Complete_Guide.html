1. Theoretical Foundation: Petrological & Engineering Properties of Granite

Granite is an acid, plutonic (intrusive) igneous rock composed essentially of quartz ($20\%\text{--}40\%$), alkali feldspar (orthoclase/microcline), and sodic plagioclase, with minor biotite or hornblende. Because it crystallizes slowly at depth under high confining pressure, its mineral grains develop a tight, holocrystalline, equigranular interlocking (hypidiomorphic granular) texture. This microstructure gives granite very high crushing (compressive) strength, extremely low porosity, superior durability, and the ability to take a mirror-like polish:

"Granite is one of the strongest, hardest, and most durable of all building stones. Owing to its holocrystalline, interlocking granular texture and the predominance of hard minerals like quartz and feldspar, it possesses a very high crushing strength ranging from $1000\text{ to }2500\text{ kg/cm}^2$ ($100\text{--}250\text{ MPa}$). Its porosity is less than $1\%$, it weathers extremely slowly, and it takes an excellent, lasting polish, making it ideal for bridge piers, dam foundations, heavy masonry, and decorative monumental work."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Standard Publishers, Chapter 14: Building Stones, p. 296)

Fine-grained granites display even higher crushing strength ($180\text{--}250\text{ MPa}$) than coarse-grained pegmatitic varieties ($100\text{--}150\text{ MPa}$) in accordance with the Hall-Petch grain-size strengthening relationship ($\sigma_c \propto d^{-1/2}$).

2. Engineering Applications & One Notable Limitation of Granite
  • Heavy Load-Bearing Structures: Bridge piers, abutments, lighthouses, sea walls, dockyards, and dam foundations where immense crushing loads and hydraulic abrasion act continuously.
  • Dimension & Monumental Facing Stone: Because feldspar and quartz take a brilliant, high-gloss polish and resist acid rain far better than marble or limestone, polished granite slabs are the premier architectural cladding material.
  • Fire Resistance Limitation: The one engineering weakness of granite is poor fire resistance. Under intense fire ($> 573^\circ\text{C}$, the $\alpha \to \beta$ quartz inversion point), quartz undergoes a sudden $0.85\%$ volumetric expansion and differential thermal expansion relative to feldspar, causing granite blocks to crack, spall, and decrepitate when doused with water.

Official OPSC 2019 Question & Forensic Option Analysis
Q117. Which of the following is true about granite ?
(A) It has very high crushing strength
(B) It takes poor polish
(C) It is a highly porous rock
(D) It weathers out faster
✔ Verified Correct Answer: Option (A) — It has very high crushing strength
A It has very high crushing strength CORRECT ANSWER
Forensic Verification: Granite possesses a very high crushing (unconfined compressive) strength of $1000\text{ to }2500\text{ kg/cm}^2$ ($100\text{--}250\text{ MPa}$). Its interlocking holocrystalline fabric of hard quartz ($H=7$) and feldspar ($H=6$) crystals resists intergranular slip and compressive rupture better than almost any sedimentary rock.
B It takes poor polish INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: Granite takes an excellent, mirror-like, long-lasting polish because of its dense, non-porous interlocking crystals of hard quartz and feldspar. That is why polished granite is used worldwide for countertops, monuments, and building facades.
C It is a highly porous rock INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: Fresh granite is an extremely compact, non-porous rock with total porosity typically under $0.5\%\text{ to }1\%$ and water absorption below $0.5\%$.
D It weathers out faster INCORRECT
Why this is wrong: Granite is rich in quartz (the most stable mineral on Goldich's weathering stability series) and K-feldspar, and has negligible porosity to admit water. Therefore, it weathers very slowly and exhibits outstanding durability.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. According to Indian Standards (IS: 1121), what is the minimum crushing strength required for a stone to be classified as a good structural building stone?
Answer: At least $1000\text{ kg/cm}^2$ ($\approx 100\text{ MPa}$) for heavy-duty masonry; granite easily exceeds this ($1000\text{--}2500\text{ kg/cm}^2$).
Granite, basalt, quartzite, and charnockite all surpass the $1000\text{ kg/cm}^2$ benchmark.
2. Why do fine-grained granites possess higher crushing strength than coarse-grained (porphyritic or pegmatitic) granites?
Answer: Smaller grain size restricts the initial length of Griffith microcracks along grain boundaries and cleavage planes ($\sigma_c \propto d^{-1/2}$).
Fine, equigranular interlocking crystals distribute stress more uniformly and arrest microcrack propagation.
3. Why does granite have poor fire resistance despite its high crushing strength?
Answer: Quartz undergoes sudden volumetric expansion at $573^\circ\text{C}$ ($\alpha\text{-quartz} \to \beta\text{-quartz}$), and unequal thermal expansion of quartz and feldspar shatters the rock.
Compact sandstone (with siliceous cement) has much better fire resistance than granite.
4. What should be the maximum permissible water absorption (by weight after 24 hours immersion) for a first-class building stone like granite?
Answer: Less than $0.5\%\text{ to }1\%$ for granite (and strictly never exceeding $5\%$ for any acceptable building stone).
Low water absorption guarantees immunity against freeze-thaw frost wedging and salt crystallization damage.
5. Why does polished granite retain its exterior gloss in urban atmospheres much longer than polished marble?
Answer: Granite is composed of silicate minerals (quartz and feldspar, $H=6\text{--}7$) immune to weak acids, whereas marble is calcite ($\text{CaCO}_3, H=3$) which is etched by acidic rain ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$).
Silicate minerals resist both wind-blown quartz dust abrasion and urban acid rain dissolution.
6. What causes dark brown or rusty stains on some inferior granite building blocks over time?
Answer: Oxidation and hydration of accessory iron-bearing minerals such as pyrite ($\text{FeS}_2$) or abundant biotite/magnetite into limonite.
Dimension granites must be free of pyrite nodules to prevent unsightly rust staining and sulphuric acid release.
7. What is the typical specific gravity of granite and what does a lower value ($< 2.55$) indicate?
Answer: Fresh granite has $G = 2.63\text{--}2.75$; a lower value indicates incipient kaolinization (weathering of feldspars) and micro-fracturing.
Weathering alters dense feldspar ($G \approx 2.6$) into porous kaolinite clay ($G \approx 2.2\text{--}2.4$), reducing bulk density and strength.
8. How are Rift and Grain utilized when quarrying massive granite?
Answer: Rift (easiest splitting plane) and Grain (second easiest plane, perpendicular to rift) are microscopic fluid-inclusion microcrack alignments used by quarrymen to split rectangular granite blocks cleanly.
Even though granite lacks sedimentary bedding, orthogonal rift, grain, and hardway planes allow clean dressing of blocks.
