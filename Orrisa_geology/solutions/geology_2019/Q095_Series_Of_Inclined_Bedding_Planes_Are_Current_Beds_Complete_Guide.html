1. Theoretical Foundation: Mechanics of Current Bedding (Cross-Stratification)

Current bedding (also known as cross-bedding, cross-stratification, or false bedding) is one of the most fundamental primary depositional structures in sedimentary geology. It is defined as a series of inclined internal laminations (foresets) deposited at an angle to the main horizontal or master bedding planes, produced by the migration of ripples, sand waves, or dunes under moving water or wind currents:

Structural Element Morphology & Geometric Attitude Hydrodynamic Mechanism Geological / Geopetal Significance
Topset Beds Gently dipping or sub-horizontal upper strata; frequently truncated by erosion. Transport of bedload across the stoss side of migrating dunes. Top truncation indicates 'younging' / stratigraphic top (geopetal indicator).
CURRENT BEDS (Foresets) (Option B) Inclined planes dipping at angles up to 30–35° (angle of repose) in the direction of current flow. Avalanching and grainfall down the steep lee side of migrating sand dunes or ripples. Dip direction provides the definitive palaeocurrent flow vector.
Bottomset Beds Sub-horizontal lower strata meeting the base tangentially (asymptotic contact). Suspension settling of finer grains in the flow separation zone at the dune toe. Tangential curved base proves the right-side-up (normal) stratigraphic orientation.
"When a series of inclined bedding planes show an angular relationship to the main bedding planes and to the direction of current flow, they are termed current beds (or cross-beds). Formed by the migration of sand waves, dunes, or ripples in flowing water or wind, the inclined foresets dip down-current in the direction of flow. Current bedding is invaluable for determining palaeocurrent directions and identifying overturned beds."
— K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Sedimentary Structures, p. 132)

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 95
If a series of inclined bedding planes showing some angular relationship to the direction of current flow, then they are generally called as :
A Gradient beds
B Current beds
C Dipping beds
D Convolute beds
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (Current beds)
OPTION B • CORRECT
Current beds

By classical geological terminology (Bangar, p. 132; Mahapatra, p. 182; Pettijohn), inclined bedding planes formed within a larger sedimentary stratum at an angle to the current flow and the main horizontal bedding are formally termed current beds (or cross-beds / false beds).

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
Gradient beds

'Gradient beds' is not a recognized geological term. The vertical variation in grain size is termed graded bedding, which consists of horizontal beds, not inclined cross-strata.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
Dipping beds

'Dipping beds' is a general structural geology term for any tilted rock stratum inclined to the horizontal plane due to tectonic deformation (tilting or folding), not a primary sedimentary current-formed structure.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
Convolute beds

Convolute bedding consists of intensely crumpled, folded, or deformed laminations formed by soft-sediment syn-sedimentary deformation, not regular inclined current beds.

High-Yield Practice Problems (8 Exam-Standard Questions)
1. How can cross-bedding be used as a 'way-up' (geopetal) structure to identify overturned strata?
Answer: Foreset beds are truncated sharply at the top by erosion and curve tangentially (asymptotically) into the bottomset at the base.
If the tangential curves are at the top and the sharp truncations are at the bottom, the bed has been tectonically overturned.
2. What type of cross-bedding is characterized by alternating sets that dip in opposite directions (180° apart)?
Answer: Herringbone cross-bedding
Herringbone structure forms in intertidal environments where flood currents and ebb currents reverse flow directions every 6 hours.
3. What is the maximum dip angle (angle of repose) for subaqueous current beds?
Answer: Approximately $30^\circ$ to $34^\circ$
Avalanching of sand grains down the lee slope cannot exceed the angle of internal friction of submerged sand ($~30-34^\circ$).
4. What is the term for giant cross-bedding sets exceeding 5 to 20 meters in thickness?
Answer: Aeolian Draa / Dune cross-stratification (e.g., Navajo Sandstone, USA)
Desert sand dunes produce colossal cross-bedding sets, far thicker than subaqueous river dunes.
5. What sedimentary structure forms when storm-induced oscillatory waves scour and deposit gently undulating mounds of fine sand?
Answer: Hummocky Cross-Stratification (HCS)
HCS is the diagnostic signature of storm-dominated shelf deposition below fairweather wave base.
6. In a river channel, which direction do the inclined current beds dip?
Answer: Downstream (in the down-current direction of stream flow).
Sediment grains migrate up the stoss side and tumble down the downstream-facing lee face.
7. What is flaser bedding?
Answer: A cross-bedded sand structure in which mud streaks or lenses are preserved in the ripple troughs.
Flaser bedding reflects fluctuating energy in tidal flats: sand ripples migrate during currents, and mud settles in troughs during slack water.
8. What is the difference between planar cross-bedding and trough cross-bedding?
Answer: Planar has flat bounding surfaces; trough cross-bedding has scoop-shaped, curved erosional bounding surfaces.
Planar cross-bedding forms from 2D straight dunes; trough cross-bedding forms from 3D curved/lunate dunes.
