Geomorphological Mechanics of Dendritic Drainage Networks

A dendritic drainage pattern (derived from the Greek dendron, meaning tree) is an irregular, branching, tree-like stream network where tributaries join larger channels at acute angles ($<90^\circ$). It is universally recognized as the diagnostic geomorphic signature of a geological region that is completely lacking in structural control.

"A dendritic drainage pattern is characterized by irregular branching of tributaries in all directions, resembling the branches of an oak or apple tree. It develops in regions where the underlying rocks are completely homogeneous, flat-lying, or massive, and exert no structural control on the direction of stream erosion (such as horizontal sedimentary beds, thick plateau basalts, or massive granitic plutons)." — K.M. Bangar, Principles of Engineering Geology (Drainage Patterns & Structural Geology, p. 27)

The development of a dendritic network is governed by three primary geological conditions:

  • Uniform Substrate Erodibility (Isotropy): The rock or sediment exhibits equal resistance to fluvial erosion in all spatial directions. There are no alternating belts of hard and soft rock, no dipping beds, and no pervasive oriented joint systems to steer channel paths.
  • Headward Tributary Bifurcation: Streams extend their drainage basins upstream by headward erosion along path lines of maximum local hydraulic gradient. Because the terrain is structurally isotropic, tributary branching occurs randomly and naturally at acute angles ($30^\circ - 60^\circ$), maximizing drainage efficiency.
  • Typical Geological Settings:
    • Horizontally bedded sedimentary rocks (e.g., flat-lying plateau sandstones, shales, and limestones).
    • Massive, isotropic igneous plutons (e.g., unjointed granite batholiths).
    • Uniform unconsolidated sediment sheets (e.g., thick glacial till plains, alluvial plains, coastal plains).
    • Flood basalt plateaus with uniform horizontal layering (e.g., Deccan Traps).

Official Question Statement — OPSC Assistant Geologist 2019 Paper-II (Question 15)
15. If a region is lacking in structural control, then the drainage pattern in such regions will be :
(A) Dendritic  ✔
(B) Trellis
(C) Rectangular
(D) Radial
✔
Correct Answer: (A) Dendritic
In photogeology, structural geomorphology, and remote sensing (K.M. Bangar, p. 27; Arthur L. Bloom, p. 272; Arthur N. Strahler, 1957; W.D. Thornbury, p. 120), a region that is lacking in structural control universally develops a Dendritic drainage pattern.
(A) Dendritic Correct Verified Option
Why this option is correct: A dendritic drainage pattern is the defining diagnostic hallmark of terrain lacking structural control. In isotropic, flat-lying, or massive rocks with uniform resistance to erosion, stream channels branch randomly and organically like tree limbs, joining at acute angles.
(B) Trellis Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A trellis pattern exhibits extreme structural control, developing specifically in folded mountain belts with alternating resistant and non-resistant strata where streams follow parallel strike valleys and dip slopes at right angles.
(C) Rectangular Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A rectangular pattern develops under intense structural control dictated by intersecting sets of orthogonal bedrock joints or fault systems, causing streams to make sharp $90^\circ$ bends.
(D) Radial Incorrect Distractor
Why this distractor is incorrect: A radial pattern reflects strong geomorphic and structural control dictated by an isolated volcanic cone or structural dome from which streams radiate outward in all directions.

Topological Properties of Dendritic Networks: Shreve & Strahler Analysis

In quantitative morphometry (Shreve, 1966; Strahler, 1957), topologically random channel networks (where channels develop without structural bias) inevitably produce dendritic topology:

  • Bifurcation Ratio ($R_b$): In true dendritic networks, Horton's bifurcation ratio ($R_b = N_u / N_{u+1}$) falls consistently within the narrow theoretical range of $3.0\text{ to } 5.0$. Significant deviation ($R_b > 5$ or $<3$) indicates structural distortion (e.g., fault elongation).
  • Junction Angles: Tributaries join master channels at acute angles governed by the ratio of stream gradients (Howard, 1971): $\cos \theta = S_m / S_t$, where $S_m$ is the main stream slope and $S_t$ is the tributary slope.
High-Yield Practice Questions & Worked Derivations (8 Problems)
  • Practice Q1: What is a "pinnate" drainage pattern, and how does it relate to dendritic drainage?
    ✔ Correct Answer: A fine-textured, feather-like modification of dendritic drainage where closely spaced parallel tributaries enter a main channel at acute angles, diagnostic of easily erodible, uniform loess or fine silt deposits.
    Explanation: In thick loess blankets (e.g., Loess Plateau in China), extreme rill erodibility in uniform sediment produces delicate feather-like (pinnate) dendritic networks.
  • Practice Q2: What does an "angular" or "sub-dendritic" drainage pattern indicate on aerial photographs?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Moderate structural control where a generally dendritic network is partially modified by secondary joint sets or gentle regional dips.
    Explanation: When a terrain has weak or widely spaced joints, the overall tree-like pattern persists, but individual tributary segments straighten along joint trends.
  • Practice Q3: In what rock formation of India is the classic dendritic drainage pattern most spectacularly developed?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The horizontal basalt flows of the Deccan Traps (Maharashtra/Madhya Pradesh) and flat-lying Vindhyan sandstones.
    Explanation: The flat-lying, homogeneous basalt sheets offer uniform erodibility in all directions, developing perfect dendritic drainage across thousands of square kilometers.
  • Practice Q4: In Horton's morphometric laws, what does a constant Bifurcation Ratio ($R_b \approx 3.5$) across all stream orders signify?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Complete geological homogeneity and lack of structural control throughout the entire drainage basin.
    Explanation: If a fault or fold belt intersected part of the basin, the bifurcation ratio for that specific order would jump anomalously. Constant $R_b$ proves isotropic dendritic development.
  • Practice Q5: What is a "centripetal" drainage pattern, and how does its symmetry contrast with radial drainage?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Streams converge inward toward a central topographic depression (e.g., a volcanic caldera, desert playa, or sinkhole), the exact reverse of outward-radiating radial drainage.
    Explanation: Inward drainage terminating in an ephemeral lake or ponor characterizes closed structural or volcanic basins.
  • Practice Q6: What happens to a dendritic drainage network if regional tectonic tilting occurs after its establishment?
    ✔ Correct Answer: The network becomes asymmetric; tributaries on one side of the trunk stream elongate while tributaries on the opposite side are shortened and steepened.
    Explanation: Tectonic tilting modifies local hydraulic gradients, favoring preferential tributary growth down the tilt direction.
  • Practice Q7: What is a "deranged" (or contorted) drainage pattern, and what geologic process produces it?
    ✔ Correct Answer: An unorganized, chaotic network of irregular lakes, swamps, and short meandering channels produced by recent continental glaciation (e.g., the Canadian Shield).
    Explanation: Glacial scour and moraine dumping completely obliterate pre-existing drainage systems, leaving a juvenile, disorganized landscape that has not had time to achieve equilibrium.
  • Practice Q8: Why do tributaries in a dendritic pattern typically join at acute angles ($<90^\circ$) rather than right angles?
    ✔ Correct Answer: Fluid energy minimization; acute junction angles minimize turbulence and energy loss at the confluence.
    Explanation: Streams naturally seek the path of least work (Playfair's Law and Leopold's minimum work hypothesis), aligning tributary junctions smoothly downstream.
