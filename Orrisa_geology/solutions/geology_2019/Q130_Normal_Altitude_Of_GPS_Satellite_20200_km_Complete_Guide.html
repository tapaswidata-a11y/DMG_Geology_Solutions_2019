1. Theoretical Foundation: GPS Space Segment Orbital Architecture

The Global Positioning System (GPS, formally NAVSTAR GPS) consists of three interconnected segments: the Space Segment (satellite constellation), the Control Segment (master control stations, tracking stations, and ground antennas), and the User Segment (GPS receivers).

The baseline nominal constellation of the GPS Space Segment is designed as a Walker $24/6/1$ constellation (24 operational satellites distributed evenly across 6 orbital planes inclined at $55^\circ$ to the equator, with 4 satellites per plane plus on-orbit spares). According to Kepler's Third Law, the orbital period ($T$) of a satellite depends strictly on the semi-major axis ($a$) of its orbit:

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{G M_E}}$$

where $G M_E = 3.986004418 \times 10^{14}\text{ m}^3\text{s}^{-2}$ is the Earth's standard gravitational parameter. GPS satellites are engineered into a semi-synchronous circular orbit having an orbital period of exactly half a sidereal day (11 hours, 58 minutes, or $43,080\text{ seconds}$):

$$a = \left[G M_E \left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^2\right]^{1/3} \approx 26,560\text{ km}$$

Subtracting the mean equatorial radius of the Earth ($R_E \approx 6,378\text{ km}$), we obtain the nominal orbital altitude ($h$) above the Earth's surface:

$$h = a - R_E = 26,560\text{ km} - 6,378\text{ km} \approx \mathbf{20,182\text{ km}} \approx \mathbf{20,200\text{ km}}$$

2. Official Examination Question & Forensic Option Analysis
QUESTION 130
The normal altitude of GPS satellite is about:
A 16,200 km
B 20,200 km
C 24,400 km
D 36,100 km
CORRECT ANSWER: OPTION B (20,200 km)
OPTION B • CORRECT
20,200 km

By international GPS geodetic specification (ICD-GPS-200; Hofmann-Wellenhof et al.; Bangar, p. 212), the semi-major axis of a GPS satellite orbit is $a = 26,560\text{ km}$. Subtracting the Earth's radius ($R_E \approx 6,378\text{ km}$) yields the nominal orbital altitude of $\approx 20,200\text{ km}$ (or $20,182\text{ km}$ exact). This semi-synchronous orbit delivers a repeat track period of 12 sidereal hours.

OPTION A • DISTRACTOR
16,200 km

Incorrect altitude. An altitude of $16,200\text{ km}$ would produce an orbital period of $\approx 8.8\text{ hours}$, which is not resonant with Earth's rotation and would destroy the repeating ground-track geometry of the GPS constellation.

OPTION C • DISTRACTOR
24,400 km

Incorrect altitude. While closer to the European Galileo constellation ($23,222\text{ km}$), it does not correspond to the United States GPS constellation altitude.

OPTION D • DISTRACTOR
36,100 km

This approximates the altitude of a Geostationary / Geosynchronous orbit ($h \approx 35,786\text{ km}$), typical of communication and meteorological satellites (e.g. INSAT, Meteosat, GOES) and SBAS augmentation payloads (GAGAN, WAAS), but not the core MEO GPS satellites.

1. Why do GPS satellite ground tracks repeat approximately 4 minutes earlier each solar day?
Answer: Because the GPS orbital period is half a sidereal day ($11\text{ hr } 58\text{ min}$); two full orbits take 1 sidereal day ($23\text{ hr } 56\text{ min } 4\text{ sec}$), which is $\approx 3\text{ min } 56\text{ sec}$ shorter than a mean solar day ($24\text{ hours}$).
A surveyor observing satellite geometry at 10:00 AM on Monday will see the identical constellation geometry at 09:56 AM on Tuesday.
2. Why are GPS satellites placed at $20,200\text{ km}$ altitude rather than in Low Earth Orbit (LEO, $800\text{ km}$)?
Answer: At $20,200\text{ km}$, each satellite has a vast footprint covering over $38\%$ of Earth's surface, requiring only 24 satellites for global continuous coverage (compared to hundreds needed in LEO).
Higher altitude also avoids atmospheric drag and provides stable, precisely predictable Keplerian orbits.
3. What is the minimum number of GPS satellites that a receiver must track simultaneously to determine a unique 3D position and receiver clock offset?
Answer: Exactly 4 satellites.
Four pseudorange equations are mathematically required to solve for the four unknowns: $X, Y, Z$ coordinates and receiver clock bias $\Delta t_r$.
4. How does the orbital altitude of $20,200\text{ km}$ impact the signal travel time from a GPS satellite to a ground receiver?
Answer: The one-way travel time of the radio wave ($t = d/c$) ranges from approximately $0.067\text{ seconds}$ (at zenith) to $0.086\text{ seconds}$ (at the horizon).
$$\frac{20,200\text{ km}}{300,000\text{ km/s}} \approx 0.0673\text{ s}$$.
5. What relativistic effect must be corrected because of the GPS orbital altitude of $20,200\text{ km}$?
Answer: General relativistic gravitational blueshift: clocks at $20,200\text{ km}$ tick faster than ground clocks by $+45.9\text{ }\mu\text{s/day}$, while special relativistic velocity slows them by $-7.2\text{ }\mu\text{s/day}$ (net $+38.7\text{ }\mu\text{s/day}$).
Satellite atomic clocks are intentionally pre-set to run slower at $10.22999999543\text{ MHz}$ instead of $10.23\text{ MHz}$ so they match ground time exactly.
6. What is Dilution of Precision (DOP) in GPS positioning?
Answer: A dimensionless multiplier that expresses how the geometric distribution of tracked satellites magnifies ranging measurement errors into final position errors ($\sigma_{\text{pos}} = \text{GDOP} \cdot \sigma_0$).
Low DOP values ($<2$) indicate excellent geometry with satellites widely spaced across the sky.
7. What is the name of India's regional satellite navigation system and how do its satellite altitudes differ from GPS?
Answer: NavIC (IRNSS); it operates at geostationary and inclined geosynchronous altitudes ($\approx 36,000\text{ km}$), whereas GPS operates at $20,200\text{ km}$ MEO.
NavIC's higher orbit focuses continuous coverage over India and the surrounding $1500\text{ km}$ region with only 7 satellites.
8. What is the difference between Pseudorange (code phase) and Carrier Phase measurements in GPS?
Answer: Pseudorange measures transit time using PRN chipping codes (meter-scale accuracy); carrier phase tracks the cycles of the RF carrier wave ($\lambda \approx 19\text{ cm}$, achieving millimeter-scale geodetic accuracy).
Carrier phase tracking requires resolving the integer cycle ambiguity ($N$).
