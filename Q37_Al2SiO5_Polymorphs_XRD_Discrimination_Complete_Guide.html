DGMS 2019 Geology • Question 37

Analytical Mineralogy: Distinguishing Al₂SiO₅ Polymorphs via XRD

Topic: Mineral Analysis, Crystallography & Geochemistry | Primary Reference: Dexter Perkins (Mineralogy, 3rd ed., Ch. 12)

📝 Question Statement

Q.37. Which one of the following instruments can be used to distinguish kyanite, sillimanite and andalusite?

(a) EPMA
(b) XRD
(c) XRF
(d) ICP-MS
a EPMA (Electron Probe Microanalyzer)
b XRD (X-Ray Diffraction) [Correct Answer]
c XRF (X-Ray Fluorescence)
d ICP-MS (Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry)
✓ Correct Answer: (b) XRD [Because kyanite, sillimanite, and andalusite are polymorphs with identical chemical composition (Al₂SiO₅), chemical analytical methods cannot distinguish them; only XRD distinguishes their unique crystal structures and d-spacings].

🖼️ Textbook Evidence: Dexter Perkins (Chapter 12)

Figure 12.15: Identification of Minerals by Diagnostic X-Ray Diffraction Patterns (Dexter Perkins, Mineralogy, 3rd ed., p. 336). Diffraction patterns plotted as intensity vs. diffraction angle $2\theta$. Each crystalline phase has a unique arrangement of atomic planes with specific interplanar $d$-spacings ($d_{hkl}$). In polymorphs having identical bulk chemistries, the distinct spatial arrangement of atoms produces unmistakable differences in peak positions and intensities.
"Chemical analyses (such as wet chemistry, XRF, EPMA, or ICP-MS) tell us the elemental composition of a mineral but provide no information about atomic arrangement or crystal structure. In contrast, X-ray diffraction (XRD) provides direct information about the 3-dimensional arrangement of atoms and interplanar spacings. Consequently, XRD is the definitive technique for distinguishing polymorphs—minerals that share identical chemical formulas but possess distinct crystal structures (such as calcite vs. aragonite, diamond vs. graphite, or kyanite vs. sillimanite vs. andalusite)."
— Dexter Perkins, Mineralogy, Chapter 12 (X-ray Diffraction and Mineral Analysis), pp. 326–335

🔍 Comprehensive Option Analysis

(b) XRD (X-Ray Powder Diffraction) [CORRECT]

Crystallographic Principle: Kyanite, sillimanite, and andalusite are the three classic Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs (trimorphs):

  • Kyanite: Triclinic crystal system ($P\bar{1}$); $a = 7.12\text{ \AA}, b = 7.85\text{ \AA}, c = 5.57\text{ \AA}$; density $\approx 3.6\text{ g/cm}^3$. High-pressure polymorph.
  • Andalusite: Orthorhombic crystal system ($Pnnm$); $a = 7.79\text{ \AA}, b = 7.90\text{ \AA}, c = 5.56\text{ \AA}$; density $\approx 3.15\text{ g/cm}^3$. Low-P, moderate-T polymorph.
  • Sillimanite: Orthorhombic crystal system ($Pbnm$); $a = 7.48\text{ \AA}, b = 7.67\text{ \AA}, c = 5.77\text{ \AA}$; density $\approx 3.23\text{ g/cm}^3$. High-temperature polymorph.

Because their crystal lattices and space groups differ radically, their interplanar $d$-spacings satisfy Bragg's law ($\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$) at completely distinct diffraction angles ($2\theta$). XRD powder diffractograms yield diagnostic, non-overlapping peak fingerprints that distinguish the three phases effortlessly.

(a) EPMA (Electron Probe Microanalyzer) [INCORRECT]

EPMA focuses a high-energy electron beam onto a 1-micron spot and measures characteristic X-rays emitted by elements. Because kyanite, sillimanite, and andalusite have the exact same chemical formula ($62.92\text{ wt}\%\text{ Al}_2\text{O}_3$ and $37.08\text{ wt}\%\text{ SiO}_2$), an EPMA analysis of all three minerals will yield identical Al:Si molar ratios (2:1). EPMA cannot detect crystal structure.

(c) XRF (X-Ray Fluorescence Spectrometry) [INCORRECT]

XRF is a bulk geochemical technique used to determine elemental concentrations in whole-rock or powdered samples. Like EPMA, it measures secondary characteristic fluorescent X-rays from elements, not structural diffraction. It reports identical bulk oxide weight percentages for all three polymorphs.

(d) ICP-MS (Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry) [INCORRECT]

ICP-MS ionizes dissolved sample solutions (or laser-ablated aerosols) into an argon plasma and separates ions by mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$). It is celebrated for ultra-trace element and isotope analysis down to parts-per-trillion (ppt), but since the sample's crystal lattice is completely destroyed in the 7,000 K plasma, it cannot distinguish polymorphs.

📊 Comparison: Analytical Techniques in Mineralogy

Instrument / Method What It Measures Sample State Can It Distinguish Polymorphs?
XRD (X-Ray Diffraction) Lattice parameters, $d$-spacings, crystal symmetry Intact crystalline powder or single crystal YES (Unambiguously separates polymorphs)
EPMA In-situ elemental composition (spot size ~1 µm) Polished thin section / mount NO (Identical composition: 63% Al₂O₃, 37% SiO₂)

ICP-MS Trace element & isotope concentrations Acid-digested liquid or laser-ablated vapor NO (Crystal structure is completely destroyed)

💡 Key Takeaway & Exam Strategy

Universal Geochemical Rule:
  • Whenever a question asks to distinguish polymorphs (e.g., Kyanite–Sillimanite–Andalusite, Calcite–Aragonite, Quartz–Coesite–Stishovite, Rutile–Anatase–Brookite, Diamond–Graphite), always select structural methods: XRD (or Raman / FTIR spectroscopy / Optical microscopy).
  • Chemical/elemental methods (EPMA, XRF, ICP-MS, AAS, EDS) FAIL on polymorphs because chemistry is identical.
