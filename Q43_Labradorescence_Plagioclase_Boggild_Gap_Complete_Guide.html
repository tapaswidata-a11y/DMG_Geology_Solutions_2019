Plagioclase Subsolidus Phase Relations: Labradorescence & The Bøggild Gap

Topic: Mineral Physics, Feldspar Petrology & Optics | Primary Reference: Dexter Perkins (Mineralogy, 3rd ed., Ch. 17) & Winter (Petrology, Ch. 6)

📝 Question Statement

Q.43. 'Labradorescence' observed in plagioclase is caused by

(a) Polymorphism
(b) The presence of closely spaced lamellae of An₁ and An₁₀ composition
(c) Fine exsolution lamellae of An₅₀ to An₆₀ composition (Bøggild gap)
(d) Peristerite gap
a Polymorphism
b The presence of closely spaced lamellae of An₁ and An₁₀ composition
c Fine exsolution lamellae of An₅₀ to An₆₀ composition (Correct Answer)
d Peristerite gap
✓ Correct Answer: (c) Fine exsolution lamellae of An₅₀ to An₆₀ composition [Labradorescence is caused by optical interference of visible light from submicroscopic exsolution lamellae within the Bøggild miscibility gap in intermediate plagioclase (An₄₈–An₅₈ / An₅₀–An₆₀)].

🖼️ Subsolidus Phase Diagram: Plagioclase Solvi

Figure 43.1: Subsolidus Phase Relations and Miscibility Gaps in Plagioclase Feldspars. Plagioclase forms a complete solid solution at magmatic temperatures (>1100°C), but at lower temperatures (subsolidus), three distinct miscibility gaps emerge: (1) Peristerite gap ($An_2–An_{16}$), causing peristerescence in albite-oligoclase; (2) Bøggild gap ($An_{48}–An_{58} / An_{50}–An_{60}$), where exsolution lamellae on the scale of visible light wavelengths produce iridescent labradorescence; and (3) Huttenlocher gap ($An_{65}–An_{90}$), occurring in bytownite.
"Plagioclases in the range $\text{An}_{48}$ to $\text{An}_{58}$ (or $\text{An}_{50}–\text{An}_{60}$) unmix upon slow cooling across the Bøggild miscibility gap into alternating submicroscopic lamellae of slightly differing anorthite contents. The lamellar spacing typically ranges between 100 nm and 300 nm, which matches the wavelength of visible light. Light reflected from these alternating layers undergoes constructive interference, producing the spectacular iridescent blue, green, and golden flashes known as labradorescence (the Schiller effect)."
— Dexter Perkins, Mineralogy, Chapter 17 (Tectosilicates: Feldspars), pp. 381–383

🔍 Comprehensive Option Analysis

(c) Fine exsolution lamellae of An₅₀ to An₆₀ composition (Bøggild Intergrowths) [CORRECT]

Optical & Crystallographic Mechanism: Labradorescence is not a pigmentary or chemical color; it is a physical interference phenomenon (structural color):

  • As high-temperature plagioclase of labradorite composition ($An_{50}–An_{60}$) cools below ~800°C, it enters the Bøggild miscibility gap.
  • The crystal unmixes via spinodal decomposition into alternating, planar lamellae differing by only ~10–12% An content (e.g., $An_{44}$ and $An_{56}$).
  • Because these alternating layers have slightly different refractive indices and thicknesses matching the wavelength of visible light ($\lambda \approx 400\text{–}700\text{ nm}$), light reflected from successive lamellar interfaces interferes constructively (Bragg-type diffraction of light). This creates the brilliant spectral Schiller effect known as labradorescence.

(a) Polymorphism [INCORRECT]

Polymorphism describes a substance with a fixed chemical composition existing in multiple crystal structures (e.g., graphite vs. diamond, calcite vs. aragonite). Plagioclase feldspars are a solid solution series ($\text{NaAlSi}_3\text{O}_8 - \text{CaAl}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_8$), not polymorphs. Labradorescence is caused by subsolidus exsolution, not a polymorphic phase transition.

(b) The presence of closely spaced lamellae of An₁ and An₁₀ composition [INCORRECT]

This composition range corresponds to the albite/peristerite region, not labradorite. Furthermore, lamellae of $An_1$ and $An_{10}$ do not produce labradorescence; they produce the faint bluish-white sheen of moonstone (peristerescence).

(d) Peristerite gap [INCORRECT / DISTINCT PHENOMENON]

The peristerite gap is located at the sodic end of the plagioclase series, between $An_2$ and $An_{16}$ (albite and oligoclase). It causes a pale bluish-white, cloudy opalescence termed peristerescence (from Greek peristera, pigeon, referring to the sheen on a pigeon's neck feathers). It is completely distinct from the vivid, colorful labradorescence of the Bøggild gap ($An_{50}–An_{60}$).

📊 The Three Subsolidus Miscibility Gaps in Plagioclase

Miscibility Gap Name Composition Range Plagioclase Variety Optical Effect Produced Peak Solvus Temperature
Peristerite Gap $\text{An}_2 - \text{An}_{16}$ Albite – Oligoclase Peristerescence (Whitish-blue opalescent sheen / moonstone) ~550°C
Bøggild Gap $\text{An}_{48} - \text{An}_{58}$ (or $\text{An}_{50} - \text{An}_{60}$) Labradorite Labradorescence (Spectacular blue, green, golden Schiller effect) ~800°C
Huttenlocher Gap $\text{An}_{65} - \text{An}_{90}$ Bytownite No visible iridescence (Lamellae are submicroscopic / coarse) ~900°C

💡 Key Takeaway & Exam Strategy

Essential Plagioclase Gap Rules for Competitive Exams:
  • Bøggild Gap $\rightarrow$ $An_{50}–An_{60}$ $\rightarrow$ Labradorescence (Schiller effect in Labradorite).
  • Peristerite Gap $\rightarrow$ $An_2–An_{16}$ $\rightarrow$ Peristerescence (Bluish sheen in Albite/Oligoclase).
  • Huttenlocher Gap $\rightarrow$ $An_{65}–An_{90}$ in Bytownite.
  • Physical cause is always: Submicroscopic exsolution lamellae whose thickness matches the wavelength of visible light (~100–300 nm) causing constructive interference.
