QUESTION 51
Which one of the following pairs is NOT correctly matched?
(a) Nunatak : Unglaciated island of bedrock, formerly surrounded by ice
(b) Cirque : Steep-walled semicircular basin in mountain glaciers
(c) Col : Narrow, sharp-edged ridge in mountain glaciers NOT CORRECTLY MATCHED (CORRECT OPTION)
(d) Fjord : U-shaped glacial trough below sea level

1. Executive Summary & Answer Verification

The correct option to choose is (c) Col : Narrow, sharp-edged ridge in mountain glaciers.

In glacial geomorphology:

  • A narrow, sharp-edged, knife-like ridge separating two adjacent glacial valleys or cirques is universally termed an Arête (from the French for "fishbone" or "ridge").
  • A Col is not the sharp ridge itself; rather, it is a low mountain pass, notch, or saddle formed along an arête where two opposing cirques erode headward toward each other and breach the intervening ridge crest.

Therefore, statement (c) attributes the diagnostic morphological definition of an arête to a col, making it NOT correctly matched.

Figure 51.1: Glacial erosional landforms in alpine mountainous terrains: An Arête is the narrow, knife-edge rocky ridge; a Col is the saddle-shaped notch or pass breaching the arête between two opposing Cirques; a Horn is the sharp pyramidal peak formed by 3 or more cirques.

2. Comprehensive Analysis of Glacial Landforms

2.1 The Col vs. Arête Distinction

During intense alpine glaciation, cirque glaciers erode bedrock by freeze-thaw frost wedging (plucking) and basal abrasion:

  • Arête: When two parallel alpine valley glaciers or two adjacent cirques erode headward from opposite sides of a divide, the divide is carved into a serrated, razor-sharp, knife-edged rocky ridge called an arête.
  • Col: Continued headward erosion from opposite sides causes the backwalls of two opposing cirques to intersect and break through the intervening arête. This carves a prominent, U- or saddle-shaped depression or pass across the ridge crest known as a col (or mountain pass).
  • Horn: When three or more cirques carve backwalls inward from different sides of a single mountain, they leave a sharp, spire-like pyramidal peak called a glacial horn (e.g., the Matterhorn in Switzerland).

2.2 Verification of the Other Three Landforms

  1. (a) Nunatak — Correctly Matched: A native Inuktitut term for an isolated rock island, peak, or ridge that projects through an ice sheet or ice cap, remaining unglaciated while completely surrounded by flowing glacial ice.
  2. (b) Cirque — Correctly Matched: A classic amphitheater-shaped, steep-walled, semicircular depression carved into high mountain headwalls by rotational ice slip, frost plucking, and abrasion (also called a corrie in Scotland or cwm in Wales). Often contains a small glacial tarn lake after ice retreat.
  3. (d) Fjord — Correctly Matched: A deep, steep-walled, flat-bottomed U-shaped glacial trough that was carved by a valley glacier extending below sea level, and subsequently drowned by marine waters following deglaciation and post-glacial eustatic sea-level rise (common in Norway, Greenland, Alaska, and New Zealand).

Textbook Authority

Arthur L. Bloom, Geomorphology: A Systematic Analysis of Late Cenozoic Landforms:
"An arête is a narrow, serrate, knife-edge ridge separating two glacial troughs or cirques. A col is a low point or saddle on an arête created by the intersection of the headwalls of two opposing cirques."
P. K. Mukherjee, Textbook of Geology:
"Arêtes are sharp-edged, saw-tooth ridges separating two valley glaciers. Where two cirques eat backwards towards each other, they make a breach in the dividing arête, forming a saddle or pass known as a col."
